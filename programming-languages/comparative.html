Programming Languages Comparative

This content was generated with the assistance of AI and should be verified against the official documentation before being relied on in production.

This page compares 35 programming languages on the same metrics: the 21 languages documented on this site, plus 14 more that matter for context. Every number carries a source and a date, and anything that could not be sourced is shown as n/a instead of being estimated. The data snapshot date is 2026-10-26: all sources were read on that day unless a caption says otherwise. Metrics such as popularity indices and registry counts change every month, so treat the figures as a dated snapshot, not as current values.

The raw data, the chart generator and the scoring rubric are kept in the repository under scripts/language-charts/, so the charts can be regenerated when new data is published. Jump to the Summary and recommendations if you only need the conclusions.

Scope and inclusion criteria

A language is included when at least one of these holds:

  1. It is already documented on this site. These 21 languages are linked wherever they appear:

C, COBOL, C++, C#, Fortran, Go, Groovy, Java, JavaScript, Kotlin, MATLAB, Objective-C, PHP, Python, R, Ruby, Rust, Scala, Swift, TypeScript, Visual Basic. . It ranks in the TIOBE top 50 (October 2026) or in another main index used here (PYPL, IEEE Spectrum, RedMonk, the Stack Overflow survey). . It matters for history or growth: a historic standardised language (Ada, Lisp, Pascal) or a recent fast-growing one (Zig, Mojo, Elixir).

The 14 extra languages are Pascal/Delphi, Perl, Ada, Lisp, Assembly, Dart, Julia, Zig, Elixir, Lua, Haskell, Mojo, SQL and Bash. Some of them get fewer metrics because data is scarce:

  • Mojo, Bash, Elixir and Groovy are not ranked in the TIOBE top 50. Mojo, Elixir and Bash have no PYPL entry, and Mojo and Bash have no Benchmarks Game or energy data.

  • Assembly, SQL and Bash are not a single implementation, so they have no registry, release cadence, licence or benchmark.

  • Zig, Mojo and Julia are absent from the energy study; 15 of the 35 languages are.

  • The official sites of COBOL, Assembly, SQL, Bash, Lisp, Pascal/Delphi and MATLAB could not be checked automatically, so their onboarding score is n/a.

Other metrics

Scales. Raw metrics are shown on their own units (percent, ratio to the best, USD). The scatter and radar charts use a 0-10 score, where 10 is the best value in the set. Metrics that span orders of magnitude (ratings, packages, time and energy ratios) use a logarithmic scale before conversion, so a language is not ranked by a single outlier.

Scores. Every radar score is computed by a formula from the sourced CSV values; no score is typed by hand. The rubric is summarised below. A language with a missing input on an axis gets n/a on that axis.

The onboarding score is 10 * (number of yes) / (number of answered checks) over four checks on the official site: O1 a getting-started tutorial, O2 an online reference or specification, O3 an online playground or REPL, O4 a one-command toolchain installer. Checks that could not be answered are left out of the denominator, and the score is n/a if fewer than three could be answered.

Known limits of the data. Read these before drawing conclusions.

  • The popularity indices measure attention, not quality or market share. TIOBE counts search-engine hits, PYPL counts tutorial searches, and the Stack Overflow survey reflects its respondents.

  • Benchmarks Game ratios are derived by us from per-program tables (7 programs, edition 25.03); the site publishes only box plots. Benchmarks reward tuned programs and say little about a typical application.

  • The energy figures come from one study (Pereira et al., preprint dated 2021-03-23), which predates the current releases of every language. 15 languages are absent from it.

  • Registry counts are not comparable across registries and only LuaRocks has a historical series; no other history could be reached, and none is estimated.

  • The NSA June 2025 memory-safe language list could not be retrieved (HTTP 403), so most memory-safety classifications are n/a.

  • Salary is from the 2024 survey, because later surveys do not publish salary by language. It is a median over self-selected respondents, not a market survey.

  • The versatility score is a yes/no checklist of nine domains, each yes backed by a cited source. It measures breadth of use, not how good a language is in each domain.

Popularity

Popularity is the most requested and the least precise metric. This page uses three independent signals: the TIOBE index (search-engine hits), PYPL (tutorial searches) and the Stack Overflow Developer Survey (what developers say they use and admire). In October 2026 the TIOBE top six are Python (19.23%), C (11.33%), C++ (8.87%), Java (8.41%), C# (3.79%) and JavaScript (2.77%). Python is also first in PYPL with a 53.9% share, followed by Java with 12.98%, while C and C++ share one combined PYPL entry (7.97%). The Stack Overflow survey tells a different story because it counts usage by respondents: JavaScript (62%), SQL (58.4%), Python (58%), Bash (51.3%) and TypeScript (43.8%) lead, and Rust has the highest admired score (67.4%).

Ecosystem

A language is as productive as its libraries. The registry sizes below are a 2026 snapshot taken from each registry’s own API: npm holds 4,532,034 packages (shared by JavaScript and TypeScript), PyPI 923,102 projects, Maven Central 661,801 artifacts (shared by Java, Kotlin, Scala and Groovy), NuGet 528,060 package IDs (C# and Visual Basic), Packagist 470,008 package names, crates.io 361,763 crates and RubyGems 197,949 gems. Smaller registries are pub.dev (93,435), CPAN (52,878 distributions), CRAN (25,529), Hex (22,923), Hackage (19,594), Julia General (14,627), the Swift Package Index (12,380) and LuaRocks (6,943). The units are not identical (packages, artifacts, distributions, modules), and registries differ in how much they split a project into packages, so use the chart for orders of magnitude, not for exact ranking. C, C++, Go, Fortran and COBOL have no verified central registry count and show as n/a, which does not mean they have no libraries. For the same reason their Ecosystem axis on the radar charts is a gap, not a low score.

Performance

Three measurements are used: CPU time and memory from the Benchmarks Game (7 programs: fannkuch-redux, n-body, spectral-norm, mandelbrot, fasta, k-nucleotide and reverse-complement), and energy from a peer-reviewed study. They answer different questions: how fast and how lean a tuned program can be, and how much energy it needs.

CPU time. Compiled languages cluster near the fastest: C is 1.19 times the fastest implementation, C++ 1.35, Rust 1.45 and C# 1.93. Java is 4.16, Go 4.04 and JavaScript 6.78. Interpreted languages are one to two orders of magnitude slower: PHP 40.68, Lua 56.17, Ruby 60.09, Python 74.18 and Perl 78.29. Kotlin, Scala, Groovy, MATLAB, R and others are not in the benchmark set and show n/a.

Figure 10. CPU time relative to the fastest implementation, lower is better. Source: Benchmarks Game edition 25.03 (derived by us, geometric mean over 7 programs), measured 2025-03-05.

Memory. Pascal uses the least (1.10 times the lowest) and C sits at 2.81. Garbage-collected runtimes use much more: Python 12.30, Java 33.20 and JavaScript 37.21, with Dart at 81.72 and Julia at 123.98. Fast does not mean lean: Julia is among the faster languages on CPU (2.28) but at the top of the memory range.

Figure 11. Memory use relative to the lowest, lower is better. Source: Benchmarks Game edition 25.03 (derived by us, geometric mean over 7 programs), measured 2025-03-05.

Energy. Relative to C (1.00), Rust uses 1.03, C++ 1.34, Ada 1.70, Java 1.98, C# 3.14, Go 3.23 and JavaScript 4.45; TypeScript uses 21.50, PHP 29.30, Ruby 69.91, Python 75.88 and Perl 79.58. The ranking follows the CPU ranking closely, because energy is mostly time spent running. The study is from 2021 and some languages have changed since, so read it as a rough guide.

Figure 12. Energy use relative to C (C = 1.00), lower is better. Source: Pereira et al. 2021, Table 4. Languages absent from the paper show n/a.

Learning curve and ease of use

Ease of learning has no universal measure, so this page uses transparent proxies and labels them: the onboarding score (what the official site offers a newcomer), the size of the benchmark programs (a verbosity proxy), the type system, and how many people who are learning to code use the language. Python, C#, Go, Rust, Scala, Visual Basic, Ada and Haskell reach the maximum onboarding score of 10: their official sites pass all four checks. C and JavaScript score 2.5, one of the four checks each (the C standard is maintained by a committee, so its official site hosts no tutorial). Source size ranges from 442.54 gzip bytes for Ruby, 483.39 for Python and 510.95 for PHP, up to 1,003.60 for Rust and 1,375.31 for Ada. Among people learning to code, Python (71.8%) and JavaScript (62.8%) are the most used in the 2025 Stack Overflow survey.

The onboarding score measures how well the official site helps a beginner, not how hard the language is; a language with a rich third-party community can be easy to learn and still score low. Use it together with the usage figures. The Python, Go and Rust references on this site start with a getting-started page for that reason.

Security and safety

Memory-safety bugs (buffer overflows, use after free) are the largest class of serious vulnerabilities in large C and C++ code bases: Microsoft reported that about 70% of the vulnerabilities it fixes are memory-safety issues (MSRC, 2019-07-18), the Chromium project says about 70% of its serious security bugs are of that kind (Chromium security page), and Google reported that memory-safety issues were 76% of Android vulnerabilities in 2019 and 24% in 2024 (Google Security Blog, 2024-09-25). The CISA roadmaps guidance of December 2023 (CISA) and a CISA alert of 2025-06-24 (CISA alert) recommend moving to memory-safe languages.

Only five languages have a classification that could be read in a retrieved document: Java is memory safe (Wikipedia, Memory safety), Rust is memory safe unless an unsafe construct is used (CISA roadmaps, December 2023), C and C++ are not memory safe (ONCD, February 2024), and Fortran is not memory safe (Wikipedia, Memory safety). All other languages are n/a; this does not mean they are unsafe, only that no retrieved document classifies them. Languages with a garbage collector or managed runtime (Java, C#, Go, Python and others) avoid most memory errors by design, but the data here does not classify them and the page does not guess. No reliable per-language CVE statistics were found, so none are shown.

Other properties that matter when choosing a language are whether it has a formal standard, how often it is released, and under which licence its reference implementation is distributed. Summary and recommendations

These recommendations are an editorial judgement built on the data above; they are not part of the sourced data. Every one cites the metric it relies on, and every metric has a snapshot date of 2026-10-26. The data change fast: popularity indices move every month, registry counts every day, and the energy study is from 2021. Re-check the numbers before a decision that will last years.

Avoid for new projects.

  • COBOL ranks 16th in TIOBE with 0.89%, has a PYPL share of 0.04%, 1.1% usage in the Stack Overflow survey 2026 and no verified registry (Popularity, Ecosystem). Keep it where it already runs (its median salary is USD 70,000), but do not start a new system in it.

  • Visual Basic has fallen from 8.66% of PYPL in 2004 to 0.10% and has 4% usage in the 2026 survey (Popularity). Existing .NET applications can continue; new ones fit C# better, on the same NuGet ecosystem (528,060 package IDs).

Use with care.

  • PHP still has 17.7% survey usage and a large registry (470,008 package names), but its PYPL share fell from 19.71% to 1.74%, it is 40.68 times the fastest CPU time with 29.30 times the energy of C, and its median salary (USD 49,586) is the third lowest in the dataset, above only Ada and Dart. It is a reasonable choice for maintaining an existing site; weigh it against alternatives for a new one.

Safe mainstream choices.

  • JavaScript and TypeScript lead survey usage (62% and 43.8%) and share the largest registry (npm, 4,532,034 packages); TypeScript has the highest admired score of the two (53%). They are the default for the web.

  • Python is first in TIOBE (19.23%) and PYPL (53.9%), has 923,102 PyPI projects and the highest learning-to-code usage (71.8%). Its cost is performance: 74.18 times the fastest CPU time and 75.88 times the energy of C, which is why it is paired with compiled libraries.

  • Java (8.41% TIOBE, 661,801 Maven Central artifacts) is classified as memory safe and has a six-month release cadence with LTS releases. C# (3.79% TIOBE, 528,060 NuGet package IDs) is 1.93 times the fastest CPU time, the best of the managed languages here.

Trends.

  • Python for AI and machine learning. The dataset does not measure domains, so this is editorial: Python’s TIOBE rating grew from 3.90% in 2016 to 19.23% and its ecosystem is the second largest of the registries compared. Treat the domain claim as a hypothesis the data are consistent with, not a result.

  • Rust for safety and performance. Rust has the highest admired score (67.4%), CPU time 1.45 times the fastest and energy 1.03 times that of C, a memory-safe-unless-unsafe classification, 361,763 crates and growth in TIOBE from 0.26% (2016) to 1.44%.
