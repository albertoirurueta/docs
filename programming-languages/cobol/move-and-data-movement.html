MOVE and Data Movement

This content was generated with the assistance of AI and should be verified against the official documentation before being relied on in production.

This page is written against ISO/IEC 1989:2023 (edition 3, January 2023; an edition 4 is upcoming, in DIS ballot), GnuCOBOL 3.2 (28 July 2023, the latest release) and IBM Enterprise COBOL for z/OS 6.5 (PTFs through September 2026), and was checked on 2026-10-20. Every portable example below was compiled with cobc -x -Wall (GnuCOBOL 3.2.0, fixed reference format) and run, without a warning except in the ones labelled deliberately wrong; the output shown is real, and where the text says that an example was also run with -std=ibm or another option, the differences are given. The ISO standard is paywalled and was not read: the rules come from the vendor manuals. The IBM statements, including everything about S0C7, NUMCHECK and INVDATA, come from the Enterprise COBOL documentation and were not run here (z/OS; not run here).

MOVE is the most used statement in COBOL and the one with the most rules hidden behind its two-word syntax. It copies data from a sender to one or more receivers, and what "copies" means depends on the categories of both sides: digits are lined up on the decimal point, text is lined up on the left, numeric-edited senders are turned back into numbers, group items are copied byte for byte, and nothing at all is reported when the receiver is too small. This page covers the statement itself, the table of which category may be moved to which, the alignment and truncation rules, sign handling, group moves, MOVE CORRESPONDING, figurative constants and MOVE ALL, de-editing, reference modification, and the bugs that come from numeric receivers that do not hold valid numbers, including the S0C7 abend and the compiler options that detect it. The data categories themselves are defined in PICTURE Clause, the storage formats in USAGE and Numeric Representation, and the arithmetic that moves a computed value into a receiver in Arithmetic and COMPUTE.

The MOVE statement

MOVE has two formats. Format 1 moves one sender to one or more receivers; format 2, MOVE CORRESPONDING (abbreviated MOVE CORR), moves the elementary items that two group items have in common.

Format Written as Effect

1

MOVE identifier-1 TO identifier-2 ... (or a literal instead of identifier-1)

The sending area is moved to each receiver in the order the receivers are written. Every receiver is an identifier; a receiver cannot be an intrinsic function.

2

MOVE CORRESPONDING group-1 TO group-2

Both operands must be group items. The result is the same as one separate MOVE for each pair of corresponding items.

The rules that apply to every MOVE:

  • After the statement the sender holds the same data as before, unless the same item is also a receiver.

  • With several receivers the sender is evaluated once and the receivers are filled from left to right. A reference-modified or subscripted sender, or a function that returns text, is evaluated once, immediately before the first receiver is filled; the length, subscripts and reference modification of each receiver are evaluated just before that receiver is filled (see Reference modification as sender and receiver).

  • Items of USAGE INDEX, POINTER, FUNCTION-POINTER, PROCEDURE-POINTER and OBJECT REFERENCE cannot be named in a MOVE. They can sit inside a group that is moved as a group, and SET is the statement that assigns them (see Pointers and Dynamic Storage).

  • Data may be moved to or from a file record area only while the file is open: the record area becomes available when OPEN succeeds and disappears at CLOSE.

  • Overlapping operands give unpredictable results (see below for GnuCOBOL’s warning).

  • When the receiver is a strongly typed group (a TYPEDEF STRONG item, Enterprise COBOL 6.5), the sender must be a group of the same type; see REDEFINES, RENAMES and TYPEDEF.

A MOVE is either an elementary move (the receiver is an elementary item and the sender is an elementary item, a literal or a figurative constant) or a group move (an alphanumeric group is involved on either side). The two follow completely different rules, and most of the surprises in this page come from using one while thinking of the other.

Source: Enterprise COBOL 6.5 Language Reference: MOVE statement

Categories and the legality table

Every operand of an elementary move belongs to one of the data categories that PICTURE Clause defines: alphabetic, alphanumeric, alphanumeric-edited, numeric, numeric-edited, external floating point (USAGE DISPLAY or NATIONAL floating point), internal floating point (COMP-1, COMP-2), DBCS, national, national-edited and UTF-8. Literals and figurative constants count by what they look like: an alphanumeric literal is alphanumeric, ZERO and an integer literal are numeric integers, a literal with a decimal point is a numeric non-integer, and SPACE counts as alphabetic.

  • (b) and (c): a figurative constant or alphanumeric literal moved to a numeric, numeric-edited or floating-point receiver must consist only of digits and is treated as a numeric integer; the ALL literal cannot be the sender of a move to a floating-point receiver.   • (d) includes integer literals; (e) includes non-integer literals; (f) includes floating-point literals and the external and internal floating-point data items; (g) includes DBCS items, DBCS literals and SPACE; (h) includes national items, national literals, national functions and the figurative constants ZERO, SPACE, QUOTE and ALL national-literal; (i) the same with UTF-8.

Three consequences are worth memorising, because they are the ones that surprise people:

  • A numeric item with decimals cannot be moved to an alphanumeric or alphabetic item. To show or store 18.99 as text, move it to a numeric-edited item first (PIC Z(3)9.99) and move that to the text item, or use a numeric-edited receiver and STRING.

  • An integer can be moved to an alphanumeric item (the digits are copied, left-aligned) but not to an alphabetic one.

  • Numeric-edited senders are valid wherever a numeric one is, because the compiler de-edits them (see De-editing), but moving one to an alphanumeric receiver copies the edited characters, comma and sign letters included.

The rules for the code-page conversions of national, UTF-8 and DBCS receivers are in National, UTF-8 and Code Pages.

The last two lines show another category rule. A COMP-2 item moved to a fixed-point receiver is converted to a decimal value, and 18.99 is not exactly representable in binary floating point: GnuCOBOL 3.2 printed 18.98, so the conversion truncated the binary value 18.9899999... instead of rounding it. The Enterprise COBOL Programming Guide says that when a COMP-1, COMP-2 or external floating-point item or a floating-point literal is moved to a fixed-point item, rounding occurs in the low-order position of the receiver, and that a COMP-2 item moved to a fixed-point item of more than 18 digits keeps only 18 significant digits. The two compilers are not guaranteed to agree on the last digit, which is one more reason to keep money in fixed-point fields and never in floating point.

Illegal moves and what the compiler says

GnuCOBOL enforces the table at compile time for the clear-cut cases, and warns about literals that cannot be numeric. Alignment and truncation

The receiver, not the sender, decides how the data is lined up.

Receiver Rule

Numeric, numeric-edited

The data is aligned on the (implied) decimal point and padded with zeros or truncated at either end. A receiver without a decimal point is treated as if the point were just to its right, so decimals of the sender are dropped, not rounded.

Alphanumeric, alphanumeric-edited, alphabetic

The data is aligned at the left and padded with spaces on the right, or truncated on the right. JUSTIFIED RIGHT reverses this: the data is aligned at the right and a too-long sender loses its left-most characters (see PICTURE Clause).

National, national-edited, UTF-8

Aligned at the left, padded with national or UTF-8 spaces on the right, and truncated only at a character boundary; the programmer is responsible for not splitting a character made of several encoding units.

Internal floating point

A decimal point is assumed at the left; the data is aligned on the first digit position with the exponent adjusted.

DBCS

Padded or truncated on the right with DBCS spaces.

Truncation is silent. A numeric move that does not fit loses its high-order (left) digits and, if there are more decimals than the receiver holds, its low-order ones; a text move loses its trailing characters. No SIZE ERROR exists for MOVE (only the arithmetic statements have it), and no condition is raised. The program below moves a PIC 9(5)V99 value to smaller and larger receivers and a 21-character title to shorter and longer text receivers.

The 21-character title in X(12) loses its last nine characters; the three-digit copies count 007 moved to X(2) leaves 00, the first two digits, because a numeric integer sent to an alphanumeric receiver is treated as text. With -std=ibm the same program prints 3456, 345 and 0012345670: the values are identical, but that dialect’s DISPLAY writes implied-decimal digits without the decimal point (see the notes on DISPLAY in PICTURE Clause). Binary receivers and TRUNC

A DISPLAY or packed-decimal receiver always keeps exactly as many digits as its PICTURE allows. A binary (COMP, BINARY, COMP-4) receiver is different, because a halfword, fullword or doubleword can hold more than the picture’s digits. Which happens is decided by the compiler option TRUNC in Enterprise COBOL: with TRUNC(STD) (the default) the value is truncated to the digits of the PICTURE, with TRUNC(OPT) the compiler assumes the data fits and the result of a value that does not is unpredictable, and with TRUNC(BIN) the item is handled like COMP-5 and keeps the whole binary content. COMP-5 is not affected by TRUNC. Binary integers are exact; what goes wrong with them is truncation and decimal scaling, not rounding. GnuCOBOL’s equivalent is the pair of dialect settings binary-truncate and binary-size.

       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. BINTRUNC.
      * High-order truncation in DISPLAY, packed and binary receivers.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  W-BIG             PIC 9(5)  VALUE 12345.
       01  W-DISPLAY         PIC 9(4).
       01  W-PACKED          PIC 9(4)  COMP-3.
       01  W-BINARY          PIC 9(4)  COMP.
       01  W-BINARY5         PIC 9(4)  COMP-5.
       01  W-SHOW            PIC Z(4)9.
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           MOVE W-BIG TO W-DISPLAY
           MOVE W-DISPLAY TO W-SHOW
           DISPLAY "PIC 9(4) DISPLAY: " W-SHOW
           MOVE W-BIG TO W-PACKED
           MOVE W-PACKED TO W-SHOW
           DISPLAY "PIC 9(4) COMP-3:  " W-SHOW
           MOVE W-BIG TO W-BINARY
           MOVE W-BINARY TO W-SHOW
           DISPLAY "PIC 9(4) COMP:    " W-SHOW
           MOVE W-BIG TO W-BINARY5
           MOVE W-BINARY5 TO W-SHOW
           DISPLAY "PIC 9(4) COMP-5:  " W-SHOW
           STOP RUN.

PIC 9(4) COMP-3:   2345
PIC 9(4) COMP:     2345

Sign handling

A sign is part of the data only when the receiver has one. The rules for a numeric or numeric-edited receiver are:

  • If the receiver is signed, it receives the sender’s sign, converted to the receiver’s sign representation. If the sender is unsigned, a positive sign is generated.

  • If the receiver is unsigned, no sign is stored and the absolute value of the sender is moved. A negative amount moved to an unsigned field silently becomes positive.

  • A numeric-edited receiver without a sign symbol behaves like an unsigned one: -12.50 is shown as 12.50.

  • A numeric sender moved to an alphanumeric (or national or UTF-8) receiver contributes its unsigned value; a separate sign character is not moved and the size of the sender counts as one less.

  • An alphanumeric, alphanumeric-edited, national or UTF-8 sender moved to a numeric receiver is treated as an unsigned integer.

The representation of signs in zoned and packed-decimal data, the preferred signs C, D and F, and the effect of NUMPROC are in USAGE and Numeric Representation. On z/OS an unsigned zoned sender moved to an alphanumeric receiver keeps its bytes unchanged, even with NUMPROC(NOPFD).

Group moves

A move is a group move when the sender or the receiver is an alphanumeric group item (a group item that is not a national or UTF-8 group). The compiler then treats that group as plain alphanumeric text. The group move rules are short and unforgiving:

  • The move is treated as an alphanumeric-to-alphanumeric elementary move, except that nothing is converted. The receiving area is filled without regard to the elementary items inside either side.

  • The sender is aligned at the left. If it is shorter than the receiver, the rest of the receiver is filled with spaces, whatever the items there are; if it is longer, the excess on the right is cut off.

  • A group may be moved to an elementary item and an elementary item or literal to a group, in both cases as alphanumeric text. A national group and a UTF-8 group may be moved to each other, and either may be moved to an alphanumeric group.

  • A dynamic-length group cannot be moved to or from another group.

  • The OCCURS clause is the one exception to "regardless of the elementary items": the length of a table governed by OCCURS DEPENDING ON is evaluated before the move.

The classic accident is a numeric item that is part of the receiving group but lies beyond the end of the sending group. The program below moves a 30-byte LOAN-RECORD into a 33-byte working group whose last field is a numeric WORK-RETRIES that was just set to zero.

MOVE CORRESPONDING

MOVE CORRESPONDING group-1 TO group-2 moves every pair of subordinate items that correspond, as if each pair had its own MOVE. Two items correspond when all of these hold:

  • They have the same name and the same qualifiers, up to but not including group-1 and group-2.

  • At least one of the two is an elementary item, and a MOVE between them is allowed by the table above. In ADD and SUBTRACT CORRESPONDING both must be elementary numeric items; in MOVE the numeric-edited, text and numeric categories mix freely under the move rules.

  • Neither is FILLER, a level 66, 77 or 88 item, an index data item or a pointer-like item (POINTER, FUNCTION-POINTER, PROCEDURE-POINTER, OBJECT REFERENCE).

  • Neither has a REDEFINES, RENAMES or OCCURS clause, nor is it a USAGE INDEX or pointer item. The two group operands themselves may contain or be subordinate to items with REDEFINES or OCCURS.

  • Neither group operand is reference-modified, and the name of each matching item is unique after implicit qualification.

Items without a counterpart are left alone, and so are the filler areas of the receiver. The statement is a compile-time shorthand: if no pair corresponds it does nothing at run time. GnuCOBOL warns about that case (warning: no CORRESPONDING items found [-Wcorresponding]); this page does not verify whether Enterprise COBOL does.

The first program builds the Circulation member report line from the member master record. The two groups share the names MEMBER-ID, MEMBER-NAME, MEMBER-FINES and MEMBER-STATUS (names are qualified with OF where the program refers to the master record); MEMBER-JOINED exists only in the master and LINE-NOTE only in the line.

  • The qualification of the operands has to be spelled out when both groups come from copybooks with identical names, as the example does with OF.

  • GnuCOBOL’s manual describes the effect the same way: as if a series of individual moves were done, one for each match.

Figurative constants and MOVE ALL

A figurative constant is allowed wherever a literal is, except where the syntax forbids it. Its length is that of the associated item, or one when it stands alone. In a MOVE:

Sender Result

SPACE, SPACES

Spaces in every position of an alphabetic, alphanumeric, group, national or edited-text receiver. Not allowed with a numeric receiver (the table above).

ZERO, ZEROS, ZEROES

Numeric zero in a numeric receiver (no sign problem); the character 0 in every position of a text or group receiver.

HIGH-VALUE, LOW-VALUE

The highest and lowest character of the collating sequence (hexadecimal FF and 00 unless PROGRAM COLLATING SEQUENCE changes them) in every position of a text or group receiver. Not allowed with a numeric receiver.

QUOTE, QUOTES

The quotation mark character, repeated.

ALL literal

The string repeated and cut to the receiver’s length, for example ALL "-=". For a numeric receiver the literal must consist only of digits. When the literal is itself a figurative constant, ALL is only for readability: MOVE ALL SPACES equals MOVE SPACES.

Singular and plural spellings of SPACE, ZERO, QUOTE, HIGH-VALUE and LOW-VALUE are interchangeable. MOVE ZERO TO group fills a group with the character 0, including its packed and binary items, which makes them invalid; use INITIALIZE for groups that mix categories. De-editing

When the sender of an elementary move is numeric-edited and the receiver is numeric or numeric-edited, the compiler de-edits the sender: it works out the unedited value (the sign included) that the edited text stands for, and moves that value under the usual numeric rules. So 1,234.50CR becomes -1234.50 in a PIC S9(5)V99 receiver, 1234.50 in a PIC 9(5)V99 one (the sign is dropped) and $1,234.50 in another edited item whose picture has no sign position. When the receiver is a text item there is nothing to de-edit: the edited characters are copied as they are.

When the sending and receiving areas overlap, the result of a MOVE is unpredictable in Enterprise COBOL; the usual overlap is a reference-modified item moved onto itself with a shift. GnuCOBOL notices the shift at compile time. This program is deliberately risky:

       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. OVERLAP.
      * Deliberately risky: sending and receiving areas overlap.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  W-T PIC X(10) VALUE "ABCDEFGHIJ".
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
           MOVE W-T(1:5) TO W-T(2:5)
           DISPLAY W-T
           MOVE "ABCDEFGHIJ" TO W-T
           MOVE W-T(2:5) TO W-T(1:5)
           DISPLAY W-T
           STOP RUN.

With cobc -x -Wall overlap.cob the compiler warns twice and the program runs:

overlap.cob:8: warning: overlapping MOVE may produce unpredictable results [-Woverlap]
overlap.cob:11: warning: overlapping MOVE may produce unpredictable results [-Woverlap]
AABCDEGHIJ
BCDEFFGHIJ

GnuCOBOL happened to behave like a memory move that is safe for overlaps (AABCDEGHIJ and BCDEFFGHIJ). Do not rely on that, and do not rely on the opposite on z/OS either: shift text with a separate work item, as in MOVE W-T(1:5) TO W-WORK, MOVE W-WORK TO W-T(2:5).

Moving numbers that are not valid

A numeric receiver does not guarantee that the bytes in it are a number. The compiler trusts the PICTURE and USAGE and generates code that assumes valid digits and signs; if they are not, the behaviour depends on the compiler and the options, and ranges from wrong results to an abend.

Invalid numeric data enters a program in a few well-known ways:

  • A record read from a file or message whose numeric column is blank, shorter than the record layout, or contains letters or an edited value.

  • A group move that spaces out a numeric item (the group move example above) or MOVE SPACES to a group that contains numeric items.

  • A REDEFINES of text over numeric storage, or a numeric field that was never given a value (see VALUE, INITIALIZE and Condition Names).

  • An alphanumeric sender moved to a numeric receiver: the compiler cannot check the content.

  • Packed or zoned data produced by another program, or converted wrongly from ASCII to EBCDIC and back.

  • Truncation (for example of a binary field with TRUNC(OPT)) that leaves a value outside the picture.

What GnuCOBOL does

GnuCOBOL does not abend on invalid data in the default build: spaces count as zero digits, letters contribute their low four bits, and class tests report the truth. The program below puts a book record with a blank copies column into BOOK-RECORD (the short-record case), tests it, and uses it.

       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. BADNUM.
      * How spaces reach a numeric field, and what the class test says.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       COPY BOOKREC.
       01  W-INPUT-LINE      PIC X(60).
       01  W-TOTAL           PIC 9(5)    VALUE ZERO.
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
      * A short input line: the copies column is blank.
           MOVE SPACES TO W-INPUT-LINE
           MOVE "000001" TO W-INPUT-LINE(1:6)
           MOVE W-INPUT-LINE TO BOOK-RECORD
           DISPLAY "copies field: [" BOOK-COPIES "]"
           IF BOOK-COPIES IS NUMERIC
               DISPLAY "BOOK-COPIES is numeric"
           ELSE
               DISPLAY "BOOK-COPIES is NOT numeric"
           END-IF
           MOVE BOOK-COPIES TO W-TOTAL
           DISPLAY "after MOVE to 9(5): " W-TOTAL
           ADD BOOK-COPIES TO W-TOTAL
           DISPLAY "after ADD:          " W-TOTAL
           STOP RUN.

Compiled with cobc -x -Wall -I. invalid.cob, it prints:

after MOVE to 9(5): 00000

The numeric class test

The class condition identifier IS NUMERIC checks the content against what is valid for the item’s PICTURE and USAGE: digits 0 to 9 in every digit position, a valid sign in the sign position, and for a zoned item a valid zone code in the upper four bits of every byte. Leading or trailing spaces make a zoned item non-numeric; a negative sign makes an unsigned item non-numeric. The valid sign values depend on the NUMPROC option and the installation’s NUMCLS setting. The condition is explained with the other tests in Conditions, IF and EVALUATE. The guarded move below applies it to five three-character texts from a transaction file before moving them to PIC 9(3).

       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. GUARD.
      * Guarding a MOVE to a numeric field with the class test.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  W-TEXTS.
           05  FILLER        PIC X(3) VALUE "012".
           05  FILLER        PIC X(3) VALUE "  7".
           05  FILLER        PIC X(3) VALUE "1A3".
           05  FILLER        PIC X(3) VALUE SPACES.
           05  FILLER        PIC X(3) VALUE "-05".
       01  W-TABLE REDEFINES W-TEXTS.
           05  W-TEXT        PIC X(3) OCCURS 5 TIMES.
       01  W-IX              PIC 9.
       01  W-COPIES          PIC 9(3).
       01  W-ACCEPTED        PIC 9      VALUE ZERO.
       01  W-REJECTED        PIC 9      VALUE ZERO.
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           PERFORM VARYING W-IX FROM 1 BY 1 UNTIL W-IX > 5
               IF W-TEXT(W-IX) IS NUMERIC
                   MOVE W-TEXT(W-IX) TO W-COPIES
                   ADD 1 TO W-ACCEPTED
                   DISPLAY "[" W-TEXT(W-IX) "] accepted: " W-COPIES
               ELSE
                   ADD 1 TO W-REJECTED
                   DISPLAY "[" W-TEXT(W-IX) "] rejected"
               END-IF
           END-PERFORM
           DISPLAY "accepted " W-ACCEPTED ", rejected " W-REJECTED
           STOP RUN.

Compiled with cobc -x -Wall guard.cob (the same with -std=ibm), it prints:

[012] accepted: 012
[  7] rejected
[1A3] rejected
[   ] rejected
[-05] rejected
accepted 1, rejected 4

Only 012 passes. Right-justified ` 7` is not numeric, which surprises people who think of it as the number 7, 1A3 contains a letter, the blank field is not a number, and -05 fails because the field is an alphanumeric item (PIC X(3)) with a sign character in it, which is not a digit. The remedy for the first and last is to convert with a function (NUMVAL) or to use UNSTRING/INSPECT (see STRING, UNSTRING and INSPECT) and then move the result.

What z/OS does: S0C7

On z/OS invalid data in a numeric item typically surfaces as the system completion code S0C7, a data exception program check. The hardware’s decimal instructions, which Enterprise COBOL uses for zoned and packed-decimal arithmetic and comparisons, raise it when an operand has an invalid digit or an invalid sign (general z/Architecture behaviour, which the Enterprise COBOL manuals read for this page do not restate). Which statement fails, and whether any does, depends on the generated code: a MOVE between two zoned items may be a byte copy that the processor never checks, so the abend often appears later, in the ADD or COMPUTE that finally uses the field, and the faulty statement is not the one that stored the spaces. The compiler is allowed to assume valid data (that is the price of NUMPROC(PFD) and NOINVDATA), and the Programming Guide says the results of using an item with invalid data are undefined.

In the Circulation nightly run the typical story is a LOANTRAN record whose date or member id was blank or shifted by a column: the validation step does an unguarded MOVE to a numeric working item, and the update step abends with an S0C7 at 2 a.m. The usual steps are to find the failing statement from the dump (offset or statement number in the compile listing, and the contents of the field in the CEEDUMP), find who put the bad bytes there, and then add the guard shown above. See Debugging and Troubleshooting for reading the dump and Error Handling and Declaratives for what the program can do on a bad record.

NUMCHECK

NUMCHECK makes the Enterprise COBOL compiler generate extra checks so that invalid data is reported where it is used and not only where an instruction later fails. The default is NONUMCHECK. For each statement it checks the sending operands, not the receivers (a receiver that is also a sender, as B in ADD A TO B, is checked as a sender). Zoned and packed senders get an implicit numeric class test, binary senders a size check, and an alphanumeric sender moved to a numeric receiver is treated as a numeric integer and tested too.

Suboption Meaning

ZON(ALPHNUM|NOALPHNUM, LAX|STRICT), NOZON

An implicit numeric class test for zoned decimal (USAGE DISPLAY) senders. ALPHNUM includes the senders compared with an alphanumeric item, literal or figurative constant; NOALPHNUM excludes those comparisons. LAX tolerates the valid layouts that come from a REDEFINES (an unsigned overlay of a signed item, spaces in the leading bytes of an overlay of a numeric-edited item, a zoned item just moved to a zoned or alphanumeric item); STRICT does not.

PAC, NOPAC

An implicit class test for packed-decimal (COMP-3) senders; for an even number of digits the unused bits are checked as well.

BIN(TRUNCBIN|NOTRUNCBIN), NOBIN

A check, similar to ON SIZE ERROR, that the content of a binary sender does not exceed its PICTURE. COMP-5 items are not checked. TRUNCBIN generates the check even under TRUNC(BIN); NOTRUNCBIN skips it for modules compiled with TRUNC(BIN).

MSG, ABD

MSG issues a run-time warning message with the line number, the data item name, the data item content and the program name, and the statement still runs; ABD issues a terminating message that ends in an abend.

NUMCHECK alone means NUMCHECK(ZON(ALPHNUM,STRICT),PAC,BIN(TRUNCBIN),MSG). If you name only some data types, the others are off: NUMCHECK(BIN) is NUMCHECK(NOZON,NOPAC,BIN(TRUNCBIN),MSG), and if every data type is off the listing shows NONUMCHECK. For CALL, BY CONTENT zoned and packed arguments are checked and BY REFERENCE ones are not. When the compiler can see at compile time that a check will always find invalid data it issues a compile-time message and drops the run-time check. ZONECHECK is deprecated; it is replaced by NUMCHECK(ZON(ALPHNUM)). NUMCHECK(ZON(LAX)) cannot be used together with NUMPROC(PFD). The Programming Guide warns that NUMCHECK is much slower than NONUMCHECK, and that more of the redundant checks are removed at higher OPT levels, so fewer messages appear there. It is a testing option, not a production setting.

The option is given in the PARM of the compile step or in a CBL (PROCESS) statement at the start of the program. These two fragments are z/OS; not run here:

//COBOL    EXEC IGYWCL,PARM.COBOL='NUMCHECK(ZON,PAC,MSG),TEST'
       CBL NUMCHECK(ZON,PAC,ABD)
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. NIGHTLY.

Use MSG while hunting for the records that carry bad data, and ABD in a test where you want the run to stop at the first one. The procedures and the way compiler options are specified are in Compiling, Linking and Language Environment and Performance and Compiler Options.

Source: Programming Guide: NUMCHECK and Programming Guide: Specifying compiler options in the PROCESS (CBL) statement

INVDATA

INVDATA is a compatibility option for programs that were written for COBOL 4 or earlier versions and that depend on what those compilers did with invalid zoned and packed data. The default is NOINVDATA: the compiler assumes all data in USAGE DISPLAY and PACKED-DECIMAL items is valid and generates the most efficient code, for example a string comparison where it can. The Programming Guide recommends NOINVDATA even for programs that process invalid data, and recommends fixing the programs to avoid the invalid data once NUMCHECK shows where it comes from.

INVDATA without suboptions means INVDATA(NOFORCENUMCMP,CLEANSIGN). The suboptions are:

  • FORCENUMCMP makes comparisons of zoned decimal items ignore the zone bits of every digit (the value is converted to packed decimal first); NOFORCENUMCMP generates comparisons the way the older compilers did, with NUMPROC(NOPFD) or NUMPROC(PFD) as before, so the setting of NUMPROC in the new program must match the one used with the old compiler.

  • CLEANSIGN cleans the sign nibble of zoned and packed items on input to comparisons and to ADD, SUBTRACT, MULTIPLY and DIVIDE; NOCLEANSIGN does not, which raises the probability of an S0C7 when an operand has an invalid sign nibble. CLEANSIGN does not apply to SIGN IS SEPARATE items.

INVDATA affects MOVE statements, comparisons and computations on items that may hold invalid digits, signs or zone bits. The Programming Guide gives a migration table: from Enterprise COBOL 4.2 or earlier with valid data use NOINVDATA and the old NUMPROC (NUMPROC(MIG) becomes NOPFD); with invalid data and NUMPROC(MIG) use INVDATA(FORCENUMCMP,NOCLEANSIGN) and NUMPROC(NOPFD); with invalid data and NUMPROC(NOPFD) or PFD use INVDATA with the same NUMPROC. The table speaks of "COBOL 6.2 or later" while some summaries date the option to 6.3, so check the What’s New topic of your release before naming one. A zoned item with an invalid zone code is still non-numeric to the class test even when INVDATA tolerates the zone in comparisons. The wider migration is in Legacy Features and Migration.

Source: Programming Guide: INVDATA and Programming Guide: NUMPROC

Notes on the book

Tom Taulli’s Modern Mainframe Development (O’Reilly, 2022) gives MOVE a short section in its COBOL chapter. It presents MOVE as the COBOL replacement for assignment, shows that a text sender shorter than its receiver is padded with spaces and a longer one is cut off, that a number is aligned on the decimal point and truncated without rounding, and that several receivers can follow one MOVE. It lists three category combinations (alphanumeric to numeric, alphanumeric to numeric-edited, numeric to alphanumeric) as the ones allowed when the sender is an unsigned integer. That is too narrow for the current manuals: the full table above allows many more combinations (signed and non-integer numeric senders, numeric-edited senders that are de-edited, national and floating-point items), and the "unsigned integer" rule applies to an alphanumeric sender moved to a number, not to every pair. The book also points to ON SIZE ERROR as protection against truncation; that phrase belongs to the arithmetic statements, and MOVE has no SIZE ERROR phrase. The book was written against Enterprise COBOL 6.3 with examples run under GnuCOBOL, and it does not cover MOVE CORRESPONDING, de-editing, the group-move padding rule or the invalid-data topics at the end of this page: the names NUMCHECK, INVDATA and S0C7 do not appear in it. Its remark that binary data is risky for arithmetic "because of rounding" is wrong in the sense that matters here: binary integers are exact, and the real issues are TRUNC truncation and the placement of decimals, as shown above. For the current rules use the Enterprise COBOL 6.5 Language Reference and Programming Guide and the GnuCOBOL manual listed below.
