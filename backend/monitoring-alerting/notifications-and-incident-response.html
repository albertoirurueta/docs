Notifications and Incident Response

This content was generated with the assistance of AI and should be verified against the official documentation before being relied on in production.

Checked on 2026-11-09 against Grafana 13.3.x and Kibana 9.5.x.

A rule that fires correctly has done half of its job. The other half happens in the minutes after the evaluation: the alert has to reach the right person on a channel they will see, say enough that they can start working without opening five tabs, and feed a process that coordinates people when the problem is bigger than one engineer. This page covers that second half: the notification channels both stacks offer, how to write the message, how on-call and escalation fit in, the human side of an incident (roles, frameworks, communication) and the review that closes the loop.

It continues Designing Actionable Alerts, which decides which alerts deserve a notification, and builds on Alerting Fundamentals. The tool-specific pages are Contact Points and Integrations, Notification Templates, SLOs and IRM, Connectors, Actions, Frequency and Action Variables and Cases. The Prometheus-side routing is in Alertmanager, and the delivery of Bookshelf’s alerts in a cluster is in Observability, Logging and Metrics. The running example is Bookshelf, described in Metrics and Observability, and the Hands-on Lab provides a webhook receiver that shows exactly what each stack sends.

Choosing a notification channel

Each channel has a different cost of interruption and a different guarantee of delivery. Matching severity to channel is the most effective noise control available after the alert design itself.

Channel Best for Grafana contact point or Kibana connector Limits

Paging (phone, push, SMS) through an on-call tool

Critical, user-visible symptoms that need a person now, with acknowledgement and escalation.

Grafana: PagerDuty, Opsgenie, VictorOps, Grafana IRM, Pushover. Kibana: PagerDuty, Opsgenie, xMatters.

Interrupts people day and night. Must be reserved for the severity level that justifies it.

Chat (Slack, Microsoft Teams, Google Chat, Discord, Webex)

Warnings, informational alerts, incident coordination, and context sharing.

Grafana: Slack, Teams and the other chat integrations. Kibana: Slack, Microsoft Teams.

Easy to miss and to mute. No acknowledgement unless a bot adds it.

Email

Low-urgency summaries, audit trail, reports.

Grafana: Email. Kibana: Email.

Slow, filtered, and easily lost. Grafana’s "Single email" option sends one message for a whole group.

Webhook

Integration with anything else: custom automation, ticket creation, a receiver for testing.

Grafana: Webhook. Kibana: Webhook.

The receiver must be reliable; a failing webhook silently drops notifications unless delivery is monitored.

Ticketing and incident tools

Slow burns, follow-up work, the record that an incident was handled.

Grafana: Jira. Kibana: Jira, Jira Service Management, ServiceNow ITSM, ServiceNow ITOM, IBM Resilient, Swimlane, TheHive.

Tickets pile up unless somebody owns the queue.

In-product case

Tracking an incident inside the Elastic stack with the alerts attached.

Kibana: the Cases connector.

Only visible to people who use Kibana.

The integration lists above come from the products' documentation: Grafana’s contact point page lists Alertmanager, AWS SNS, Cisco Webex Teams, DingDing, Discord, Email, Google Chat, Grafana IRM, Kafka REST Proxy, Jira, Line, Microsoft Teams, MQTT, Opsgenie, PagerDuty, Pushover, Sensu Go, Slack, Telegram, Threema Gateway, VictorOps, Webhook and WeCom (and notes that some are not supported by Prometheus Alertmanager). Kibana’s connector page groups the alerting and case-management connectors as Email, Slack, Microsoft Teams, PagerDuty, Opsgenie and xMatters, a long list of ticketing and incident tools (Jira, Jira Service Management, ServiceNow ITSM, ServiceNow ITOM, ServiceNow SecOps, IBM Resilient, Swimlane, TheHive), security and automation tools, and the generic Webhook. Some connector types are paid commercial features and others are free; check the subscription comparison for the edition you run. The OpenAI, Amazon Bedrock, Google Gemini and AI connectors in the same list are deprecated since Elastic Stack 9.5 and are not alerting channels.

A sensible default mapping for Bookshelf is: critical goes to the paging tool and to the team’s chat channel, warning goes to the chat channel and to a ticket, and informational goes to the chat channel only. Alerts about the monitoring system itself (no data, data source errors, failed notifications) go to the platform team, which is a different audience from the application team. Every channel should also be tested: a contact point that has never delivered a message is a contact point that may not work.

Source: Grafana docs — Configure contact points and Elastic docs — Kibana connectors.

Routing: from alert to destination

The notification pipeline is covered in detail on the tool pages; the design points that matter here are these.

Grafana uses a tree of notification policies. The default policy is the root, matches everything, and so catches any alert that no child policy matches. A child policy inherits its contact point, grouping options and timing options from the parent unless it overrides them. The deepest matching child handles the alert, and Continue matching siblings lets other siblings handle it as well. Matchers on a policy are combined with AND. Grouping collapses many alert instances into one notification and the timing options (group wait, group interval, repeat interval) control when the first message goes out and how often it repeats. The following provisioning file expresses the Bookshelf routing: critical alerts to the paging contact point, DatasourceNoData and DatasourceError alerts to the platform team, and everything else to a ticket queue.

# provisioning/alerting/bookshelf-policies.yaml
apiVersion: 1
policies:
  - orgId: 1
    receiver: bookshelf-ticket
    group_by: ['alertname', 'grafana_folder', 'service']
    group_wait: 30s
    group_interval: 5m
    repeat_interval: 4h
    routes:
      - receiver: bookshelf-webhook
        object_matchers:
          - ['severity', '=', 'critical']
        repeat_interval: 1h
      - receiver: platform-team
        object_matchers:
          - ['alertname', '=~', 'DatasourceNoData|DatasourceError']

Reference: Grafana docs — Provision notification policies with files. The three receivers must exist as contact points before the policy is provisioned; in the lab only bookshelf-webhook exists, so bookshelf-ticket and platform-team are illustrative names you would add as further webhook contact points (the lab’s own policy tree sends everything to bookshelf-webhook). Because the policy tree is a single resource, provisioning it overwrites every existing policy; export the current tree first (see Provisioning Alerting Resources). The DatasourceNoData and DatasourceError alerts do not inherit all labels of the rule that produced them, so a matcher on severity would never see them; match on the alert name as above.

Kibana has no routing tree. Routing is a property of each rule’s actions: a connector, an action group (when it runs: Query matched, Recovered, or a severity group), a frequency, and an optional condition (a KQL filter on the alert document or a time frame). To send critical alerts to the pager and warnings to chat, add two actions to the same rule with different "run when" groups. To keep one team’s alerts out of another’s channel, use tags and per-action filters, or put the rules in separate spaces. Rules support actions that run per alert or that summarise new, ongoing and recovered alerts in a single message, which is the closest equivalent to Grafana grouping.

Source: Grafana docs — Notification policies and Elastic docs — Create and manage rules.

Writing the message

A notification is read on a phone, half awake, by someone who may not know the service. The test of a good message is whether that person can decide the first action without opening the rule. The practical checklist is short:

  • A title that names the symptom and the service, not the metric (Bookshelf: 5xx ratio above 5%).

  • A one-sentence summary of what is wrong and why it matters, from the rule’s summary annotation.

  • The current value and the threshold, so the reader can judge severity.

  • A link to the dashboard (and panel) that shows the symptom, a link to the runbook, and in Grafana a silence link.

  • The owner and the environment, in labels, so the same alert from staging is not mistaken for production.

  • Nothing else. A message that lists every label is as unreadable as one that lists none.

Group-level and instance-level information

One notification can carry many alerts, so templating has two levels, and mixing them is the commonest source of broken messages.

In Grafana the notification template receives the group as the root data (.): .Status is firing when any alert is firing, .Alerts holds every alert, .Alerts.Firing and .Alerts.Resolved filter by state, .GroupLabels are the labels the group was formed on and .CommonLabels and .CommonAnnotations are what every alert in the group shares. Inside range .Alerts, each alert has .Labels, .Annotations, .StartsAt, .EndsAt, .GeneratorURL and .Fingerprint, and for Grafana-managed alerts also .DashboardURL, .PanelURL, .SilenceURL, .Values, .ValueString and .OrgID. The dashboard and panel links cover a time range from one hour before the alert started to its end (or to now while it is firing). The template language is Go templating with helper functions such as toUpper, title, join, reReplaceAll and date. A separate, simpler templating exists for rule labels and annotations, which add context to a single alert instance rather than to a group.

Grafana’s own best practice is the key rule: do not put information about individual alert instances in the notification template, because it then appears only in the notification. Put it in an annotation or label, so that it also shows up in the alert state and its history, and print it from the template. The following template follows that rule and uses the lab’s annotations.

{{ define "bookshelf.title" }}[{{ .Status | toUpper }}] {{ .GroupLabels.alertname }} ({{ .Alerts.Firing | len }} firing){{ end }}

{{ define "bookshelf.message" }}
{{ range .Alerts.Firing }}
* {{ .Labels.service }}: {{ .Annotations.summary }}
  Runbook: {{ .Annotations.runbook_url }}
  Dashboard: {{ .DashboardURL }}
  Silence: {{ .SilenceURL }}
{{ end }}
{{ range .Alerts.Resolved }}
* RESOLVED {{ .Labels.service }}: {{ .Annotations.summary }}
{{ end }}
{{ end }}

Source: Grafana docs — Notification template reference and Grafana docs — Template notifications. Template limits worth knowing: you cannot add HTML or CSS to emails, cannot change the layout of Slack or Teams messages, and cannot add webhook headers beyond those configured. Namespaced functions such as coll., data., tmpl. and time. are documented as not yet generally available in Grafana Cloud.

The same template and a webhook contact point, as Terraform, ready for the lab’s receiver (the receiver URL is supplied as a variable because it depends on the lab profile):

terraform {
  required_providers {
    grafana = {
      source  = "grafana/grafana"
      version = ">= 4.47.0"
    }
  }
}

variable "webhook_url" {
  type        = string
  description = "URL of the lab webhook receiver."
}

resource "grafana_message_template" "bookshelf" {
  name = "bookshelf"

  template = <<-EOT
    {{ define "bookshelf.title" }}[{{ .Status | toUpper }}] {{ .GroupLabels.alertname }} ({{ .Alerts.Firing | len }} firing){{ end }}
    {{ define "bookshelf.message" }}
    {{ range .Alerts.Firing }}
    * {{ .Labels.service }}: {{ .Annotations.summary }}
      Runbook: {{ .Annotations.runbook_url }}
      Dashboard: {{ .DashboardURL }}
    {{ end }}
    {{ end }}
  EOT
}

resource "grafana_contact_point" "bookshelf_page" {
  name = "bookshelf-webhook"

  webhook {
    url     = var.webhook_url
    title   = "{{ template \"bookshelf.title\" . }}"
    message = "{{ template \"bookshelf.message\" . }}"
  }

  depends_on = [grafana_message_template.bookshelf]
}

Reference: Grafana docs — Provision alerting resources with Terraform (the grafana_message_template and grafana_contact_point examples) and grafana_contact_point (the webhook block with url, title and message). The provider requires a Grafana service account token in GRAFANA_AUTH and the URL in GRAFANA_URL; the full provider setup is on Observability as Code.

In Kibana the message is built from action variables in Mustache syntax. The general variables work in every rule: {{rule.name}}, {{rule.id}}, {{rule.spaceId}}, {{rule.tags}}, {{rule.params}}, {{rule.url}} (a link to the rule, empty if server.publicBaseUrl is not set), {{kibanaBaseUrl}} and {{date}}. For actions that run per alert there are {{alert.id}}, {{alert.uuid}}, {{alert.actionGroup}}, {{alert.consecutiveMatches}} and {{alert.flapping}}, and the rule-specific data under {{context.*}} (for the custom threshold rule {{context.reason}}, {{context.value}}, {{context.group}}, {{context.alertDetailsUrl}} and {{context.viewInAppUrl}}). For actions that summarise alerts there are {{alerts.new.count}}, {{alerts.ongoing.count}}, {{alerts.recovered.count}} and {{alerts.all.count}}, each with a .data array. This is the same group-versus-instance split: summaries work at the group level and per-alert actions at the instance level. Values are escaped in connector fields that need it (the webhook body keeps JSON valid), and triple braces such as {{{context.message}}} switch the escaping off, which can produce invalid output; use them deliberately. The tag {{{.}}} prints the whole variable context as JSON, which is the quickest way to find the path of a variable while building an action. The Mustache enhancements (.asJSON, and the EvalMath, ParseHjson, FormatDate and FormatNumber lambdas) are in technical preview.

A webhook connector and a rule action for the lab, as Terraform. The connector id is set explicitly so the rule’s action can reference it; the rule is the documented index-threshold example with a per-alert action that sends the Mustache body.

terraform {
  required_providers {
    elasticstack = {
      source  = "elastic/elasticstack"
      version = "~> 0.16"
    }
  }
}

provider "elasticstack" {
  kibana {}
}

variable "webhook_url" {
  type        = string
  description = "URL of the lab webhook receiver."
}

resource "elasticstack_kibana_action_connector" "lab_webhook" {
  name              = "lab-webhook"
  connector_id      = "7f1b0f0e-2f5a-4a6e-9a31-0b8f6c8a1d11"
  connector_type_id = ".webhook"
  config = jsonencode({
    url     = var.webhook_url
    method  = "post"
    hasAuth = false
  })
}

resource "elasticstack_kibana_alerting_rule" "bookshelf_example" {
  name         = "bookshelf-index-threshold"
  consumer     = "alerts"
  rule_type_id = ".index-threshold"
  interval     = "1m"
  enabled      = true
  tags         = ["bookshelf", "bookshelf-sre"]

  params = jsonencode({
    aggType             = "count"
    groupBy             = "all"
    timeWindowSize      = 5
    timeWindowUnit      = "m"
    threshold           = [10]
    thresholdComparator = ">"
    index               = ["bookshelf-logs-*"]
    timeField           = "@timestamp"
  })

  actions {
    id    = elasticstack_kibana_action_connector.lab_webhook.connector_id
    group = "threshold met"
    frequency {
      summary     = false
      notify_when = "onActionGroupChange"
    }
    params = jsonencode({
      body = "{\"rule\": \"{{rule.name}}\", \"reason\": \"{{context.message}}\", \"link\": \"{{rule.url}}\"}"
    })
  }
}

Reference: elasticstack_kibana_action_connector, elasticstack_kibana_alerting_rule and Elastic docs — Webhook connector. The rule parameters follow the registry example for .index-threshold; the index name and the threshold met action group are values to check against your rule type with the Kibana rule types API. Variables available to a rule depend on its type, so confirm context.message with the {{{.}}} trick before relying on it. If xpack.actions.allowedHosts is configured on the Kibana server, the receiver’s hostname must be on the list.

Source: Elastic docs — Rule action variables.

On-call schedules and escalation

A notification is not a response. Somebody has to be accountable for answering at any hour, and the system needs a rule for what happens when they do not. Two mechanisms do this.

An on-call schedule assigns responsibility to a person (or a team) for a period that ranges from hours to a week or more. Chapter 7 of Cloud Observability in Action describes the trade-off between a traditional model, where an operations team is on call for services it did not write, and a model where developers carry the pager for their own service, which gives them a strong incentive to instrument and document well. An escalation policy defines what happens if the first responder does not acknowledge, or cannot solve the problem alone. Chapman and Holmes list the questions it must answer: who is notified for an automatically detected issue and whether this changes out of hours or by severity, who is notified if the first responder is unavailable, who takes over if the first responder cannot resolve it and by what criteria (duration, severity, time of day), how the handover is done, and what happens when several incidents occur at once. They also place a duty on leadership: everyone on call must know the policy, junior engineers must not feel they are disturbing seniors by escalating, and on-call schedules should be audited so that nobody is worn down by them.

How the two stacks implement this differs:

  • Grafana IRM is the Grafana Cloud product for on-call management and incident response. Its documentation lists alert groups (incoming alerts are grouped, routed, acknowledged, escalated and resolved), on-call schedules (rotations, shifts, overrides), escalation chains (steps that decide how alerts move to the next responder) and integrations with Grafana Alerting, other monitoring tools, Slack, Microsoft Teams and the Grafana mobile app. IRM is a Grafana Cloud capability, documented as an add-on of Grafana Cloud; the documentation does not describe a self-managed or open-source variant, so on a self-managed Grafana use the PagerDuty, Opsgenie, VictorOps or webhook contact points to a third-party tool. The contact point named "Grafana IRM" sends Grafana alerts to it. Schedules and escalation chains can be managed with Terraform (the provider has grafana_oncall_schedule and grafana_oncall_escalation_chain resources, and the on-call resources use the provider’s url and auth set to a service account token).

  • Kibana has no native on-call scheduling or escalation. Use the PagerDuty, Opsgenie or xMatters connectors, which hand the alert to a system that owns the schedule. The rule decides what is sent and when; the on-call tool decides who receives it.

Source: Grafana Cloud docs — IRM, Grafana Cloud docs — On-call schedules and Elastic docs — PagerDuty connector.

Incident roles and frameworks

When a problem needs more than one person, the fastest way to waste time is to have nobody decide who is doing what. The established incident frameworks agree on this, and Chapman and Holmes name four to start from rather than inventing a new one: ITIL incident management, SRE incident management, the NIST incident response framework and the SANS incident response framework. Three roles recur in all of them:

Role Responsibility

Commander (incident manager)

Holds the authority to decide. Calls in the right people, signs off customer communication, handles senior leadership, and is the person every other role reports to. The commander coordinates and does not debug.

Communications lead

Owns internal and external communication: status updates to stakeholders, the customer-facing status page and messages. Keeps a log of what was said.

Technical lead

Directs the technical responders when several teams are investigating, decides what to try next, and knows who is looking at what.

Large organisations add a second axis borrowed from the UK emergency services: gold (strategic: senior management, media and legal questions, approving the tactical plan, leading the review), silver (tactical: managers who turn strategy into a plan and pass information between gold and bronze) and bronze (operational: the responders who take control of the incident, inform silver when it is declared, find the cause and complete the post-incident report). The value is that everyone knows beforehand how to escalate to leadership when an incident is serious.

Three practical conventions make the roles work. Give a small named group authority to declare an incident and set its severity, so that declaration does not depend on consensus. Automate what follows a declaration: open a dedicated chat channel, update the status page, notify the on-call tools. And pre-write the customer messages, including a "we are investigating" message for the case where an alert has fired but customer impact is unconfirmed, so that the person who was woken at 3 a.m. selects a message rather than composes one. Hausenblas adds the question the communication role must answer early: whether customers are really impacted or only the canaries, how many, and where.

The following diagram shows the whole flow from the alert to the postmortem. The hand-offs at the top are automated; the decisions in the middle are human.

flowchart TD A["Alert rule fires (symptom, severity label)"] --> B["Notification policy or rule action routes it"] B --> C{"Pager or chat?"} C -->|critical| D["Page the on-call responder"] C -->|warning or info| E["Chat message or ticket"] D --> F{"Acknowledged within the policy time?"} F -->|no| G["Escalate to the next step of the chain"] G --> D F -->|yes| H["Triage: read the summary, open the dashboard and runbook"] E --> H2["Handle in working hours"] H --> I{"User impact confirmed and not fixed quickly?"} I -->|no| J["Fix or mitigate, resolve the alert"] I -->|yes| K["Declare an incident and set its severity"] K --> L["Assign commander, communications lead and technical lead"] L --> M["Open incident channel or case, post status update"] M --> N["Investigate and mitigate, keep the timeline"] N --> O{"Service restored?"} O -->|no| N O -->|yes| P["Resolve the incident, notify stakeholders"] J --> Q["Review the alert: was it useful, timely, well described?"] P --> R["Blameless postmortem within days"] R --> S["Action items with owners"] S --> Q Q --> T["Tune rules, runbooks, routing and templates"]

Source: Chapman and Holmes, Observability with Grafana, chapter 9; the structure follows the Google SRE incident and postmortem material at SRE book — Managing Incidents and SRE book — Postmortem Culture.

Silences, snoozes and maintenance windows

During an incident the same alert fires every repeat interval and distracts the people trying to fix the problem. Grafana’s best-practice advice is to use silences during incidents to cut repeat notifications, scoped narrowly (by label matchers) so that unrelated alerts still reach people. A silence is for a known, unplanned condition; a mute timing is the tool for a recurring, planned window. The notification template’s .SilenceURL puts a pre-filled silence link in the message so that acknowledging noise is one click.

In Kibana the equivalents are snooze (the rule keeps checking on schedule but its alerts do not trigger actions; for a set period, indefinitely or on a one-time or recurring schedule, cancellable at any time) and maintenance windows, which suppress notifications across several rules while alerts are still created. Maintenance windows are generally available since Elastic Stack 9.2 (the page metadata says GA since 9.2 while the body text says 9.1; this page follows the metadata), use KQL filters instead of the older rule categories, and also suppress the recovery notifications of alerts that fired during the window. A few rule types (Stack Monitoring, tracking containment, anomaly detection job health, transform health) are not affected by the filters. Per-alert snooze is generally available in Elastic Stack 9.5 and replaces per-alert mute; check the release notes for the version you run.

Source: Grafana docs — Create a silence and Elastic docs — Snooze and disable rules.

Post-incident review

The review is where an incident becomes an improvement. The Google SRE book’s postmortem chapter and Chapman and Holmes agree on its shape. It is blameless: it focuses on contributing causes and assumes everyone acted in good faith with the information they had, because blame teaches people to hide problems. That is not the same as removing accountability; it removes the fear that makes honesty impossible. A review is expected after any significant event. Criteria defined in advance (user-visible downtime beyond a threshold, any data loss, an on-call intervention such as a rollback, a long resolution time) avoid argument, and the SRE chapter adds one more trigger that matters for this section: a monitoring failure, which usually means a person found the incident instead of an alert.

A good postmortem contains the written record, the impact, the actions taken, the root causes, the follow-up actions that prevent recurrence or reduce impact, and the key data (graphs, logs, the timeline). It is reviewed for completeness before it is shared widely and stored in a repository the whole organisation can search. Action items are preventive, have an owner and a priority, and are tracked to completion; an unreviewed postmortem has little effect.

For alerting specifically, close the loop with these questions, which come from Grafana’s best-practices page:

  • Which alerts fired, and did they help?

  • Did any come too early, too late, or without enough context?

  • Which alert should have fired and did not (the monitoring-failure trigger)?

  • Which thresholds, priorities, escalation steps, annotations or runbook sections should change?

Record the answers as pull requests against the alert definitions (see Observability as Code) so the improvement is reviewed, versioned and visible in the next review.

Source: Google SRE book — Postmortem Culture and Grafana docs — Alerting best practices, section 9.

Grafana IRM

Grafana IRM keeps on-call and incident response in the same product as the metrics, logs and traces that raised the alert. Its incident management documentation describes four stages:

Stage What it covers

Declare

Create an incident record from anywhere in Grafana, from Slack or through the API. Declaration includes severity levels and labels.

Document

Keep a chronological timeline of observations, metrics and decisions. Entries can be notes, queries and dashboard panels, and investigation context can be collected and shared there.

Collaborate

Add participants and assign roles, and work from Slack with /grafana commands. The documentation lists the Slack integration; it does not mention Microsoft Teams for incident management.

Resolve

Track and complete action items, and update the status. After resolution the timeline and insights support post-incident analysis and trend review.

The documentation does not name specific roles, define the severity levels, or describe a status page feature or a review template; use the sub-pages (declare an incident, timeline, investigate, collaborate, add participants, manage tasks) for the configuration and decide your own role and severity definitions using the frameworks above. Two caveats apply: IRM is documented as part of Grafana Cloud, billed separately as an add-on, and the documentation URL layout for these pages changed (older material and some links still point to grafana-cloud/alerting-and-irm/irm/, which now links onward to grafana-cloud/observe-and-act/respond-to-incidents/).

Source: Grafana Cloud docs — Manage incidents and Grafana Cloud docs — Respond to incidents.

Kibana Cases

Cases track an incident inside Elastic: a place to attach alerts and files, assign people, add comments, and push the case to an external system. They are generally available on Elastic Cloud Serverless and on the Elastic Stack, and are scoped by solution: a case created in Observability, in Security or in Stack Management is visible only in that solution, and an alert can be attached only to a case in the same solution (an Observability alert cannot be attached to a Security case).

To create a case, open Cases (global search, or Stack Management > Cases on the Elastic Stack) and select Create case. The required fields are the name, the severity and the description; optional ones are a category, assignees, tags and additional fields. A template pre-fills the values (managed on the Templates page on Serverless and on Elastic Stack 9.5 and later, in case settings on 9.0 to 9.4). Sync alert status is on by default and keeps the status of the attached alerts in step with the case. A connector selected at creation pushes the case to an external system automatically. Email notifications to assignees need a configured email connector (notifications.connectors.default.email on self-managed Kibana) and server.publicBaseUrl for links back to the case.

Cases connect to alerting in two ways. A rule can have an action that uses the in-Kibana Cases connector ("Add alerts to Cases"), so that matching alerts are attached to a case without anyone copying them; and an analyst can attach alerts, visualizations and files to an existing case manually. In the Terraform provider the resources elasticstack_kibana_case_template and elasticstack_kibana_case_field_definition manage templates and the field library as code. Cases cover the record-keeping and coordination parts of the incident flow above. They are not an on-call tool, a status page or a postmortem template, so pair them with a paging connector and a written review process.

Common mistakes

Mistake Consequence and remedy

Everything goes to one channel

People mute it. Map severity to channels and keep the pager for critical alerts.

Escalation policy that exists only on paper

Nobody acknowledges at night. Test the chain with a drill and audit the schedule.

Notification template with instance details only in the template

The detail is missing from alert history. Put it in annotations and print it from the template.

No dashboard, runbook or silence link in the message

Responders open the rule and hunt for context. Template the links.

One person is commander, communicator and debugger

Communication stops while they fix. Split the roles as soon as an incident is declared.

Postmortem skipped for "small" incidents or written as blame

The same incident repeats. Define triggers in advance and keep the review blameless.

Webhook or contact point failing silently

Alerts fire and nobody is told. Monitor delivery with the notification history and the Alertmanager metrics.

In the books

Chapter 9 of Observability with Grafana (Chapman and Holmes, Packt, 2023) is the main book source for this page: the before, during and after structure of an incident, the commander, communications and technical lead roles, the ITIL, SRE, NIST and SANS frameworks, the gold-silver-bronze structure, escalation questions and blameless postmortems. That material is tool-independent and still valid. The tools part of the chapter was written for Grafana 10.2 and presents Grafana OnCall and Grafana Incident as separate products with their own pages, and the alerting walkthrough uses the 10.x UI. Today they are the on-call, alert group, escalation and incident capabilities of Grafana IRM in Grafana Cloud, and the contact point integration is named Grafana IRM. Chapter 7 of Cloud Observability in Action (Hausenblas, Manning, 2023) covers incident management as three phases (detect, react and handle, learn), on-call models (traditional operations team versus developers on call) and the separation of an engineering role from a communication role, then alerting with Prometheus and Grafana; its Grafana alerting walkthrough predates the current rule editor and contact point list. Publisher pages: Observability with Grafana (Packt) and Cloud Observability in Action (Manning).

Verify in the lab

  • Start the profile you use and open the webhook receiver. Force the 5xx alert as described in Designing Actionable Alerts and read the payload: does the title name the symptom, and are the summary, runbook, dashboard and (in Grafana) silence links present and valid?

  • Resolve the condition and confirm the resolved message arrives. Then disable resolve messages on the contact point (Grafana: Disable resolved message) and confirm they stop, then re-enable them.

  • In Grafana, break the data source and check that the DatasourceError alert follows the platform route in the policy above and not the critical route. Open the notification history (Loki-backed on OSS and Enterprise) and find the delivery record, including a Failed badge if you point a contact point at an address that does not answer.

  • In Kibana, open the rule details and read the History tab (failed actions) after pointing the connector at a wrong URL.

  • Run a table-top incident: declare a fictitious incident, assign the three roles, write the status message from a pre-written template, and finish with a ten-minute blameless review that produces one action item against an alert or a runbook. Record it as a pull request.
