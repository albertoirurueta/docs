== What a CMS is

A *content management system* (CMS) lets people who are not programmers create, edit, review and publish the content of
a website or application, while developers decide how that content is stored, structured and presented. Whatever the
product, five ideas recur:

* *Content model.* The kinds of content the system stores (articles, pages, products, events, authors) and the fields,
  relationships and taxonomies that describe each kind.
* *Editorial workflow.* How content moves from draft to published: authoring, review, scheduling, versioning and
  rollback.
* *Roles and permissions.* Who may create, edit, publish or administer content and settings.
* *Presentation.* How stored content becomes pages, through templates, themes, visual designers or an application that
  you write yourself.
* *Extensions.* The plugins, apps, integrations and APIs that add behaviour the core product does not provide.

== Traditional, headless and hybrid

A CMS is classified by how tightly it couples the content store to the presentation layer.

* *Traditional (coupled).* The same system stores the content and renders the pages. Editors, designers and visitors all
  talk to one product, which is the quickest route to a working site and keeps hosting simple. The cost is that the
  front end is bound to the product's templating model.
* *Headless (decoupled).* The system stores and manages content and exposes it only through an API. Any front end, such
  as a single-page application, a static site generator or a mobile app, fetches the content and renders it. You gain
  freedom of technology and channel, and you take on building and hosting the front end yourself.
* *Hybrid.* The system renders pages itself and also exposes its content through an API, so a site can use the built-in
  presentation for most pages while a separate application consumes the same content.

== Open source versus SaaS

The second axis is who runs the software.

* *Open source and self-hosted.* You install the software on servers you control or rent, can read and change the
  source, and choose the surrounding stack. You are also responsible for hosting, backups, updates, performance and
  security patches.
* *SaaS (software as a service).* The vendor runs the platform, applies updates for every customer at once and bills a
  subscription. You customize through the surfaces the vendor exposes, such as a visual designer, an app marketplace,
  APIs and webhooks, and you do not control the underlying infrastructure.

The two axes are independent: SaaS products can be traditional, headless or hybrid, and so can open source ones.

== When a CMS fits, and when a framework does

A CMS is the right tool when the main job is publishing and organizing content that non-developers keep changing, with
standard features such as pages, media, forms, search engine optimization and user roles. A web framework is a better fit
when the product is mostly custom application logic, such as unusual workflows, heavy business rules or bespoke data
models, and content editing is a small part of it. The two combine well: a framework application can consume a headless
or hybrid CMS for its editorial content. For the framework side see xref:web/php-laravel/index.adoc[PHP and Laravel].
