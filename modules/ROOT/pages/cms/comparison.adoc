= CMS Comparison
:description: Side-by-side comparison of WordPress, Webflow and fourteen other content management systems (Drupal, Joomla, TYPO3, Craft CMS, Wix, Squarespace, Contentful, Strapi, Sanity, Payload, Adobe Experience Manager, Umbraco, Wagtail and Ghost): dated and cited metrics for popularity, growth, talent pool, ease of use, ecosystem, features, performance, cost, security, maintenance and lock-in, charts, scores on twelve axes, a decision flowchart and a summary of which CMS to choose today and for what.
:keywords: CMS comparison, content management system, WordPress, Webflow, Drupal, Joomla, TYPO3, Craft CMS, Wix, Squarespace, Contentful, Strapi, Sanity, Payload, Adobe Experience Manager, Umbraco, Wagtail, Ghost, headless CMS, Core Web Vitals, CVE, total cost of ownership, market share, radar chart, decision guide

This page compares the two content management systems documented in the xref:cms/index.adoc[CMS section] of this site,
xref:cms/wordpress/index.adoc[WordPress] and xref:cms/webflow/index.adoc[Webflow], with fourteen other systems that
matter because they are popular, because they are growing, or because they show a different way of building the same
site: traditional open-source systems, hosted website builders, headless and API-first products, and enterprise or
stack-specific systems. Every number was fetched on *2026-10-29* from the sources listed in the
<<_bibliography,Bibliography>>, and the figures change monthly, so treat the page as a dated snapshot. The data and the
scripts that draw the figures are in `scripts/cms-comparison-charts/`, and the charts can be regenerated with
`npm run charts:cms-comparison`.

The page follows the method of the xref:ecommerce/comparison.adoc[E-commerce Platforms Comparison], the
xref:web/frameworks-comparison.adoc[Web Frameworks Comparison] and the
xref:documentation-tools/comparison.adoc[Documentation Tools Comparison]: data kept in CSV files, charts generated from
them, a stated source and date for every figure, and `n/a` for anything that could not be sourced. It does not repeat
what the platform sections explain: for the detail of one platform, follow the links to the
xref:cms/wordpress/comparison-and-decision-guide.adoc[WordPress comparison and decision guide] and the
xref:cms/webflow/comparison-and-decision-guide.adoc[Webflow comparison and decision guide]. To go straight to the
conclusion, read the <<_summary_and_recommendations,summary and recommendations>> at the end.

== Scope and inclusion criteria

*What is compared.* Sixteen systems in four groups. The groups are not the same kind of product, and the page says so
wherever it matters:

[cols="2,3,3",options=header]
|===
|Group
|Systems
|What they have in common

|Traditional open-source (self-hosted)
|xref:cms/wordpress/index.adoc[WordPress] (documented on this site), https://www.drupal.org/[Drupal], https://www.joomla.org/[Joomla], https://typo3.org/[TYPO3], https://craftcms.com/[Craft CMS]
|Server-side PHP applications that the site owner (or a host) installs, patches and upgrades, with a plugin, module or extension ecosystem. WordPress, Drupal, Joomla and TYPO3 are free software; Craft CMS publishes its source under a commercial licence.

|Hosted website builders (SaaS)
|xref:cms/webflow/index.adoc[Webflow] (documented on this site), https://www.wix.com/[Wix], https://www.squarespace.com/[Squarespace]
|The vendor runs the servers, the editor and the upgrades; the owner configures a site inside the vendor's product.

|Headless and API-first
|https://www.contentful.com/[Contentful], https://strapi.io/[Strapi], https://www.sanity.io/[Sanity], https://payloadcms.com/[Payload]
|Content is stored and edited in the CMS and delivered through an API to a separate front end. Contentful and Sanity host the content store; Strapi and Payload are open-source applications that the owner can host.

|Enterprise and other stacks
|https://experienceleague.adobe.com/en/docs/experience-manager-cloud-service[Adobe Experience Manager] (AEM, enterprise context), https://umbraco.com/[Umbraco] (.NET), https://wagtail.org/[Wagtail] (Python and Django), https://ghost.org/[Ghost] (Node.js publishing)

|===

*Context rows.* https://www.shopify.com/[Shopify] appears only in the market-share and performance charts, because W3Techs
counts it as a content management system; it is compared with its competitors in the
xref:ecommerce/comparison.adoc[E-commerce Platforms Comparison] and is not scored here. Drupal 7 and Joomla 3 appear
only in the support-window chart, to show what the end of a major line looks like (Drupal 7 ended on 2025-01-05 and
Joomla 3 on 2023-08-17).

*Inclusion criteria.* A system is included if it ranks in at least one main source (the W3Techs share of websites, the
HTTP Archive origins, the Stack Overflow Developer Survey, registry downloads or GitHub stars) or if it matters for
enterprise or historical context. WordPress and Webflow are always included. A system without public data for a metric
gets `n/a` for that metric: hosted SaaS products have no repository, registry or advisory data, and Payload is not a
technology in the HTTP Archive Tech Report, so it has no performance series and no origins count.

*Out of scope.* E-commerce platforms (Shopify, PrestaShop, Magento and WooCommerce, covered by the
xref:ecommerce/comparison.adoc[E-commerce Platforms Comparison]; WooCommerce appears only as an extension of WordPress),
static-site generators and documentation tools (see
<<_when_you_do_not_need_a_cms,when you do not need a CMS>>), and the systems that were considered and left out for
lack of reachable primary data or marginal rank: Statamic, Concrete CMS, Duda, HubSpot Content Hub, Directus, Storyblok,
Hygraph, Sitecore, Optimizely CMS, Blogger, Tilda, Weebly, Movable Type and Plone. WordPress.com is covered as the hosted
variant of WordPress, in the cost section only.

*Snapshot date.* All values were fetched on *2026-10-29*, and every CSV row carries its own `fetched` column. Time series
end at the latest complete period at that date: 2026-09 for the HTTP Archive Tech Report, and 2026 to date for the
yearly series. A value that could not be obtained is shown as *n/a*, never as zero, and a value that came from a page
that could not be opened from the authoring environment, or from a secondary source, is marked *unverified*.

== How metrics, scales and scores were assigned

*Rules that apply throughout.*

* Absolute values from different sources are never plotted on one axis, because they count different things: W3Techs
  counts the share of websites among the sites it analyses, the HTTP Archive counts origins visited by Chrome users, and
  registries count downloads, which include continuous-integration and mirror traffic. Downloads from different
  registries (npm, Packagist, PyPI, NuGet) are never compared with each other.
* Field data and counts are reported as measured. Where a number is a proxy (CVEs as a proxy for security, Stack
  Overflow questions as a proxy for community activity), the section says what it cannot show.
* Colours identify a CMS inside its group: the palette has eight slots for sixteen systems, so a colour is consistent
  across the figures of one group, and figures that mix groups colour by group or say in their caption that colours
  are assigned by position in that figure.
* A pair of numbers from different dates is never compared as if it were one series. INP (Interaction to Next Paint)
  replaced FID (First Input Delay) in the Core Web Vitals definition on 2024-03-12, so pass rates before and after that
  date are not strictly comparable, and the charts mark the break.
* Stack Overflow's site-wide decline since 2023 affects every tag, so question counts for recent years say little about
  a CMS on their own.

*Scores.* The radar charts and the <<_scores_on_twelve_axes,scores table>> use twelve axes, each scored from 0 to 10 with
higher always better. Five axes are *computed* from the data by a formula implemented in `scores.mjs`, and seven are
*editorial*: a written rubric applied to cited evidence, with the basis and the source ids of every score recorded in
`radar-scores.csv`. A system whose inputs are missing gets no score, drawn as a gap and never as zero.

[cols="2,1,6",options=header]
|===
|Axis
|Kind
|Rule

|Popularity
|Computed
|Mean of the available log scores of the W3Techs share of all sites (0.01% scores 0, 50% scores 10) and of the HTTP Archive mobile origins (100 scores 0, 5,000,000 scores 10), latest values. It counts visible fingerprints, so headless CMSs are underestimated; Payload has neither input and gets no score.

|Growth
|Computed
|Ratio of the latest HTTP Archive mobile origins to the same month three years earlier: 5 plus 5 times the base-2 logarithm of the ratio, limited to 0 to 10 (unchanged is 5, doubled is 10, halved is 0). No score without both months.

|Talent pool (Stack Overflow)
|Computed
|Log score of the Stack Overflow questions asked with the CMS tag in the last two complete years, 2024 and 2025 (10 questions score 0, 10,000 score 10). The axis measures developer activity only: the job-market signals of <<_talent_pool_and_job_market,Talent pool and job market>> (survey usage, Hacker News job mentions) cover too few CMSs to score, so they are not part of it.

|Ecosystem and maturity
|Computed
|Mean of the available log scores of the GitHub stars of the main repositories (1,000 scores 0, 100,000 score 10; the WordPress, Drupal and TYPO3 mirrors excluded) and of the total Stack Overflow questions of the tag (100 scores 0, 300,000 score 10). Hosted systems have no repository, so only the second input counts.

|Performance
|Computed
|Mean of the mobile and desktop Core Web Vitals pass rates divided by 10 and of the mobile and desktop median Lighthouse performance scores times 10, latest HTTP Archive month. No score when one input is missing.

|Ease of use (editors)
|Editorial
|Can editors write, preview, schedule and approve without a developer; the visual building, draft preview, scheduling and workflow rows of the feature matrix are quoted in the basis.

|Ease of use (developers)
|Editorial
|Concepts to learn before a first custom template or content type, local setup, API and documentation; the content modelling, configuration-as-code and REST rows of the feature matrix are quoted.

|Features and productivity
|Editorial, rule-based
|Points per feature of the 19-row feature matrix (yes 1, by extension 0.7, partial 0.5, n/a 0) divided by 19, times 10. `n/a` means no evidence was found, so it earns no credit, and scope differs by CMS type.

|Low cost
|Editorial
|10 is free software with low hosting cost; lower for paid plans and licences. USD and EUR prices are not converted. No score where no sourced price exists (Contentful and AEM).

|Security posture
|Editorial
|Patching model, advisory process, severity and exploitation history, never raw CVE counts, which are not comparable between SaaS and self-hosted systems.

|Low maintenance effort
|Editorial
|10 is vendor-managed with no customer patching; lower for self-hosted systems with frequent major upgrades and short support windows.

|Portability (low lock-in)
|Editorial
|Licence, data format, export and the ability to change host.
|===

The scores summarise the evidence on this page. They are not a benchmark, and one point is within the uncertainty of the
method.

== Popularity

Popularity answers one question: how many websites run this CMS, and how much attention does it get from developers?
No single source answers it, so the page uses four that count different things and never puts them on one axis: the
W3Techs share of websites, the HTTP Archive count of origins, the activity of the developer ecosystem (GitHub, registries)
and the questions asked on Stack Overflow. All of them count what can be *seen* from outside, which favours
systems that leave a visible fingerprint in the page. A headless CMS serves content to a front end that shows no sign
of it, so it is underestimated by W3Techs and the HTTP Archive; the popularity score of this page has that bias, and
the registry downloads below are the better signal for headless products.

=== Market share

W3Techs reports the share of all websites it analyses and the share of the websites with a known CMS. WordPress is on
39.5% of all websites and on 58.3% of the websites with a known CMS, which is more than the other fifteen systems
and Shopify together. Wix (4.2%), Squarespace (2.4%) and Webflow (0.8%) lead the hosted builders, and Joomla (1.1%),
Drupal (0.6%), TYPO3 (0.3%) and Craft CMS (0.1%) are small shares of a very large denominator. W3Techs reports "less than
0.1%" for Umbraco, Strapi, Sanity and Wagtail, which is too small to give a number, and does not track Contentful as a
content management system; for AEM it tracks only the umbrella "Adobe Systems" (0.6% of all websites), so the AEM cells

=== Developer activity and downloads

GitHub stars and contributors measure the interest of developers in an open-source CMS. They are not usage, and
the main repository of some systems is a read-only mirror: the WordPress repository is a mirror of the Subversion
repository (21,509 stars, and 3,496 more on the `wordpress-develop` repository where core development happens), and the
Drupal and TYPO3 repositories are mirrors of their own Git hosts. Hosted SaaS products (Webflow, Wix, Squarespace,
Contentful) and AEM are closed and have no repository (`n/a`). The leaders are Strapi (73,401 stars, 1,412 contributors),
Ghost (55,630 and 943) and Payload (45,570 and 592), the Node.js CMSs, followed by WordPress (mirror), Wagtail (20,594
and 1,125), Sanity (6,380), Umbraco (5,278 and 1,215), Joomla (5,157 and 1,210), Drupal (4,295, mirror), Craft CMS
(3,621) and TYPO3 (1,218 stars, mirror, and 1,289 contributors).

and are not counted in Packagist (`n/a`).

=== Reading the popularity data

WordPress leads every signal that counts websites by a wide margin: about 40% of all websites, 58% of those with a
known CMS and about 2.7 million detected mobile origins, 15 times more than the next system. Wix is a distant
second, then Drupal, Squarespace, Joomla and Webflow in the same order of magnitude. Among developers the leaders are
different: the Node.js CMSs (Strapi, Ghost, Payload) lead GitHub stars, and Contentful, Sanity and Payload lead the
growth of npm downloads. Popularity should be read together with the next section: the origins of most systems peaked
before 2025.

== Growth trends

Growth is measured with the yearly W3Techs share and the monthly HTTP Archive origins. The two series agree on direction
for the systems they share, and they show that the market is consolidating around WordPress and the hosted builders at
the top while the older open-source systems decline.

The yearly W3Techs share of all websites shows WordPress rising from 23.3% in 2015 to 43.6% in 2025 and then falling
to 39.5% in 2026-10, the first fall in the series. Wix rose from 0.1% to 4.2% and Squarespace from 0.2% to 2.4%
(Shopify, as context, from 0.3% to 5.3%), and Webflow from 0.1% in 2019 to 0.8%. Joomla fell from 3.3% in 2015 to 1.1% and Drupal from 2.0% to
0.6%.

== Talent pool and job market

The size of the talent pool decides how easy it is to hire a developer or an agency. Public data on it is thin, and
the page uses what exists, labelled with its limits.

*Developer survey.* The Stack Overflow Developer Survey lists only a few CMSs under web frameworks and technologies:
WordPress, Drupal and (in 2024 only) Strapi. Joomla, TYPO3, Craft CMS, the hosted builders, Contentful, Sanity, Payload, AEM,
Umbraco, Wagtail and Ghost are not answer options in 2023 to 2026 (`n/a`). The share of respondents who did extensive
development work with WordPress was 13.38% in 2023, 11.8% in 2024, 13.61% in 2025 and 13.3% in 2026; for Drupal it was
1.87%, 1.9%, 2.22% and 2.0%; Strapi had 1.7% in 2024. Among professional developers the figures are close
(WordPress 12.59% in 2023, 11.1% in 2024 and 12.38% in 2025; Drupal 1.85%, 1.9% and 2.07%; the 2026 professional
breakdown is not published). The share of users who want to use it again (admired) was 26.5% for WordPress in 2026,
down from 34.5% in 2024, and 37.7% for Drupal, up from 25.69% in 2023; Strapi was 43.4% admired in 2024. The survey
sample shrank from 71,802 respondents to the web frameworks question in 2023 to 10,281 in 2026, so shares are less precise
in the later years.

image::cms-comparison-stackoverflow-usage.svg["Line chart of the share of Stack Overflow Developer Survey respondents who used WordPress, Drupal and Strapi, from 2023 to 2026",width=860]

Figure 16: share of Stack Overflow Developer Survey respondents who did extensive development work with the CMS in the past
year, 2023 to 2026. Source: https://survey.stackoverflow.co/[Stack Overflow Developer Survey]. Only these CMSs are

*Job demand.* The share of the "Ask HN: Who is hiring?" posts on Hacker News that mention each CMS is a demand proxy
biased to startups. In 2025, WordPress was mentioned by 14 of 4,032 top-level comments (0.35%), Webflow by 12 (0.3%), Wix by

Wagtail and Ghost had none. In 2026 to date (2,933 comments in 10 threads), Squarespace has 3 mentions (0.1%), Wix 2 (0.07%), and Webflow and
Sanity 1 each, and WordPress has none. The series is too sparse for a score and the talent-pool score uses Stack Overflow
questions instead.

*Certification and partner programmes.* They signal how easy it is to hire an agency. Both a certification and a partner
programme exist for TYPO3 (certified consultant, developer, integrator and editor, plus official partners), Webflow
(Webflow University certifications and the Webflow Certified Partner programme), Umbraco (training certification in three
tiers, and partners), Sanity (Sanity Learn certifications and agency partners) and Adobe (Adobe Certification and the
Adobe Partner Program, neither specific to AEM). Joomla has a certification exam (Certification Administrator) but no
partner programme page. Craft CMS (Craft Verified partners), Strapi, Payload, Wix (Wix Studio Partner Program),
Squarespace (Squarespace Circle) and Ghost (the Ghost Experts directory) have partner programmes and no certification
page. Drupal's certification is run by the vendor Acquia, not by the Drupal Association, and its partner programme page
could not be opened (*unverified*). WordPress has free training on Learn WordPress but no certification or partner
programme page, and Wagtail has neither. Contentful's pages were unreachable (HTTP 429, *unverified*). Salary by CMS is not
published by any source used here (`n/a`).

*Stacks and hiring.* A CMS shares its language and framework with a wider talent pool. The PHP CMSs draw on the
developers described in xref:web/php-laravel/index.adoc[PHP and Laravel], Wagtail on those of
xref:web/django/index.adoc[Django], Umbraco on those of xref:web/aspnet/index.adoc[ASP.NET], and the headless front
ends of Contentful, Sanity, Strapi and Payload usually on xref:web/nextjs/index.adoc[Next.js] and
xref:web/react/index.adoc[React] developers; the language-level context is in the
xref:programming-languages/comparative.adoc[programming languages comparison].

The talent-pool scores computed from Stack Overflow questions in 2024 and 2025 are 8.4 for WordPress, 5.0 for TYPO3,
4.8 for Strapi, 4.6 for Wix, 3.9 for Drupal and 3.8 for Wagtail, and at most 2.8 for the others; Ghost scores 0.0 and Craft CMS 0.1
because they had almost no tagged questions. WordPress has by far the largest pool of developers and agencies, and
Webflow has the clearest dedicated certification and partner path.

== Ecosystem and maturity

*Ecosystem and maturity* is a computed score: the mean of the log score of the GitHub stars of the main repository
(1,000 scores 0, 100,000 scores 10) and of the log score of the total Stack Overflow questions of the tag (100 scores 0,
300,000 scores 10). The WordPress, Drupal and TYPO3 repositories are read-only mirrors whose stars understate their
communities, so for those three only the Stack Overflow part counts. The score rewards developer attention and accumulated
community knowledge, which is a sign of maturity, and it undervalues hosted systems that have no repository. The scores are
WordPress 9.4, Strapi 6.8, Drupal 6.6, Wix 5.8, TYPO3 5.7, Ghost 5.4, Wagtail 5.3, Joomla 5.0, AEM 4.9, Payload 4.2,
Umbraco 4.2, Sanity 3.3, Contentful 2.7, Squarespace 2.6, Craft CMS 2.0 and Webflow 1.1; the last two figures show the limit of the method, because Webflow's
extension ecosystem is a closed marketplace that a repository count cannot see.

The ecosystem a project depends on is better described by what exists around the core:

* *Extensions:* WordPress.org lists 73,152 plugins and 8,799 themes (2026-10-29, official count, a live figure). The
  directories of the other systems (Drupal modules, the Joomla Extensions Directory, the TYPO3 Extension Repository, the
  Craft Plugin Store, the Umbraco Marketplace, the Webflow Marketplace, the Wix App Market) were not counted from a
  primary source, so their sizes are `n/a` and are not estimated. On Packagist, 1,640, 4,315 and 2,948 packages require
  Drupal core, TYPO3 core and Craft CMS respectively, a measure of third-party code built on each.
* *Managed hosting and cloud offers:* WordPress.com (Free to Commerce plans, 0 to 70 USD per month billed monthly),
  Craft Cloud, Strapi Cloud, Umbraco Cloud, Ghost(Pro), the Webflow site plans and the Wix and Squarespace plans; Payload
  Cloud shows no public price (`n/a`) and Contentful's pricing was unreachable.
* *Licences and business model:* WordPress (GPL), Drupal (GPL), Joomla (GPL-2.0) and TYPO3 (GPL-2.0) are free
  software; Strapi, Payload, Umbraco and Ghost are MIT-licensed and Wagtail is BSD-3-Clause, each with a paid cloud
  or enterprise offer; Craft CMS publishes its source under the Craft License and needs a commercial licence for Team and
  Pro projects; Sanity's Studio is open source but its content store is a hosted service; Webflow, Wix, Squarespace,
  Contentful and AEM are proprietary.
* *Current lines and support:* WordPress 7.1, Drupal 11, Joomla 6, TYPO3 14 LTS, Craft CMS 5, Strapi 5, Ghost 6, Umbraco
  17 LTS and Wagtail 7.4 LTS, with dated windows in <<_support_windows,Support windows>>. Drupal 7 (ended 2025-01-05) and
  Joomla 3 (ended 2023-08-17) are the cautionary tales of the previous generation.

For the platform stacks, see xref:web/php-laravel/index.adoc[PHP and Laravel] (the PHP CMSs), xref:web/django/index.adoc[Django]
(Wagtail), xref:web/aspnet/index.adoc[ASP.NET] (Umbraco) and xref:web/nextjs/index.adoc[Next.js] (headless front ends).

== Performance

*What it measures.* The *Core Web Vitals pass rate* is the share of the origins detected for a CMS in which at least 75% of
real Chrome page loads are good on all three vitals: Largest Contentful Paint (LCP), Interaction to Next Paint (INP)
and Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS). It comes from the HTTP Archive Tech Report, which combines the technology detection of
the crawl with the Chrome UX Report field data. The *median Lighthouse performance score* is a lab score (0 to 100) of
the same origins from the monthly crawl under a throttled test profile, so it is lower than the real-user experience on
fast devices. Both describe populations of sites, not the product in a controlled test.

*Caveats.* Field data is per origin, not a benchmark: it reflects themes, plugins, hosting and audiences as much as the
CMS. INP replaced FID on 2024-03-12, so the series are not strictly comparable across that date, and every chart marks
the break. Small or shrinking populations can be biased by survivor effects, and Strapi, with 14 mobile origins, has
percentages that say little. A headless CMS does not decide the performance of the site it feeds: the front end does,
so the numbers for Contentful, Strapi and Sanity describe the sites that happen to be detected, and the choice of
front end is discussed in the xref:web/frameworks-comparison.adoc[Web Frameworks Comparison]. API latency and rate limits
published by the headless vendors were not collected for this page (`n/a`), and no house measurement is used.

== Security

*What the data can and cannot show.* CVE counts measure *disclosure*, not real risk: a project with a mature security team
and a large ecosystem publishes many CVEs, and a project that fixes silently publishes few. The sections below use four
kinds of evidence: the CVEs published per year in the National Vulnerability Database (NVD), the advisories of the
projects themselves, notable incidents with their CVSS score and their entry in the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities
(KEV) catalogue, and the patching model of each product. The security score of the radar charts is editorial and is based
on the patching model, the advisory process and the severity and exploitation history, never on raw CVE counts.

=== Why self-hosted CMSs with large plugin ecosystems have a larger attack surface

Three facts in the data explain why the exposure of the self-hosted CMSs, and of WordPress in particular, is larger:

* *The site owner is the patch manager.* On a self-hosted system a fix does not protect a site until someone applies it.
  The KEV catalogue added the 2026 Drupal SQL injection two days after publication and the Craft CMS flaw CVE-2025-32432
  eleven months after, which shows that exploitation continues long after a fix exists.
* *Third-party code runs with the privileges of the site.* Plugins, modules and extensions run inside the same PHP process, with
  access to content and users. 91% of the new WordPress vulnerabilities of 2025 were in plugins (Patchstack), Drupal publishes 126 contributed-module
  advisories against 8 for the core, and the exploited Joomla flaws of 2026 are in extensions.
* *The stack is larger.* The web server, PHP or Node.js, the database and the operating system are the owner's (or the
  host's) responsibility, together with the CMS and every extension.

A hosted builder removes the first and third of these, but not the risk of third-party apps with excessive access scopes
or of compromised editor accounts. See the WordPress
xref:cms/wordpress/security-hardening.adoc[security hardening] page for the measures on that platform.

=== Why SaaS CVE counts are not comparable

Webflow, Wix, Squarespace, Contentful and Sanity (the hosted content store) have no NVD series, and the same is true of
their advisories (`n/a`). This does not mean they have fewer vulnerabilities. When a SaaS vendor fixes a vulnerability in
its platform, it deploys the fix on its own servers and the customers take no action, so the fix does not become a CVE:
CVEs exist to tell the users of *installed* software what to update. A count of zero or near zero for a SaaS product
therefore means that its vulnerabilities do not need to be announced to the customers. The same applies in reverse: a
large number of CVEs for a self-hosted product shows transparent disclosure as much as a large number of vulnerabilities,
and AEM's several hundred CVEs a year show how one vendor files its components, not how insecure AEM is. Counts should be
compared only within one deployment model, and even then they measure disclosure effort, not risk. Hosted vendors are
better compared by their security programmes and compliance (SOC 2 and ISO 27001 attestations, bug bounty and
disclosure programmes, single sign-on, role granularity), which this page does not score numerically because the
attestations were not collected from primary sources (`n/a`); the security scores of the hosted systems reflect only
that the vendor, and not the customer, patches the platform.

*Reading the security data.* The evidence supports three statements. First, the owner of a hosted site delegates platform
patching, which removes a whole class of risk. Second, on self-hosted systems the exploited flaws cluster in extensions
(WordPress plugins, Joomla extensions) and in a few core flaws that are exploited within days, so patching speed and the number of third-party
extensions a site installs decide the exposure. Third, Drupal, TYPO3 and WordPress have an advisory process and automatic
or coordinated updates, while the young headless projects publish few advisories, which could mean fewer problems or less
scrutiny. Share of detected sites running unsupported versions, hacked-site report shares and per-version install
statistics were not collected for this snapshot (`n/a`).

== Maintenance

*Maintenance effort* is the work needed to keep a site secure and supported: applying core, plugin and theme updates,
running backups, moving to a new major line before the old one ends, and keeping the hosting stack current. The editorial
score is 10 for a vendor-managed product with no customer patching and lower for self-hosted systems with frequent major
upgrades and short support windows.

Reading the scenarios gives three conclusions. First, *labour is the largest component* in every scenario: about 70% of
the total for the brochure site, 61% for the blog, 76% for the corporate site and 86% for the headless setup. The assumed hours are those of
a site that is patched by a developer (updates, backups, security patches and minor edits), so they are the hours that
a hosted system removes: a hosted builder (maintenance score 9 to 10) saves more than any licence discount, while a free
system with a heavy upgrade path (Drupal and TYPO3, maintenance score 4) costs more in people than its licence saves.
Second, *usage limits decide the real price of hosted and API-first products*: seats and locales (Sanity per-seat pricing,
Webflow Localize per locale), API calls and bandwidth (Strapi Cloud, Sanity) and CMS items (Webflow Premium) change the plan
that a scenario needs. Third, the *multilingual and headless scenarios* are where plans start to matter: Webflow's Team
platform plan is USD 2,500 a month on an annual contract and Umbraco Cloud Professional is EUR 730 a month, which is
to be put against the labour and hosting of an equivalent self-hosted setup.

== Summary and recommendations

The data snapshot date for every figure below is *2026-10-29*, and the data changes monthly (the HTTP Archive and
W3Techs figures each month, the NVD and the registries every day). The recommendations are justified only by the data on
this page. They name the option the evidence favours first and what to check; they are not rankings, and a team with deep
experience of one system can reasonably choose it against the table.

*In short.* Choose WordPress for a self-hosted site where the largest talent pool, ecosystem and portability matter and where
you can pay for managed hosting and plugin vetting, and Webflow for a design-led marketing site that should have no
server to run and no patching. For headless content, Sanity (hosted) or Payload and Strapi (self-hosted) are the
current choices, Drupal or TYPO3 fit complex multilingual or regulated sites, and Joomla, Drupal 7 and
other end-of-life lines should be kept only for existing sites.

=== Trends, cautions and legacy

* *Trends to watch.* Sanity (+228% detected origins in three years and rising downloads), Umbraco (+74%), Wagtail (+61%) and
  Webflow (+21%) are the only systems whose detected origins grew in three years, and Payload has the fastest npm growth; each
  starts from a small base except Webflow.
* *Established, but under pressure.* WordPress and Wix lost about a fifth of their detected origins since 2023, and the
  W3Techs share of WordPress fell for the first time, from 43.6% to 39.5%. They remain the largest by far, so the
  figures describe a market that is consolidating, not collapsing.
* *Keep for legacy maintenance, avoid for new projects.* Drupal 7 (ended 2025-01-05), Joomla 3 (ended 2023-08-17),
  Joomla 4 (ended 2025-10-14), Craft 4 (security ended 2026-04-30) and Strapi 4 (security ended 2026-06-09) are out of
  support. Joomla as a whole declines in every popularity signal (origins -48%, W3Techs share from 3.3% to 1.1%, weakest
  growth), so a new project is better served by another system.
* *Cost and security perspective.* The hosted builders minimise labour (maintenance 9 to 10) and move patching to the
  vendor, which removes the largest class of risk, at the price of portability (2 to 3). Among the self-hosted systems the
  exploited flaws cluster in extensions and a few core flaws exploited within days, so budget for patching speed and for
  a short list of vetted extensions.
* *Hiring perspective.* WordPress has by far the largest pool (talent score 8.4 and 13.3% of the 2026 survey
  respondents). TYPO3, Drupal and Webflow have dedicated certification and partner paths; the headless systems draw on
  JavaScript and TypeScript developers, and Umbraco and Wagtail on .NET and Python ones.

=== Framework alternatives

When the requirements go beyond content (application logic, a customised user experience, data that is not editorial), a
web framework may fit better than a CMS, or a CMS can be used as the editorial back end of a framework-built front end.
The xref:web/frameworks-comparison.adoc[Web Frameworks Comparison] and the
xref:backend/frameworks-comparison.adoc[Backend Frameworks Comparison] cover Next.js, Laravel, Django and the others;
the stacks are described in xref:web/nextjs/index.adoc[Next.js], xref:web/php-laravel/index.adoc[PHP and Laravel] and
xref:web/django/index.adoc[Django].

=== When you do not need a CMS

A CMS earns its cost when non-developers edit content regularly. A documentation site or a small site edited by developers
in Git needs none: a documentation generator or a static-site generator (Hugo, Jekyll, Eleventy or Gatsby, which are out
of scope here) gives lower hosting cost, no patching of a database-backed application and no plugin attack surface. See the
xref:documentation-tools/comparison.adoc[Documentation Tools Comparison] for that choice.
