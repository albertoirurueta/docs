== Play Console Accounts

Every app on Google Play is published from a **Play Console** developer account, and the account type shapes both
the verification the owner goes through and, for newer accounts, how quickly a first app can reach production.

[cols="1,2,2",options="header"]
|===
| | Personal account | Organization account

| Who it is for
| An individual -- a student, hobbyist or solo developer, such as the one publishing TrailMate
| A legal entity -- a company, nonprofit, government body or other organization

| Identity evidence
| Legal name, address, a government ID and a payment card in the same legal name; verified contact email and
  phone
| A **D-U-N-S Number** for the organization (Play uses it to verify the organization's identity), plus verified
  organization contact details

| Mandatory for
| --
| Some app categories must be published from an organization account: financial services (banking, crypto
  exchanges and similar), health apps, VPN apps and government apps

| Extra gate before production
| Accounts created after **November 13, 2023** must complete a closed test with at least 12 testers opted in
  for 14 continuous days (see <<closed-testing-requirement>>)
| None of that kind

| Functionality once verified
| The same as an organization account, including monetization through a payments profile
| The same as a personal account
|===

Both account types pay the same **one-time registration fee of US$25**, and the account holder must be at least
18. Registration is: sign in with a Google Account, accept the Google Play Developer Distribution Agreement, pay
the fee, choose the account type, and complete **identity verification**. Details that do not match the ID or
payment card cause the verification to fail, and the registration fee is not refunded in that case -- so the
legal name on the account should be entered exactly as it appears on the documents.

Choosing between the two is mostly a question of who owns the app. A personal account is tied to one person;
moving an app to an organization account later is possible through an app transfer, but it is extra paperwork
and it changes the developer name shown on the listing. If TrailMate were ever to become a company product, it
would be cleaner to start in an organization account from day one.

=== Developer Verification

Separately from the Play Console identity checks, Google is rolling out **Android developer verification**: a
requirement that the developer of *any* app installed on a certified Android device -- not only apps distributed
through Play -- is verified and has registered the app's package name, so that the device can block installation
of apps from unverified developers regardless of where the APK came from. It applies to certified devices running
Android 7 and higher.

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
| When | What happens

| August 2026
| Developer verification launches: the developer APIs, limited-distribution accounts and an "advanced flow"
  that lets power users knowingly install unverified apps.

| September 30, 2026
| Enforcement begins on certified Android devices in **Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand**, for
  installs from participating app stores (Google Play and several OEM stores) and sideloading.

| 2027 onward
| Enforcement rolls out globally.
|===

What this means in practice depends on how an app is distributed:

* **Play developers** are already verified through Play Console. Google Play registers the vast majority of
  apps automatically; Play Console is also where a Play developer manually registers any remaining package names,
  including apps that are *also* distributed outside Play. TrailMate, published on Play, needs no extra step
  beyond confirming its package is registered.
* **Developers who distribute exclusively outside Play** sign up in the new **Android Developer Console**
  instead. After verifying their identity, they register each package name on the console's *Packages* page by
  entering it together with the SHA-256 fingerprint of the app's signing certificate, and -- for a package name
  that already exists in the wild -- prove ownership by uploading an APK signed with that key. Registration can
  also be automated with the Android Developer Console API.
* **Limited-distribution accounts** are for students, teachers and hobbyists who only want to share an app with
  a small circle: they can install on up to **20 devices**, need no government-issued ID and pay no registration
  fee. This is the right path for a personal prototype that will never be published broadly.

Because verification is keyed on the package name *plus* the signing certificate, the key-management decisions
from xref:apps/android/building-signing-and-releasing.adoc[Building, Signing and Preparing a Release] now matter
for sideloaded builds as well as Play builds: a package name registered with one key cannot simply be re-signed
with another.

== Setting Up the App in Play Console

Creating an app record (**Create app** on the Play Console home page) asks for the default language, the app
name, whether it is an app or a game, and whether it is free or paid -- a free app can never later become paid.
The **Dashboard** then shows a checklist of tasks that must be completed before a release can reach any track
beyond internal testing. They fall into two groups: the **store listing** (what users see) and **app content**
declarations (what Play reviews).

=== Store Listing

The main store listing is the product page on Google Play. Its text and graphics have hard limits:

[cols="1,2,2",options="header"]
|===
| Element | Requirement | TrailMate's choice

| App name
| Up to 30 characters
| `TrailMate: Hiking Trails`

| Short description
| Up to 80 characters; shown on the listing and in search results
| "Find hiking trails near you, save favorites and record your hikes offline."

| Full description
| Up to 4,000 characters
| Features, offline support, units, privacy summary

| App icon
| 512 x 512 px, 32-bit PNG with alpha, at most 1,024 KB
| Exported from the adaptive icon's foreground on a solid background

| Feature graphic
| 1,024 x 500 px, JPEG or 24-bit PNG (no alpha); also the cover for a preview video
| A trail photo with the app name

| Screenshots
| At least 2 across device types, up to 8 per device type; 320 px to 3,840 px per side; JPEG or 24-bit PNG
| 4 phone screenshots (list, detail, recording, settings) plus 7-inch and 10-inch tablet sets from the adaptive
  layouts

| Preview video
| Optional YouTube URL, public or unlisted, ads disabled
| None at launch
|===

Play's listing guidelines also forbid promotional claims in the listing text and graphics ("Best", "#1",
"Download now", price or ranking badges), and expect screenshots to show the actual in-app experience rather than
marketing art. Large-screen screenshots matter: without them, Play shows the listing less prominently on tablets,
foldables and ChromeOS, which undoes much of the work described in
xref:apps/android/adaptive-layouts-edge-to-edge-and-large-screens.adoc[Adaptive Layouts, Edge-to-Edge and Large
Screens].

**Localized listings.** Each listing field can be translated per language (**Grow users -> Store presence ->
Main store listing -> Manage translations**), either by entering translations manually or by buying them from
Play's translation service. Screenshots and the feature graphic can also be localized. A user whose device is set
to a language with a translation sees it; everyone else sees the default language. TrailMate already ships
`values-es/` and `values-de/` string resources (see
xref:apps/android/resources-and-configuration.adoc[Resources, Configuration, Localization and Themes]), so it
makes sense to translate the listing into the same languages -- a Spanish UI behind an English-only listing looks
unfinished. **Custom store listings** go further and show a different listing to a specific country or to users
arriving from a particular URL.

=== App Content Declarations

The **Policy and programs -> App content** page collects every declaration Play reviews against the binary:

* **Privacy policy** -- a public URL (not a PDF, not geofenced, not behind a login) describing what data the app
  accesses, collects, uses and shares. It is required for every app that targets children and for every app that
  handles personal or sensitive user data -- TrailMate's location access and user photos included -- and in
  practice for almost every app, because the Data safety form links to it.
* **App access** -- if any part of the app is behind a login, a subscription or a location check, reviewers
  need a way in: working demo credentials, instructions, or a flag that disables the restriction. TrailMate's
  optional account sign-in (used for sync) gets a dedicated review account whose credentials are entered here,
  never shipped in the app.
* **Ads** -- whether the app contains ads, including ads served by any bundled SDK. Apps that declare ads get an
  "Contains ads" label on the listing. TrailMate has none.
* **Content rating** -- the IARC questionnaire: questions about violence, sexuality, language, controlled
  substances, gambling, user interaction and the sharing of location with other users. Play converts the answers
  into ratings for each regional authority (ESRB, PEGI, USK, ClassInd and others) automatically. Answering
  inaccurately can get the app removed; answering again is required whenever the app's content changes.
* **Target audience and content** -- the age groups the app is designed for. Selecting any group under 13
  brings in the Families policy (restricted SDKs, ad networks and data collection, and a stricter review), so
  TrailMate declares 13+ and adult audiences only.
* **Data safety** -- described next.
* Further declarations that apply only to some apps: news apps, COVID-19 apps, government apps, financial
  features, health apps, advertising ID usage, and the **permission declarations** for sensitive permissions such
  as background location, SMS or call log (TrailMate must justify `ACCESS_BACKGROUND_LOCATION` if it ever requests
  it; see xref:apps/android/permissions-privacy-and-security.adoc[Permissions, Privacy and Security]).

=== The Data Safety Form

The **Data safety** form produces the "Data safety" section on the store listing. For each data type it asks
whether the app **collects** it (sends it off the device), whether it **shares** it (transfers it to a third
party), whether collection is **optional**, whether it is **encrypted in transit**, whether users can **request
deletion**, and for what **purposes** (app functionality, analytics, personalization, account management,
fraud prevention, and so on). The answers cover the whole app, including every bundled SDK -- an analytics or
crash-reporting library is data collection even though TrailMate's own code never touches its network calls.

A worked mapping for TrailMate:

[cols="1,1,1,2",options="header"]
|===
| Data type | Collected? | Shared? | Purpose and notes

| Precise location
| Yes (only when the user uploads a recorded hike)
| No
| App functionality; optional; processing of GPS points for the recorded track happens on-device otherwise

| Photos
| Yes (only when attached to a trail review)
| No
| App functionality; optional; user can delete them from the app

| Email address
| Yes (optional sign-in for sync)
| No
| Account management; deletion available in-app and via a web form

| Crash logs and diagnostics
| Yes (crash-reporting SDK)
| No
| Analytics / app functionality; not linked to identity
|===

Data processed only on the device, or sent end-to-end encrypted and never readable by the developer, does not
count as "collected". Play cross-checks the form against the app's declared permissions and observed network
behavior, so the common failure is not deliberate misrepresentation but drift: an SDK added later, or a new
feature that uploads something, without a form update. The form is part of every release checklist, not a
one-time launch task. The broader privacy context is in
xref:apps/android/permissions-privacy-and-security.adoc[Permissions, Privacy and Security].

== Releases

With the listing and declarations complete, releases are created under **Test and release**. A release is
always *created on a specific track*, contains one or more App Bundles (identified by `versionCode`), carries
release notes, and is then sent for review and rolled out.

=== Play App Signing

xref:apps/android/building-signing-and-releasing.adoc[Building, Signing and Preparing a Release] covers signing
the App Bundle with an **upload key**. **Play App Signing** -- mandatory for every new app, since App Bundles are
required -- is the other half: Play verifies the upload signature, discards it, and signs the APKs it generates
from the bundle with an **app signing key** that Google holds in its key management service. Enrollment happens
the first time a bundle is uploaded to a new app: Play Console offers to generate the app signing key (the
recommended default) or to use an existing one exported with the Play Encrypt Private Key (PEPK) tool, which is
what an app migrating from self-signed APKs does to keep its signature.

The consequences of the split are worth spelling out:

* A **lost or leaked upload key** is recoverable: the account owner requests an upload key reset in **Test and
  release -> App integrity**, registers a new upload certificate, and carries on. A lost app signing key under
  the old self-managed model meant the app could never be updated again.
* **API keys and App Links** that pin a certificate fingerprint (Maps API keys, OAuth clients, `assetlinks.json`)
  must use the **app signing key**'s SHA-256 fingerprint, shown on the App integrity page -- not the upload key's,
  which only Play ever sees. This mismatch is the most common "works in debug, broken on Play" bug after launch.
* The App integrity page is also where a **key upgrade** (rotating to a stronger app signing key, for new
  installs and, via APK Signature Scheme v3 rotation, for existing ones) is requested.

=== Uploading the App Bundle

Before the first upload, it is worth building the bundle and checking which certificate it is signed with, since
a bundle signed with the debug key or with the wrong upload key is rejected at upload time:

[source,bash]
----
# Run from the TrailMate project root.
./gradlew :app:bundleRelease

# The artifact Play Console expects.
ls -lh app/build/outputs/bundle/release/app-release.aab

# Print the signing certificate; its SHA-256 must match the upload certificate registered in Play Console.
keytool -printcert -jarfile app/build/outputs/bundle/release/app-release.aab
----

See https://developer.android.com/studio/publish/upload-bundle[Android Developers -- Upload your app to the Play Console].

In Play Console the upload itself is part of creating a release: choose a track, **Create new release**, drag
the `.aab` into the **App bundles** area, and Play processes it -- validating the signature, the `versionCode`
(it must be higher than every `versionCode` already uploaded to the app, on any track), the `targetSdk`, the
16 KB page-size alignment of any native libraries, and the manifest. The **App bundle explorer** then shows the
generated APK splits, their download sizes per device, and any warnings. The R8 `mapping.txt` uploaded
alongside the bundle (Play extracts it automatically when it is embedded, or it can be attached manually) is
what makes the crash stack traces in Android vitals readable.

=== Testing Tracks

A bundle is uploaded once and then **promoted** between tracks without rebuilding -- the exact binary that was
tested is the one that reaches production:

[cols="1,1,3",options="header"]
|===
| Track | Audience | Purpose and characteristics

| Internal testing
| Up to 100 testers, added by email list
| Available to testers within minutes, before the full review completes. Every TrailMate build lands here first
  to confirm the signed artifact installs, starts and signs in.

| Closed testing
| Email lists (up to 2,000 users per list) or Google Groups; several closed tracks can coexist
| Invite-only beta, reviewed like a production release. Also the track that satisfies the new-account testing
  requirement below.

| Open testing
| Anyone who joins from the store listing; unlimited by default, or capped at a number no lower than 1,000
| Public beta for device diversity and load, with testers' feedback kept private rather than shown as public
  reviews.

| Production
| All Play users in the selected countries and regions
| The public release, usually rolled out in stages (see below).
|===

Testers join a test through an **opt-in URL** shown on the track's *Testers* tab; after a track is published for
the first time the link can take several hours to become active. The tester's Google Account must be in the
list (or group), and the tester must accept the invitation from the opt-in page -- being on the email list alone
does not install anything. Once opted in, the test version arrives as a normal Play update. **Internal app
sharing** is a separate, even faster mechanism: any build can be uploaded and shared by link with no versionCode
rules and no review, which suits QA of individual feature branches.

[[closed-testing-requirement]]
==== The 12-Testers-for-14-Days Requirement

For a **personal developer account created after November 13, 2023**, Play will not grant access to the
production track until the developer has run a **closed test with at least 12 testers who have been opted in
continuously for at least 14 days**. Once the requirement is met, the developer **applies for production
access** from the Dashboard, answering questions about the closed test (how testers were recruited, what
feedback came in), about the app, and about production readiness; that application is itself reviewed, usually
within seven days. Organization accounts and older personal accounts are exempt.

"Continuously" is the operative word: a tester who opts out on day 10 resets nothing for the others but no
longer counts, so recruiting a few more than 12 is prudent. For TrailMate the practical plan is to start the
closed test as soon as the app is minimally usable, with a Google Group of friends and hiking-club members, and
to keep shipping updates to that track during the 14 days -- the production-access questions ask what was
learned and changed, and an untouched build suggests the test was only a formality.

=== The Pre-Launch Report

Every bundle uploaded to a testing track triggers a **pre-launch report**: Google installs the app on a range of
real devices in its test lab, crawls it automatically for a few minutes, and reports **stability** issues
(crashes, ANRs), **performance** (startup time, frame rendering, memory), **accessibility** findings (small touch
targets, missing content descriptions, low contrast), **security and trust** warnings (vulnerable libraries,
cleartext traffic), and screenshots of every screen the crawler reached, per device and language.

Two settings under **Test and release -> Testing -> Pre-launch report -> Settings** make the report much more
useful for TrailMate:

* **Test account credentials** so the crawler can get past the optional sign-in screen (a dedicated test
  account, never a real user's).
* A **Robo script** -- a recorded sequence of user actions uploaded in the same settings page -- that drives
  the crawler through the critical path -- open a trail, start a recording -- before it explores on its own.

The pre-launch report does not block a release, but a crash it finds on day one is a crash production users would
have found on day two. It complements, rather than replaces, the tests described in
xref:apps/android/instrumented-and-ui-testing.adoc[Instrumented and UI Testing].

=== Release Notes

Each release carries **release notes** ("What's new"), up to **500 Unicode characters per language**, shown on the
listing and in the Play Store's update list. In Play Console they are entered in one text box, with each language
wrapped in its BCP-47 language tag on separate lines:

[source,text]
----
<en-US>
Offline maps now load twice as fast.
Recorded hikes show elevation gain.
Fixed a crash when rotating the trail detail screen.
</en-US>
<es-ES>
Los mapas sin conexión cargan el doble de rápido.
Las rutas grabadas muestran el desnivel acumulado.
Corregido un fallo al girar la pantalla de detalle.
</es-ES>
----

See https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9859348[Google Play Console Help -- Prepare and roll out a release].

Release notes are for users: they should say what changed for them, not list ticket numbers, and Play's
guidelines forbid using them for promotion. Keeping them in the repository (one file per locale, as the CI
example below does) means they are reviewed with the code and can be uploaded automatically.

=== Production and Staged Rollouts

The **first** production release of a new app is published to all selected countries at once. Every update
after that can use a **staged rollout**: the release is offered to a random percentage of users -- for example 5%,
then 20%, 50% and finally 100% -- while the rest stay on the previous version. Users in the rollout get the update
normally; those outside it see no update until the percentage grows. Rollouts can also be limited to specific
countries or regions first.

Managing a rollout happens on the track's *Releases* tab:

* **Increase rollout** -- raise the percentage. There is no automatic progression; each step is a deliberate
  decision after checking Android vitals, the crash rate and new reviews for the users who already received it.
* **Halt rollout** -- stop offering the release to anyone new. Users who already installed it *keep* it: Play
  cannot downgrade an installed app. A halted release can be **resumed** later if the problem turns out to be
  unrelated.
* **Replace a halted or in-progress release** -- the real fix for a bad release is a new bundle with a higher
  `versionCode`. Creating a new release on the production track supersedes the current one; users who got the
  broken version update to the fix, and everyone else skips straight to it.
* **Complete rollout** -- set 100% to finish.

The rollout percentage is the main safety valve of the release process, which is why TrailMate's production
releases always start at 5% and why Android vitals thresholds (below) are evaluated per release. A related
control is **managed publishing**: with it enabled, approved changes wait until the developer clicks *Publish*,
which is useful to coordinate a release with a backend deployment or an announcement.

=== The Review Process

Releases to closed testing, open testing and production -- and changes to the store listing or app content
declarations -- are **reviewed** before they go live. Review checks the binary and the declarations together
against the Developer Program Policies: permissions against the declared use, the Data safety answers against
observed behavior, the listing against the metadata policy, app access instructions against what the reviewer
actually meets. Most reviews complete within hours to a few days; for new or recently changed developer accounts
Google states that reviews can take **up to seven days or longer** in exceptional cases.

The **Publishing overview** page shows the state of each pending change (*In review*, *Approved*, *Rejected*,
*Changes not yet sent for review*). A rejection comes with an email and a policy-status entry naming the policy
and, usually, the offending element; the fix is a corrected release or declaration followed by a new submission,
and an appeal is available when the developer believes the decision is wrong. Planning a public launch date around
a build that has not yet passed review is the classic scheduling mistake -- the safer pattern is to get the
release approved with managed publishing on, then publish on the day.

== Policies That Keep a Listing Live

Publishing once is not the end of the obligations Play attaches to a listing; two policy areas apply to every
update and, in the case of Android vitals, continuously.

=== Target API Level

Google Play requires every app to target a recent Android API level, so that new platform privacy and security
behaviors actually apply to the apps users install:

[cols="2,3",options="header"]
|===
| Requirement | Detail

| New apps and app updates (phone and tablet)
| Must target **API 36 (Android 16)** since **August 31, 2026**. An **extension to November 1, 2026** can be
  requested in Play Console for apps that need more time.

| Wear OS, Android Automotive OS, Android TV and Android XR
| Lower floors apply: API 35 for Wear OS and Android Automotive OS, API 34 for Android TV and Android XR.

| Existing apps that are not updated
| Must target at least API 35 to remain available to new users on devices running a newer Android version than
  the app's target; older targets stay installed for existing users but are not offered to new ones.
|===

For TrailMate this is a one-line setting, already at the required level:

[source,kotlin]
----
// app/build.gradle.kts
android {
    namespace = "com.example.trailmate"
    compileSdk = 37

    defaultConfig {
        applicationId = "com.example.trailmate"
        minSdk = 26
        // Play requires API 36 for new apps and updates since August 31, 2026.
        targetSdk = 36
    }
}
----

See https://developer.android.com/google/play/requirements/target-sdk[Android Developers -- Meet Google Play's target API level requirement].

Raising `targetSdk` is never *only* a number change: each level opts the app into new behaviors. Targeting 35
enforced edge-to-edge; targeting 36 ignores the edge-to-edge opt-out, enables predictive back animations by
default (so `onBackPressed()` is no longer called and `OnBackPressedCallback` must be used) and ignores
orientation and resizability restrictions on screens at least 600 dp wide. Those changes, and how TrailMate
handles them, are covered in xref:apps/android/adaptive-layouts-edge-to-edge-and-large-screens.adoc[Adaptive
Layouts, Edge-to-Edge and Large Screens]. The sensible habit is to raise `targetSdk` shortly after each new
platform release, on a branch, with the behavior-change list open -- not in the week before the Play deadline.

=== Android Vitals

**Android vitals** collects performance and stability data from opted-in devices and reports it in Play Console
under **Monitor and improve -> Android vitals**. A few *core vitals* have **bad behavior thresholds** that affect
the app's standing on Play:

[cols="2,1,1",options="header"]
|===
| Core vital | Overall threshold | Per phone model

| User-perceived crash rate (share of daily active users who saw a crash)
| 1.09%
| 8%

| User-perceived ANR rate (share of daily active users who saw an ANR)
| 0.47%
| 8%

| Excessive partial wake locks
| 5%
| --
|===

Play evaluates these on a **28-day rolling average**. An app above the **overall** threshold may be made less
visible in Play surfaces (search, recommendations); an app above the **per-model** threshold may be less visible
on that device model and may show a warning on its store listing to users of that model. The warning disappears
once the 28-day average recovers. Google has also announced that memory-related metrics will start affecting
store visibility from February 2027.

The practical consequences for a release process:

* **Staged rollouts are where vitals regressions are caught.** Android vitals breaks the metrics down by release,
  so a 5% rollout that doubles the ANR rate is visible within a day or two, long before it can move the 28-day
  average for the whole user base -- halt it and ship a fix.
* **ANRs come from blocking the main thread**: disk or network I/O on the UI thread, long `BroadcastReceiver`
  work, or lock contention. Moving work to coroutines on the right dispatcher, as in
  xref:apps/android/viewmodel-lifecycle-and-coroutines.adoc[ViewModel, Lifecycle-Aware Components, Coroutines and
  Flow], and to WorkManager for deferrable jobs, is the main fix.
* **Crash clusters need symbols.** Without the release's `mapping.txt` (and native debug symbols, if any), the
  stack traces in vitals are obfuscated and much harder to act on.

Diagnosing the underlying problems -- StrictMode, the profiler, Baseline Profiles, ANR traces -- is covered in
xref:apps/android/debugging-code-quality-and-performance.adoc[Debugging, Code Quality and Performance].

== Play Libraries

Several Google Play libraries integrate an app with Play features that have no equivalent in the Android
framework. They talk to the Play Store app on the device, so they only work for installs that came from Play (a
sideloaded or debug build typically gets an error or a "not available" result), and each has a testing fake. The
two TrailMate uses directly are added to the version catalog:

[source,toml]
----
# gradle/libs.versions.toml
[versions]
playAppUpdate = "2.1.0"
playReview = "2.0.2"

[libraries]
play-app-update = { group = "com.google.android.play", name = "app-update", version.ref = "playAppUpdate" }
play-app-update-ktx = { group = "com.google.android.play", name = "app-update-ktx", version.ref = "playAppUpdate" }
play-review = { group = "com.google.android.play", name = "review", version.ref = "playReview" }
play-review-ktx = { group = "com.google.android.play", name = "review-ktx", version.ref = "playReview" }
----

See https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore[Android Developers -- Overview of the Google Play Core libraries].

[source,kotlin]
----
// app/build.gradle.kts

    implementation(libs.play.review.ktx)

See https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/in-app-updates/kotlin-java[Android Developers -- Support in-app updates (Kotlin or Java)].

The `-ktx` artifacts add suspend functions and `Flow`-based APIs (package `com.google.android.play.core.ktx`) on top
of the `Task`-based Java APIs, which is what the examples below use. The managers are provided through Hilt so
that tests and debug builds can swap in fakes:

[source,kotlin]
----

=== In-App Updates

**In-app updates** (`app-update` / `app-update-ktx` 2.1.0) let an app detect that a newer version is available on
Play and install it without the user leaving for the Play Store. There are two flows:

[cols="1,2,2",options="header"]
|===
| | Flexible | Immediate

| User experience
| A dialog offers the update; the download runs in the background while the app stays usable; the app then asks
  the user to restart to install it
| A full-screen Play UI blocks the app until the update is downloaded and installed, then the app restarts

| Who completes the install
| The app, by calling `completeUpdate()` when the user agrees
| Play, automatically

| When to use
| Most updates: new features, non-critical fixes
| Critical updates: a security fix, a broken sync protocol, a data-corrupting bug

| Can the user decline
| Yes
| Yes, by cancelling -- the app then decides whether it can keep running
|===

The app decides which flow to use from the `AppUpdateInfo` Play returns: `updateAvailability()`, whether each
type is allowed, `clientVersionStalenessDays` (days since Play learned about the update on this device), and
`updatePriority` -- an integer from 0 to 5 set **per release** when it is created through the Play Developer
Publishing API (it cannot be set in the Play Console UI). TrailMate's rule: priority 4 or 5 means immediate;
otherwise offer a flexible update once the installed version is three days stale.

[source,kotlin]
----
// app/src/main/kotlin/com/example/trailmate/play/InAppUpdateController.kt
package com.example.trailmate.play

import androidx.activity.result.ActivityResultLauncher
import androidx.activity.result.IntentSenderRequest
import com.google.android.play.core.appupdate.AppUpdateInfo
import com.google.android.play.core.appupdate.AppUpdateManager
import com.google.android.play.core.appupdate.AppUpdateOptions
import com.google.android.play.core.install.InstallStateUpdatedListener
import com.google.android.play.core.install.model.AppUpdateType
import com.google.android.play.core.install.model.InstallStatus
import com.google.android.play.core.install.model.UpdateAvailability
import com.google.android.play.core.ktx.clientVersionStalenessDays
import com.google.android.play.core.ktx.isFlexibleUpdateAllowed
import com.google.android.play.core.ktx.isImmediateUpdateAllowed
import com.google.android.play.core.ktx.requestAppUpdateInfo
import com.google.android.play.core.ktx.updatePriority
import dagger.hilt.android.scopes.ActivityScoped
import javax.inject.Inject
import kotlinx.coroutines.flow.MutableStateFlow
import kotlinx.coroutines.flow.StateFlow
import kotlinx.coroutines.flow.asStateFlow

@ActivityScoped
class InAppUpdateController @Inject constructor(
    private val appUpdateManager: AppUpdateManager,
) {
    private val _updateDownloaded = MutableStateFlow(false)

    /** True once a flexible update has been downloaded and is waiting for a restart. */
    val updateDownloaded: StateFlow<Boolean> = _updateDownloaded.asStateFlow()

    private val installListener = InstallStateUpdatedListener { state ->
        if (state.installStatus() == InstallStatus.DOWNLOADED) {
            _updateDownloaded.value = true
        }
    }

    /** Called once when the activity is first created. */
    suspend fun checkForUpdate(launcher: ActivityResultLauncher<IntentSenderRequest>) {
        val info = fetchInfo() ?: return
        if (info.updateAvailability() != UpdateAvailability.UPDATE_AVAILABLE) return

        when {
            info.updatePriority >= HIGH_PRIORITY && info.isImmediateUpdateAllowed ->
                startFlow(info, AppUpdateType.IMMEDIATE, launcher)

            (info.clientVersionStalenessDays ?: 0) >= DAYS_FOR_FLEXIBLE_UPDATE &&
                info.isFlexibleUpdateAllowed -> {
                appUpdateManager.registerListener(installListener)
                startFlow(info, AppUpdateType.FLEXIBLE, launcher)
            }
        }
    }

    /** Called from onResume(): resumes interrupted immediate updates, re-surfaces finished downloads. */
    suspend fun onResume(launcher: ActivityResultLauncher<IntentSenderRequest>) {
        val info = fetchInfo() ?: return
        if (info.installStatus() == InstallStatus.DOWNLOADED) {
            _updateDownloaded.value = true
        }
        if (info.updateAvailability() == UpdateAvailability.DEVELOPER_TRIGGERED_UPDATE_IN_PROGRESS) {
            startFlow(info, AppUpdateType.IMMEDIATE, launcher)
        }
    }

    /** Installs a downloaded flexible update; the app process restarts. */
    fun completeUpdate() {
        appUpdateManager.unregisterListener(installListener)
        appUpdateManager.completeUpdate()
    }

    fun dispose() {
        appUpdateManager.unregisterListener(installListener)
    }

    // Fails for installs that did not come from Play (debug builds, sideloads): treat as "no update".
    private suspend fun fetchInfo(): AppUpdateInfo? =
        runCatching { appUpdateManager.requestAppUpdateInfo() }.getOrNull()

    private fun startFlow(
        info: AppUpdateInfo,
        type: Int,
        launcher: ActivityResultLauncher<IntentSenderRequest>,
    ) {
        appUpdateManager.startUpdateFlowForResult(
            info,
            launcher,
            AppUpdateOptions.newBuilder(type).build(),
        )
    }

    private companion object {
        const val HIGH_PRIORITY = 4
        const val DAYS_FOR_FLEXIBLE_UPDATE = 3
    }
}
----

See https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/in-app-updates/kotlin-java[Android Developers -- Support in-app updates (Kotlin or Java)].

`MainActivity` registers the `IntentSender` launcher (it must be registered before the activity is started, so it
is a property), runs the first check once, resumes on every `onResume()`, and shows a "Restart" snackbar in
Compose when a flexible update has finished downloading:

[source,kotlin]
----
// app/src/main/kotlin/com/example/trailmate/MainActivity.kt
package com.example.trailmate

import android.os.Bundle
import android.util.Log
import androidx.activity.ComponentActivity
import androidx.activity.compose.setContent
import androidx.activity.enableEdgeToEdge
import androidx.activity.result.contract.ActivityResultContracts
import androidx.compose.foundation.layout.Box
import androidx.compose.foundation.layout.fillMaxSize
import androidx.compose.foundation.layout.navigationBarsPadding
import androidx.compose.foundation.layout.padding
import androidx.compose.material3.Snackbar
import androidx.compose.material3.Text
import androidx.compose.material3.TextButton
import androidx.compose.runtime.getValue
import androidx.compose.ui.Alignment
import androidx.compose.ui.Modifier
import androidx.compose.ui.res.stringResource
import androidx.compose.ui.unit.dp
import androidx.lifecycle.compose.collectAsStateWithLifecycle
import androidx.lifecycle.lifecycleScope
import com.example.trailmate.play.InAppUpdateController
import com.example.trailmate.ui.TrailMateApp
import com.example.trailmate.ui.theme.TrailMateTheme
import com.google.android.play.core.install.model.ActivityResult as PlayActivityResult
import dagger.hilt.android.AndroidEntryPoint
import javax.inject.Inject
import kotlinx.coroutines.launch

@AndroidEntryPoint
class MainActivity : ComponentActivity() {

    @Inject lateinit var updateController: InAppUpdateController

    private val updateLauncher = registerForActivityResult(
        ActivityResultContracts.StartIntentSenderForResult(),
    ) { result ->
        when (result.resultCode) {
            RESULT_OK -> Unit // Update accepted; flexible downloads continue in the background.
            RESULT_CANCELED -> Log.i(TAG, "User declined the update")
            PlayActivityResult.RESULT_IN_APP_UPDATE_FAILED -> Log.w(TAG, "In-app update failed")
        }
    }

    override fun onCreate(savedInstanceState: Bundle?) {
        super.onCreate(savedInstanceState)
        enableEdgeToEdge()
        if (savedInstanceState == null) {
            lifecycleScope.launch { updateController.checkForUpdate(updateLauncher) }
        }
        setContent {
            TrailMateTheme {
                val updateDownloaded by updateController.updateDownloaded.collectAsStateWithLifecycle()
                Box(Modifier.fillMaxSize()) {
                    TrailMateApp()
                    if (updateDownloaded) {
                        Snackbar(
                            modifier = Modifier
                                .align(Alignment.BottomCenter)
                                .navigationBarsPadding()
                                .padding(16.dp),
                            action = {
                                TextButton(onClick = updateController::completeUpdate) {
                                    Text(stringResource(R.string.update_restart))
                                }
                            },
                        ) {
                            Text(stringResource(R.string.update_downloaded))
                        }
                    }
                }
            }
        }
    }

    override fun onResume() {
        super.onResume()
        lifecycleScope.launch { updateController.onResume(updateLauncher) }
    }

    override fun onDestroy() {
        updateController.dispose()
        super.onDestroy()
    }

    private companion object {
        const val TAG = "TrailMateUpdate"
    }
}
----

See https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/in-app-updates/kotlin-java[Android Developers -- Support in-app updates (Kotlin or Java)].

Common mistakes with in-app updates: calling `checkForUpdate` on every `onResume()` (which re-prompts a user who
just declined), forgetting the `DEVELOPER_TRIGGERED_UPDATE_IN_PROGRESS` check (an immediate update interrupted by
the user switching apps then never resumes), never calling `completeUpdate()` (a flexible update downloads but
only installs when the app is next killed), and expecting any of it to work in a debug build. Testing uses
**internal app sharing** or an internal track with two uploaded versions, or `FakeAppUpdateManager` in
instrumented tests, as described in
https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/in-app-updates/test[Android Developers -- Test in-app updates].

=== In-App Review

**In-app review** (`review` / `review-ktx` 2.0.2) shows Play's own rating-and-review card on top of the app, so a
user can rate TrailMate without leaving it. The flow is deliberately opaque: the API never says whether the card
was shown or whether the user rated, and Play enforces a **quota** on how often the card may appear for a given
user, so a call may complete without any UI. The app's job is only to ask at a good moment and then carry on
exactly as if nothing happened.

[source,kotlin]
----
// app/src/main/kotlin/com/example/trailmate/play/ReviewPrompter.kt
package com.example.trailmate.play

import android.app.Activity
import com.google.android.play.core.ktx.launchReview
import com.google.android.play.core.ktx.requestReview
import com.google.android.play.core.review.ReviewException
import com.google.android.play.core.review.ReviewManager
import javax.inject.Inject

class ReviewPrompter @Inject constructor(
    private val reviewManager: ReviewManager,
) {
    /**
     * Asks Play to show the review card after the user has completed a few hikes.
     * Never shows an error and never changes the app flow: the API gives no feedback by design.
     */
    suspend fun maybeAskForReview(activity: Activity, completedHikes: Int) {
        if (completedHikes < MIN_COMPLETED_HIKES) return

        val reviewInfo = try {
            reviewManager.requestReview()
        } catch (e: ReviewException) {
            return // Play not available, quota exceeded, network error: silently skip.
        }
        // Suspends until the flow finishes (or immediately, if Play decides not to show it).
        reviewManager.launchReview(activity, reviewInfo)
    }

    private companion object {
        const val MIN_COMPLETED_HIKES = 3
    }
}
----

See https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/in-app-review/kotlin-java[Android Developers -- Integrate in-app reviews (Kotlin or Java)].

TrailMate calls `maybeAskForReview` after the summary screen of a finished hike -- a natural pause in which the
user has just got value from the app -- never at launch, never in the middle of recording, and never behind a
custom "Do you like TrailMate?" pre-dialog, which Play's guidelines prohibit because it filters for positive
reviews. The `ReviewInfo` object is only valid for a limited time, so it is requested right before launching the
flow rather than cached at startup. With `FakeReviewManager` injected in debug builds (see `PlayModule` above),
the code path runs end to end without UI; testing the real card requires an internal-track or internal app
sharing install.

=== Play Feature Delivery (Brief)

**Play Feature Delivery** splits optional functionality into **dynamic feature modules**
(`com.android.dynamic-feature` plugin) that Play delivers at install time, conditionally (by country, API level or
device feature), or **on demand** when the app requests them. The benefit is a smaller initial download; the cost
is extra modularization, a `SplitCompat` setup, and error handling for downloads that fail. TrailMate is small
enough not to need it, but a hypothetical `offlinemaps` module holding large map-rendering assets would be
declared on demand in its own manifest:

[source,xml]
----
<!-- offlinemaps/src/main/AndroidManifest.xml -->
<manifest xmlns:android="http://schemas.android.com/apk/res/android"
    xmlns:dist="http://schemas.android.com/apk/distribution">

    <dist:module dist:title="@string/title_offline_maps">
        <dist:delivery>
            <dist:on-demand />
        </dist:delivery>
        <dist:fusing dist:include="false" />
    </dist:module>
</manifest>
----

See https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/feature-delivery/on-demand[Android Developers -- Configure on demand delivery].

and requested from the base module with the `feature-delivery-ktx` suspend API when the user first opens the
offline maps screen:

[source,kotlin]
----
// app/src/main/kotlin/com/example/trailmate/play/OfflineMapsInstaller.kt
package com.example.trailmate.play

import android.content.Context
import com.google.android.play.core.ktx.requestInstall
import com.google.android.play.core.splitinstall.SplitInstallManagerFactory

private const val OFFLINE_MAPS_MODULE = "offlinemaps"

/** Returns true if the module is already installed; otherwise starts the download and returns false. */
suspend fun ensureOfflineMapsInstalled(context: Context): Boolean {
    val manager = SplitInstallManagerFactory.create(context)
    if (OFFLINE_MAPS_MODULE in manager.installedModules) return true
    manager.requestInstall(modules = listOf(OFFLINE_MAPS_MODULE)) // Progress: requestProgressFlow().
    return false
}
----

See https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/feature-delivery[Android Developers -- Overview of Play Feature Delivery].

Dynamic feature modules can be tested locally with `bundletool build-apks --local-testing`, which simulates Play's
on-demand delivery on a connected device. **Play Asset Delivery**, the sibling mechanism for large game assets,
follows the same model.

=== Play Billing (Mention)

**Google Play Billing** is required for selling digital goods and subscriptions inside an app distributed on
Play: in-app items, subscriptions, premium feature unlocks. It involves the Play Billing Library in the app, product
configuration in Play Console (**Monetize with Play -> Products**), and -- essential for anything beyond a toy --
server-side purchase verification and acknowledgement through the Play Developer API, since unacknowledged
purchases are refunded automatically. TrailMate is free and sells nothing, so Billing is out of scope here; the
integration guide is at https://developer.android.com/google/play/billing/integrate[Android Developers --
Integrate the Google Play Billing Library into your app]. Physical goods and services (a guided-hike booking, for
example) are *not* sold through Play Billing.

=== Play Integrity

The **Play Integrity API** lets TrailMate's backend check that a request comes from the genuine, Play-installed
TrailMate binary on a genuine Android device, rather than from a repackaged APK or an automated abuse setup. The
app requests an integrity token and forwards it to the backend, which decrypts and verifies the verdict
server-side. The TrailMate integration, including the Kotlin client code and how it combines with OAuth, is
covered in xref:apps/android/permissions-privacy-and-security.adoc[Permissions, Privacy and Security]; the API
itself is described in https://developer.android.com/google/play/integrity/overview[Android Developers --
Overview of the Play Integrity API]. It requires linking a Google Cloud project to the app in Play Console
(**Test and release -> App integrity**).

== Automating Releases: the Play Developer Publishing API

Everything done in the Releases section above can be automated with the **Google Play Developer API**, whose
**Publishing API** part manages bundles, tracks, rollouts, release notes and store listings (the other part covers
subscriptions and in-app purchases). Setting it up is a one-time job:

. Create or pick a Google Cloud project and **enable the Google Play Android Developer API** in it.
. Create a **service account** in that project and a JSON key for it.
. In Play Console, **Users and permissions -> Invite new users**, invite the service account's email and grant it
  only the app-level permissions it needs (for a release pipeline: release to testing tracks and, if it will
  promote, release to production).
. Store the JSON key as a CI secret -- never in the repository.

The API is transactional through **edits**: create an edit, make any number of changes inside it (upload a
bundle, update a track, change listing text), then **commit** the edit; nothing takes effect until the commit, and
an uncommitted edit is simply discarded. The raw REST sequence for a 5% staged production rollout looks like
this:

[source,bash]
----
# scripts/play-release.sh -- requires ACCESS_TOKEN: an OAuth 2.0 access token for the service account
# with the https://www.googleapis.com/auth/androidpublisher scope.
set -euo pipefail

PKG=com.example.trailmate
API=https://androidpublisher.googleapis.com/androidpublisher/v3/applications/$PKG
UPLOAD_API=https://androidpublisher.googleapis.com/upload/androidpublisher/v3/applications/$PKG
AUTH="Authorization: Bearer $ACCESS_TOKEN"

# 1. Open an edit.
EDIT_ID=$(curl -sf -X POST -H "$AUTH" "$API/edits" | jq -r '.id')

# 2. Upload the signed App Bundle into the edit; the response contains its versionCode.
VERSION_CODE=$(curl -sf -X POST -H "$AUTH" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/octet-stream" \
  --data-binary @app/build/outputs/bundle/release/app-release.aab \
  "$UPLOAD_API/edits/$EDIT_ID/bundles?uploadType=media" | jq -r '.versionCode')

# 3. Assign it to production as a 5% staged rollout with release notes and an update priority.
curl -sf -X PUT -H "$AUTH" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  "$API/edits/$EDIT_ID/tracks/production" \
  -d @- <<JSON
{
  "track": "production",
  "releases": [{
    "name": "1.4.0",
    "versionCodes": ["$VERSION_CODE"],
    "status": "inProgress",
    "userFraction": 0.05,
    "inAppUpdatePriority": 2,
    "releaseNotes": [{ "language": "en-US", "text": "Offline maps now load twice as fast." }]
  }]
}
JSON

# 4. Commit: only now does the release exist and go to review.
curl -sf -X POST -H "$AUTH" "$API/edits/$EDIT_ID:commit"
----

See https://developers.google.com/android-publisher/edits[Google for Developers -- Edits (Google Play Developer API)].

A release's `status` maps directly onto the rollout controls described earlier: `draft` (not served),
`inProgress` with a `userFraction` (a staged rollout), `halted` (stop serving it to new users) and `completed`
(everyone). Increasing a rollout is another edit that updates the same release with a larger `userFraction`;
halting is an edit that sets `status` to `halted`. `inAppUpdatePriority` is the only way to set the priority the
in-app updates API reports -- the value TrailMate's `InAppUpdateController` compares against 4.

Few teams call the REST API by hand. The usual wrappers are the **`r0adkll/upload-google-play`** GitHub Action
and the **Gradle Play Publisher** plugin. The TrailMate production step, with release notes read from
per-locale files in the repository (`distribution/whatsnew/whatsnew-en-US`, `whatsnew-es-ES`, ...):

[source,yaml]
----
# .github/workflows/release-app.yml (excerpt; the full workflow is on the CI/CD page)
      - name: Promote to production (5% staged rollout)
        uses: r0adkll/upload-google-play@v1
        with:
          serviceAccountJsonPlainText: ${{ secrets.PLAY_SERVICE_ACCOUNT_JSON }}
          packageName: com.example.trailmate
          releaseFiles: app/build/outputs/bundle/release/app-release.aab
          track: production
          status: inProgress
          userFraction: 0.05
          inAppUpdatePriority: 2
          whatsNewDirectory: distribution/whatsnew
          mappingFile: app/build/outputs/mapping/release/mapping.txt
----

See https://github.com/r0adkll/upload-google-play[GitHub -- r0adkll/upload-google-play].

The complete workflows -- building and signing in CI from secrets, uploading to the internal track on every merge,
and promoting to production on a tag -- are in xref:apps/android/ci-cd-with-github-actions.adoc[CI/CD with GitHub
Actions]. A good split of responsibilities is: automation uploads and promotes; a human decides when to increase a
staged rollout, after looking at Android vitals.

== Alternative Distribution

Google Play is not the only way to get an Android app onto a device, and Android has always allowed installing
apps from other sources.

**Sideloading** means installing an APK directly -- from a browser download, a file manager, an MDM system, or
`adb install` during development. An App Bundle cannot be installed directly, so a sideloadable artifact is either
an APK built with `assembleRelease` or a universal APK extracted from the bundle with `bundletool`:

[source,bash]
----
# Build a single universal APK from TrailMate's bundle, signed with the release (or upload) key.
bundletool build-apks \
  --bundle=app/build/outputs/bundle/release/app-release.aab \
  --output=build/trailmate-universal.apks \
  --mode=universal \
  --ks=release.keystore --ks-key-alias=trailmate

# The .apks file is a ZIP archive containing universal.apk.
unzip -o build/trailmate-universal.apks universal.apk -d build/

# Install on a connected device (for testing or a manual hand-over).
adb install -r build/universal.apk
----

See https://developer.android.com/tools/bundletool[Android Developers -- bundletool].

On the device, installing from a browser or file manager requires the user to grant that app the **"Install unknown
apps"** permission, and Google Play Protect scans the APK. Two constraints matter for anyone combining channels:

* **Signatures must match for updates.** An APK signed with the upload key cannot update a Play install (signed
  with Google's app signing key), and vice versa -- Android refuses an update whose signing certificate differs.
  Distributing the same app both on Play and elsewhere therefore needs a deliberate choice of which key signs the
  non-Play build, and users cannot move between channels without uninstalling.
* **Developer verification applies.** From September 30, 2026 in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand, and
  globally from 2027, a sideloaded app on a certified device must come from a verified developer with a
  registered package name (or be installed through the power-user advanced flow). Non-Play developers register in
  the Android Developer Console; a limited-distribution account covers the "my own app on up to 20 of my own
  devices" case.

**Other app stores** -- Samsung Galaxy Store, Amazon Appstore, Huawei AppGallery, OEM stores such as those from
Xiaomi, OPPO and HONOR, and **F-Droid** for free and open-source apps -- each have their own developer account,
submission format (most accept APKs; some now accept bundles), review and policies. Play-specific libraries do not
work outside Play: in-app updates, in-app review, Feature Delivery, Play Billing and Play Integrity all depend on
the Play Store, so a multi-store TrailMate would need a product flavor per store that swaps those implementations
(the `play` flavor using the classes on this page, a `foss` flavor with no-op implementations), as described in
xref:apps/android/project-structure-and-gradle.adoc[Project Structure, Gradle and Build Variants]. For enterprise
and internal apps, **managed Google Play** and private apps, or direct MDM distribution, are the usual channels.
Google's summary of the options is at
https://developer.android.com/distribute/marketing-tools/alternative-distribution[Android Developers --
Alternative distribution options].
