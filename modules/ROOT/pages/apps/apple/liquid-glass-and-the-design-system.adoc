The 26 releases gave every Apple platform a new visual language built around **Liquid Glass**, a material that
bends and reflects the content behind it, and the 27 releases refine it. This page explains the design model
behind it (content at the bottom, a *functional layer* of glass controls above it) and then the code: what you
get for free by rebuilding, which custom styling to remove, how to put glass on your own controls in SwiftUI,
UIKit and AppKit, how the accessibility settings change what people see, and how to build the layered app icon
that goes with the new look. Everything is written against **iOS, iPadOS, macOS, watchOS and visionOS 27**,
built with **Xcode 27** and **Swift 6.4**, with *FieldNotes* keeping a **deployment minimum of 26**. Liquid Glass
APIs date from the 26 SDK, so most examples need no availability check; the few things that exist only on the
27 releases, such as the new icon rendering, are called out as "new in 27".

The examples extend *FieldNotes*: the `ContentView` split view, `NoteList`, `NoteDetail` and, above all, the
floating `CaptureButton` that page 1 introduced on xref:apps/apple/getting-started.adoc[Getting Started with Apple
Platforms]. Toolbars, tab bars and sheets themselves are covered on
xref:apps/apple/swiftui-navigation-and-presentation.adoc[Navigation and Presentation]; the broader design
principles are on xref:apps/apple/human-interface-guidelines.adoc[Human Interface Guidelines in Practice].

== What Liquid Glass Is

Liquid Glass is a *dynamic* material. Unlike the blur-based materials of earlier releases, which simply frosted
whatever was behind them, it combines several optical effects that update continuously:

* **Lensing.** It bends and concentrates the light of the content behind it, so the edges of a glass capsule
  refract the list scrolling beneath, instead of just blurring it.
* **Reflection and highlights.** It picks up color and light from surrounding content and responds to device
  motion with specular highlights.
* **Adaptivity.** It switches between a lighter and darker appearance depending on what is behind it, so that
  symbols and text on top of it stay legible, and small elements such as a toolbar button adapt more strongly
  than large ones such as a sidebar.
* **Fluidity.** Interactive glass reacts to touch and pointer input (it gently scales and glows under the
  finger), and nearby glass shapes can merge and split apart, *morphing* into one another as the interface
  changes, for example when a button expands into a menu.

The material comes in two variants. **Regular** glass blurs and adjusts the luminosity of the background to keep
foreground content legible; it is what almost every system component uses. **Clear** glass is more transparent
and is meant only for elements that sit over visually rich content such as photos or video, usually with a
dimming layer behind them when the content is bright. People can also choose a preferred look for Liquid Glass
in their device settings (a clearer or a more tinted appearance), and standard components follow that
preference automatically.

See https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/materials[Human Interface Guidelines -- Materials]
and https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/[WWDC25 -- Meet Liquid Glass].

== The Content Layer and the Functional Layer

The single most important rule of the new design is about *where* glass goes. Apple's interfaces are now
organized in layers:

. The **content layer**: what people came for. In *FieldNotes*, the note list, the text of a note, attached
  photos, sketches and maps. It fills the screen edge to edge and scrolls *under* the controls.
. The **functional layer**: controls and navigation that act on the content, such as toolbars, the tab bar, the
  sidebar, search fields and floating buttons like `CaptureButton`. This is where Liquid Glass lives. It floats
  above the content and lets it peek through, which keeps people oriented without hiding what they are working
  on.
. **Overlays**: transient interfaces on top of both, such as sheets, popovers, menus and alerts. They also use
  glass, provided by the system.

The Human Interface Guidelines put the consequence bluntly: **don't use Liquid Glass in the content layer.** A
glass note card or a glass list row competes with the controls for attention, blurs the distinction between
"things to read" and "things to tap", and makes the hierarchy confusing. For visual separation *inside* the
content layer (a caption over a photo, a grouped panel), use the standard materials instead: SwiftUI's
`Material` (`.ultraThinMaterial`, `.thinMaterial`, `.regularMaterial`, `.thickMaterial`) on iOS and iPadOS, and
the equivalent visual-effect materials in UIKit and AppKit.

`NoteDetail` shows an attached photo with its caption. The caption belongs to the content, so it gets a standard
material, not glass:

[source,swift]
----
struct PhotoAttachmentView: View {
    let image: Image
    let caption: String

    var body: some View {
        image
            .resizable()
            .scaledToFill()
            .frame(height: 240)
            .clipShape(.rect(cornerRadius: 16))
            .overlay(alignment: .bottomLeading) {
                Text(caption)
                    .font(.footnote)
                    .padding(.horizontal, 10).padding(.vertical, 6)
                    // Content layer: a standard material, not Liquid Glass.
                    .background(.thinMaterial, in: .capsule)
                    .padding(10)
            }
    }
}
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/material[Apple Developer Documentation -- Material].

There is one sanctioned exception: a control *inside* the content layer that has a transient interactive
element, such as a slider's knob or a toggle's thumb, turns into glass while you interact with it. Standard
controls do that on their own.

== What You Get Automatically

If an app uses standard components from SwiftUI, UIKit or AppKit, rebuilding it with the current SDK is most of
the adoption work. Bars, sheets, popovers, menus, sidebars, tab bars, search fields and controls pick up Liquid
Glass by themselves, and the system adapts them to overlap and focus. Several layout-level changes come along:

* **Controls** have rounder shapes that are *concentric* with the device's corners, and a new extra-large size.
* **Tab bars** float above the content and can minimize while scrolling (`+tabBarMinimizeBehavior(_:)+`); a tab
  view can adapt into a sidebar on iPad and Mac.
* **Sheets** have a larger corner radius; half-height sheets are inset from the screen edges, and a sheet that
  expands to full height becomes more opaque. Action sheets originate from the control that triggered them.
* **Lists, tables and forms** use more padding and title-style section headers instead of all-caps ones.
* **Toolbars** group their items onto shared glass backgrounds.

In *FieldNotes*, ``ContentView``'s `NavigationSplitView`, the note list, the toolbar and the settings form needed
no code at all to look right.

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/adopting-liquid-glass[Apple Developer Documentation -- Adopting Liquid Glass].

=== Remove Custom Bar Backgrounds

What *does* need attention is custom styling that was added to make bars look a certain way. A custom background
on a navigation bar, a toolbar, a tab bar or a split view column sits on top of, or instead of, the glass and the
scroll edge effect, and it usually looks wrong: an opaque stripe where the content should peek through. Apple's
guidance is to remove those customizations and let the system choose the background. This was the pre-26
*FieldNotes* note list:

[source,swift]
----
// Before: forced an opaque, branded bar background.
NoteList()
    .toolbarBackground(Color("BrandPaper"), for: .navigationBar)
    .toolbarBackground(.visible, for: .navigationBar)
    .toolbarColorScheme(.light, for: .navigationBar)
----

See link:++https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/toolbarbackgroundvisibility(_:for:)++[+Apple Developer Documentation -- toolbarBackgroundVisibility(_:for:)+]
for the modifiers this code used to force a visible bar background.

and this is the current version, which simply deletes the three modifiers. Brand color moves to where it helps
without fighting the glass: the tint of controls, and the content itself.

[source,swift]
----
// After: the system provides Liquid Glass and the scroll edge effect.
NoteList()
    .tint(Color("BrandInk"))
----

See link:++https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/toolbarbackground(_:for:)++[+Apple Developer Documentation -- toolbarBackground(_:for:)+].

The same applies to UIKit code that configures bar appearances with an opaque background, and to popovers and
sheets whose content view adds its own `UIVisualEffectView`: remove them.

[source,swift]
----
// Before (UIKit): an opaque navigation bar appearance, applied app-wide.
let appearance = UINavigationBarAppearance()
appearance.configureWithOpaqueBackground()
appearance.backgroundColor = UIColor(named: "BrandPaper")
UINavigationBar.appearance().standardAppearance = appearance
UINavigationBar.appearance().scrollEdgeAppearance = appearance

// After: delete the appearance customization entirely and keep only what is still meaningful.
UINavigationBar.appearance().tintColor = UIColor(named: "BrandInk")
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uinavigationbarappearance[Apple Developer Documentation -- UINavigationBarAppearance].

=== No More Opting Out: UIDesignRequiresCompatibility

The 26 SDK offered a temporary escape hatch: setting the `UIDesignRequiresCompatibility` Info.plist key to `YES`
made an app built with the new SDK keep its pre-26 appearance while the team redesigned. **That escape hatch is
gone for apps built against the 27 SDKs**: the documentation states that the system ignores the key when you
build for iOS 27, iPadOS 27, Mac Catalyst 27, macOS 27 or tvOS 27 and later. An app still carrying it should
delete the entry and finish its adoption:

[source,xml]
----
<!-- FieldNotes/Info.plist: remove this; it has no effect with the 27 SDK. -->
<key>UIDesignRequiresCompatibility</key>
<true/>
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/bundleresources/information-property-list/uidesignrequirescompatibility[Apple Developer Documentation -- UIDesignRequiresCompatibility].

=== Toolbar Grouping

Because toolbar items now share glass backgrounds, the *grouping* of items is a design decision. Items in the
same `ToolbarItemGroup` share one capsule; `ToolbarSpacer` separates groups; and
`sharedBackgroundVisibility(.hidden)` takes an item (such as an avatar image) out of the shared background. Don't
mix text and icons within one group, and give every icon an accessibility label:

[source,swift]
----
.toolbar {
    ToolbarItemGroup(placement: .primaryAction) {
        Button("Share", systemImage: "square.and.arrow.up") { share(note) }
        Button("Pin", systemImage: note.isPinned ? "pin.slash" : "pin") { store.togglePin(note) }
    }
    ToolbarSpacer(.fixed, placement: .primaryAction)
    ToolbarItem(placement: .primaryAction) {
        Menu("More", systemImage: "ellipsis") { NoteActionsMenu(note: note) }
    }
}
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/toolbarspacer[Apple Developer Documentation -- ToolbarSpacer]
and link:++https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/toolbarcontent/sharedbackgroundvisibility(_:)++[+Apple Developer Documentation -- sharedBackgroundVisibility(_:)+].
Overflow menus, `visibilityPriority` and the other toolbar additions of the 27 releases are on
xref:apps/apple/swiftui-navigation-and-presentation.adoc[Navigation and Presentation].

== Custom Glass in SwiftUI

Custom Liquid Glass is for the few *custom functional elements* an app has; Apple's guidance is to use it
sparingly, on the most important controls, because every extra glass shape competes with the content. In
*FieldNotes* there is exactly one: the floating `CaptureButton` that creates a new note.

=== glassEffect(_:in:) and the Glass Configuration

`+glassEffect(_:in:)+` renders Liquid Glass *behind* a view's content, clipped to a shape. The first parameter is a
`Glass` value and the second any `Shape`; the defaults are `.regular` glass in a capsule. A `Glass` value is
configured with modifiers:

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
| Configuration | Effect

| `.regular`
| The standard variant, for almost everything.

| `.clear`
| The more transparent variant, only over media-rich backgrounds.

| `.identity`
| No glass at all, as if the modifier were not applied. Useful for switching the effect off without changing the
  view's structure (and therefore its identity and animations).

| `+.tint(_:)+`
| Tints the glass to suggest prominence, for example for a primary action.

| `+.interactive(_:)+`
| Makes the glass react to touch and pointer input, with the same scaling and highlight that glass buttons get.
|===

Here is the complete `CaptureButton`. It uses interactive, tinted glass in a circle, and applies `glassEffect`
*after* the other modifiers that affect the view's appearance, because the modifier captures the content as it
is at that point:

[source,swift]
----
// Packages/FieldNotesUI/Sources/FieldNotesUI/CaptureButton.swift
import SwiftUI

public struct CaptureButton: View {
    let action: () -> Void

    public init(action: @escaping () -> Void) {
        self.action = action
    }

    public var body: some View {
        Button("New Note", systemImage: "square.and.pencil", action: action)
            .labelStyle(.iconOnly)
            .font(.title2)
            .foregroundStyle(.white)
            .frame(width: 56, height: 56)
            #if os(visionOS)
            .glassBackgroundEffect(in: .circle)   // glassEffect(_:in:) is not available on visionOS
            #else
            .glassEffect(.regular.tint(.accentColor).interactive(), in: .circle)
            #endif
            .accessibilityHint("Creates a note in the current notebook")
    }
}
----

See link:++https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/glasseffect(_:in:)++[+Apple Developer Documentation -- glassEffect(_:in:)+],
https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/glass[Apple Developer Documentation -- Glass] and
https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/applying-liquid-glass-to-custom-views[Apple Developer Documentation -- Applying Liquid Glass to custom views].

`+glassEffect(_:in:)+` is available on iOS, iPadOS, Mac Catalyst, macOS, tvOS and watchOS 26 and later, **but not
on visionOS**, where windows already sit on the system's own glass material and custom elements use
`glassBackgroundEffect()`. A multiplatform target like *FieldNotes* therefore branches on the platform (the
`#if` mechanics are explained in xref:programming-languages/swift/attributes-and-compiler-control.adoc[Attributes
and Compiler Control]):

[source,swift]
----
extension View {
    /// FieldNotes' floating-control style on every platform the app target supports.
    @ViewBuilder
    func fieldNotesFloatingGlass() -> some View {
        #if os(visionOS)
        self.glassBackgroundEffect(in: .circle)
        #else
        self.glassEffect(.regular.tint(.accentColor).interactive(), in: .circle)
        #endif
    }
}
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/glassbackgroundeffect(in:displaymode:)[Apple Developer Documentation -- glassBackgroundEffect(in:displayMode:)].

=== GlassEffectContainer and Morphing with glassEffectID

Glass shapes that are close to each other should be rendered together. `GlassEffectContainer(spacing:content:)`
does that: every `glassEffect` inside it is rendered in one pass (which Apple recommends for performance), and
shapes that come within the container's `spacing` of each other *blend* into one continuous piece of glass. When
views appear or disappear inside an animation, glass shapes that carry an identity from `+glassEffectID(_:in:)+`
**morph**: the glass flows out of one shape into the other instead of cross-fading, the same idea as
`matchedGeometryEffect` on xref:apps/apple/swiftui-drawing-animation-and-gestures.adoc[Drawing, Animation and
Gestures].

In *FieldNotes* a long press on the capture button expands it into three capture modes, which flow out of the
main button:

[source,swift]
----
enum CaptureMode: String, CaseIterable, Identifiable {
    case text, photo, voice, sketch
    var id: Self { self }
    var symbolName: String {
        switch self {
        case .text: "square.and.pencil"
        case .photo: "camera"
        case .voice: "waveform"
        case .sketch: "scribble.variable"
        }
    }
}

// GlassEffectContainer, glassEffect and glassEffectID are not available on visionOS,
// so the expanding menu is compiled for the other platforms; visionOS keeps the single CaptureButton.
#if !os(visionOS)
struct CaptureMenu: View {
    var capture: (CaptureMode) -> Void
    @State private var isExpanded = false
    @Namespace private var glassNamespace

    var body: some View {
        GlassEffectContainer(spacing: 24) {
            VStack(spacing: 12) {
                if isExpanded {
                    ForEach([CaptureMode.photo, .voice, .sketch]) { mode in
                        Button(mode.rawValue.capitalized, systemImage: mode.symbolName) {
                            capture(mode)
                            withAnimation(.bouncy) { isExpanded = false }
                        }
                        .labelStyle(.iconOnly)
                        .frame(width: 48, height: 48)
                        .glassEffect(.regular.interactive(), in: .circle)
                        .glassEffectID(mode, in: glassNamespace)
                    }
                }

                Button("New Note", systemImage: CaptureMode.text.symbolName) { capture(.text) }
                    .labelStyle(.iconOnly)
                    .font(.title2)
                    .frame(width: 56, height: 56)
                    .glassEffect(.regular.tint(.accentColor).interactive(), in: .circle)
                    .glassEffectID(CaptureMode.text, in: glassNamespace)
                    .onLongPressGesture {
                        withAnimation(.bouncy) { isExpanded.toggle() }
                    }
            }
        }
    }
}
#endif
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/glasseffectcontainer[Apple Developer Documentation -- GlassEffectContainer]
and link:++https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/glasseffectid(_:in:)++[+Apple Developer Documentation -- glassEffectID(_:in:)+].

The spacing matters in two ways. A container spacing *larger* than the stack's spacing makes the shapes blend
together even at rest, because they are "too close"; a smaller one keeps them separate at rest and only lets them
merge as they animate toward each other. Tune it until the resting state looks like distinct buttons and the
transition looks like one drop of glass splitting apart.

=== glassEffectUnion and glassEffectTransition

Two more modifiers complete the toolkit. `glassEffectUnion(id:namespace:)` makes several views contribute to
**one** glass shape even at rest, which is handy when views are created dynamically or are not in the same stack.
*FieldNotes*' formatting bar in `NoteEditor` joins bold, italic and underline into one capsule and the list
buttons into another:

[source,swift]
----
struct FormattingBar: View {
    @Namespace private var bar

    var body: some View {
        GlassEffectContainer(spacing: 8) {
            HStack(spacing: 8) {
                ForEach(["bold", "italic", "underline"], id: \.self) { symbol in
                    Button(symbol.capitalized, systemImage: symbol) { applyStyle(symbol) }
                        .labelStyle(.iconOnly)
                        .padding(10)
                        .glassEffect()
                        .glassEffectUnion(id: "text-style", namespace: bar)
                }
                ForEach(["list.bullet", "checklist"], id: \.self) { symbol in
                    Button(symbol, systemImage: symbol) { applyList(symbol) }
                        .labelStyle(.iconOnly)
                        .padding(10)
                        .glassEffect()
                        .glassEffectUnion(id: "lists", namespace: bar)
                }
            }
        }
    }
}
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/glasseffectunion(id:namespace:)[Apple Developer Documentation -- glassEffectUnion(id:namespace:)].

`+glassEffectTransition(_:)+` picks how a glass shape appears or disappears inside a container. The default,
`.matchedGeometry`, morphs shapes that are within the container's spacing; `.materialize` makes a shape that is
farther away condense out of (or dissolve into) the background, and `.identity` disables the transition. The
formatting bar appears with `.materialize` when the keyboard comes up, because it has no nearby glass to morph
from:

[source,swift]
----
if isEditing {
    FormattingBar()
        .glassEffectTransition(.materialize)
}
----

See link:++https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/glasseffecttransition(_:)++[+Apple Developer Documentation -- glassEffectTransition(_:)+]
and https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/glasseffecttransition[Apple Developer Documentation -- GlassEffectTransition].

=== Concentric Shapes

The new design derives corner radii from the hardware: a sheet's corners are concentric with the display's, and
a button inside the sheet is concentric with the sheet. `ConcentricRectangle` is a shape whose corner radius is
computed from its *container shape* minus the distance to it, and `+containerShape(_:)+` declares that container.
*FieldNotes*' note preview card uses it so that its inner "open" button always nests neatly in the card's corner:

[source,swift]
----
struct NotePreviewCard: View {
    let note: Note
    var open: () -> Void

    var body: some View {
        VStack(alignment: .leading) {
            Text(note.title).font(.headline)
            Text(note.body).lineLimit(4)
            Button("Open", action: open)
                .frame(maxWidth: .infinity)
                .padding(.vertical, 10)
                .background(.tint.opacity(0.15), in: ConcentricRectangle())
        }
        .padding(12)
        .background(.background.secondary, in: .rect(cornerRadius: 24))
        .containerShape(.rect(cornerRadius: 24))
    }
}
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/concentricrectangle[Apple Developer Documentation -- ConcentricRectangle]
and link:++https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/containershape(_:)++[+Apple Developer Documentation -- containerShape(_:)+].

== Glass Button Styles

Most of the time, a glass *button* does not need `glassEffect` at all. The button styles `.glass` and
`.glassProminent` give a `Button` the standard Liquid Glass look, including the interactive response, and adapt
it to context (for example inside a toolbar); `+.glass(_:)+` takes a `Glass` value for the clear variant or a
tint. *FieldNotes* uses them on the onboarding screen and on the empty state of a notebook:

[source,swift]
----
struct EmptyNotebookView: View {
    var createNote: () -> Void
    var importNotes: () -> Void

    var body: some View {
        ContentUnavailableView {
            Label("No Notes Yet", systemImage: "note.text")
        } actions: {
            Button("New Note", action: createNote)
                .buttonStyle(.glassProminent)
            Button("Import…", action: importNotes)
                .buttonStyle(.glass)
        }
    }
}

struct PhotoOverlayControls: View {
    var retake: () -> Void

    var body: some View {
        // Over a full-screen photo: clear glass is appropriate here.
        Button("Retake", systemImage: "arrow.counterclockwise", action: retake)
            .buttonStyle(.glass(.clear))
    }
}
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/primitivebuttonstyle/glass[Apple Developer Documentation -- glass],
https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/primitivebuttonstyle/glassprominent[Apple Developer Documentation -- glassProminent]
and link:++https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/primitivebuttonstyle/glass(_:)++[+Apple Developer Documentation -- glass(_:)+].
The other button styles and controls are on xref:apps/apple/swiftui-controls-forms-text-and-images.adoc[Controls,
Forms, Text, Images and SF Symbols].

== Scroll Edge Effects and Background Extension

Content that scrolls *under* glass needs help to keep the controls on top legible. The **scroll edge effect**
does that: where a scroll view meets a bar, it progressively blurs and fades the content. System bars apply it
automatically. The style can be adjusted per edge with `+scrollEdgeEffectStyle(_:for:)+`: `.soft` (the default on
iOS, a gentle gradient), `.hard` (a nearly opaque, sharp boundary, typical on macOS and useful above pinned
column headers) and `.automatic`. `+scrollEdgeEffectHidden(_:for:)+` removes it, which is rarely the right call.

A **custom bar** should register with the same machinery instead of drawing its own background.
`safeAreaBar(edge:alignment:spacing:content:)` places a view above or below the content like
`safeAreaInset`, and additionally extends the scroll edge effect under it. The note list has a custom tag filter
bar, `TagFilterBar`, at the top (its accessibility is covered on
xref:apps/apple/accessibility.adoc[Accessibility]). `NoteList(notebookID:selection:)` filters by notebook only, so
`NoteListScreen` lists the notes that carry every selected tag itself, reusing `NoteRow` for each row:

[source,swift]
----
struct NoteListScreen: View {
    @Environment(NoteStore.self) private var store
    @State private var selectedTags: Set<String> = []
    @State private var selectedNote: Note.ID?

    private var allTags: [String] {
        Array(Set(store.notes.flatMap(\.tags))).sorted()
    }
    private var filteredNotes: [Note] {
        store.notes.filter { selectedTags.isSubset(of: $0.tags) }
    }

    var body: some View {
        List(filteredNotes, selection: $selectedNote) { note in
            NoteRow(note: note)
        }
        .navigationTitle("Notes")
        .safeAreaBar(edge: .top) {
            TagFilterBar(tags: allTags, selected: $selectedTags, resultCount: filteredNotes.count)
                .padding(.vertical, 6)
        }
        .scrollEdgeEffectStyle(.soft, for: .top)
    }
}
----

See link:++https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/scrolledgeeffectstyle(_:for:)++[+Apple Developer Documentation -- scrollEdgeEffectStyle(_:for:)+],
link:++https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/scrolledgeeffecthidden(_:for:)++[+Apple Developer Documentation -- scrollEdgeEffectHidden(_:for:)+]
and https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/safeareabar(edge:alignment:spacing:content:)-9hwgb[Apple Developer Documentation -- safeAreaBar(edge:alignment:spacing:content:)].

The **background extension effect** solves a related problem for sidebars and inspectors. In a split view, the
sidebar floats on glass above the detail column; a hero image at the top of the detail would normally stop at the
sidebar's edge, leaving an empty strip behind the glass. `backgroundExtensionEffect()` mirrors and blurs the view
into the available safe area around it, so the image *appears* to continue under the sidebar without its actual
content being hidden there. `NoteDetail` uses it for the note's cover photo:

[source,swift]
----
struct NoteDetail: View {
    let note: Note
    let coverImage: Image?

    var body: some View {
        ScrollView {
            if let coverImage {
                coverImage
                    .resizable()
                    .scaledToFill()
                    .frame(height: 280)
                    .clipped()
                    .backgroundExtensionEffect()
            }
            NoteBodyView(note: note)
                .padding()
        }
        .ignoresSafeArea(edges: .top)
    }
}
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/backgroundextensioneffect()[Apple Developer Documentation -- backgroundExtensionEffect()].

Apple's *Landmarks* sample is a complete app built around these two effects, with a hero image extending under
the sidebar and custom glass badges; it is worth reading side by side with this section.

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/landmarks-building-an-app-with-liquid-glass[Apple Developer Documentation -- Landmarks: Building an app with Liquid Glass]
and https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/323/[WWDC25 -- Build a SwiftUI app with the new design].

== Liquid Glass in UIKit and AppKit

The same concepts exist in the imperative frameworks, with the same names wherever possible, so a screen that is
still UIKit or AppKit can match the SwiftUI screens around it (mixing the frameworks is covered on
xref:apps/apple/uikit-and-appkit-interop.adoc[UIKit and AppKit Essentials, and Mixing Them with SwiftUI]).

=== UIKit

UIKit expresses glass as a new *visual effect*: `UIGlassEffect`, set on a `UIVisualEffectView` just like the
older blur effects. Its `style` is `.regular` or `.clear`, and it has `isInteractive` and `tintColor`.
`UIGlassContainerEffect` plays the role of `GlassEffectContainer` for several glass views, with a `spacing` that
controls merging. Buttons get the look through `UIButton.Configuration.glass()`, `.prominentGlass()` and
`.clearGlass()`. `UIScrollView` exposes its scroll edge effects per edge (`topEdgeEffect`, `bottomEdgeEffect`,
and so on) as `UIScrollEdgeEffect` objects with a `style`, and `UIBackgroundExtensionView` is the counterpart of
`backgroundExtensionEffect()`. The UIKit map screen of *FieldNotes* places its capture button like this:

[source,swift]
----
import UIKit

final class NoteMapViewController: UIViewController {
    private let scrollView = UIScrollView()

    override func viewDidLoad() {
        super.viewDidLoad()

        // A floating glass capture button.
        let glass = UIGlassEffect(style: .regular)
        glass.isInteractive = true
        glass.tintColor = .tintColor
        let glassView = UIVisualEffectView(effect: glass)
        glassView.frame = CGRect(x: 0, y: 0, width: 56, height: 56)
        glassView.layer.cornerRadius = 28   // a circle, like the SwiftUI CaptureButton

        var configuration = UIButton.Configuration.plain()
        configuration.image = UIImage(systemName: "square.and.pencil")
        let captureButton = UIButton(configuration: configuration,
                                     primaryAction: UIAction { [weak self] _ in self?.createNote() })
        captureButton.accessibilityLabel = "New Note"
        glassView.contentView.addSubview(captureButton)
        view.addSubview(glassView)

        // A standard glass button needs no effect view at all.
        let filterButton = UIButton(configuration: .glass())
        filterButton.configuration?.image = UIImage(systemName: "line.3.horizontal.decrease")
        view.addSubview(filterButton)

        // A sharper edge effect under a custom header pinned to the top of the scroll view.
        scrollView.topEdgeEffect.style = .hard
    }

    private func createNote() { /* ... */ }
}
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uiglasseffect[Apple Developer Documentation -- UIGlassEffect],
https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uiglasscontainereffect[Apple Developer Documentation -- UIGlassContainerEffect],
https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uibutton/configuration-swift.struct/glass()[Apple Developer Documentation -- UIButton.Configuration.glass()],
https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uiscrolledgeeffect[Apple Developer Documentation -- UIScrollEdgeEffect]
and https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uibackgroundextensionview[Apple Developer Documentation -- UIBackgroundExtensionView].

As in SwiftUI, animate a glass view *in* by setting its effect inside an animation block (starting from an effect
view with no effect), which lets UIKit materialize the glass instead of fading a finished view; and animate it out
by setting `effect` back to `nil`.

=== AppKit

On the Mac, glass is a view rather than an effect. `NSGlassEffectView` embeds its `contentView` in glass, with
`cornerRadius`, `tintColor` and a `style` of `.regular` or `.clear`. `NSGlassEffectContainerView` merges nearby
glass views efficiently, `NSButton` gets a `.glass` bezel style, and `NSBackgroundExtensionView` extends content
under sidebars and inspectors. The AppKit inspector panel of the Mac app shows a floating glass toolbar:

[source,swift]
----
import AppKit

final class SketchInspectorController: NSViewController {
    override func loadView() {
        let root = NSView()

        let penButton = NSButton(image: NSImage(systemSymbolName: "pencil.tip",
                                                accessibilityDescription: "Pen")!,
                                 target: self, action: #selector(selectPen))
        penButton.bezelStyle = .glass

        let eraserButton = NSButton(image: NSImage(systemSymbolName: "eraser",
                                                   accessibilityDescription: "Eraser")!,
                                    target: self, action: #selector(selectEraser))
        eraserButton.bezelStyle = .glass

        let stack = NSStackView(views: [penButton, eraserButton])

        let glass = NSGlassEffectView()
        glass.contentView = stack
        glass.cornerRadius = 18

        let container = NSGlassEffectContainerView()
        container.contentView = glass
        container.spacing = 12

        root.addSubview(container)
        view = root
    }

    @objc private func selectPen() { /* ... */ }
    @objc private func selectEraser() { /* ... */ }
}
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/appkit/nsglasseffectview[Apple Developer Documentation -- NSGlassEffectView],
https://developer.apple.com/documentation/appkit/nsglasseffectcontainerview[Apple Developer Documentation -- NSGlassEffectContainerView],
https://developer.apple.com/documentation/appkit/nsbutton/bezelstyle-swift.enum/glass[Apple Developer Documentation -- NSButton.BezelStyle.glass]
and https://developer.apple.com/documentation/appkit/nsbackgroundextensionview[Apple Developer Documentation -- NSBackgroundExtensionView].

== How Accessibility Settings Change the Glass

Translucency and fluid motion are what make Liquid Glass feel alive, and they are also what some people need to
turn down. The system handles this for standard components and for every `glassEffect`, `UIGlassEffect` and
`NSGlassEffectView`: you do not write code to make the glass itself accessible, but you do need to **test**
with the settings on, and to adapt anything *custom* that you draw next to the glass.

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
| Setting | What happens to Liquid Glass

| Reduce Transparency
| The glass becomes frostier and more opaque, obscuring much more of the content behind it. Clear glass gets
  the same treatment. Anything you made translucent yourself (a custom `Material` background, an `opacity`
  overlay) does not change unless you check the setting.

| Increase Contrast
| Glass elements become predominantly black or white with a contrasting border, so their edges stay visible
  against any content.

| Reduce Motion
| The intensity of some effects decreases and the elastic, "liquid" behavior (the stretching and wobbling of
  interactive glass, exaggerated morphs) is turned off.

| Preferred Liquid Glass look
| People can choose a clearer or a more tinted look for the material in their settings; standard and custom glass
  both follow it.
|===

These behaviors are the ones Apple describes in the *Meet Liquid Glass* session and the *Adopting Liquid Glass*
overview. In your own code, read the corresponding environment values for what you draw yourself.
``CaptureMenu``'s morph animation is purely decorative, so with Reduce Motion it switches to a simple cross-fade,
and the custom photo caption becomes opaque with Reduce Transparency:

[source,swift]
----
struct CaptureMenuAccessibilityAdapted: View {
    @Environment(\.accessibilityReduceMotion) private var reduceMotion
    @Environment(\.accessibilityReduceTransparency) private var reduceTransparency
    @Environment(\.colorSchemeContrast) private var contrast
    @State private var isExpanded = false

    var expandAnimation: Animation {
        reduceMotion ? .easeInOut(duration: 0.15) : .bouncy
    }

    var body: some View {
        VStack {
            Text("Trail notes")
                .padding(8)
                .background(reduceTransparency ? AnyShapeStyle(.background)
                                               : AnyShapeStyle(.thinMaterial),
                            in: .capsule)
                .overlay {
                    if contrast == .increased { Capsule().stroke(.primary, lineWidth: 1) }
                }
            Button("Capture modes", systemImage: "plus") {
                withAnimation(expandAnimation) { isExpanded.toggle() }
            }
            .buttonStyle(.glass)
        }
    }
}
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/environmentvalues/accessibilityreducetransparency[Apple Developer Documentation -- accessibilityReduceTransparency],
https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/environmentvalues/accessibilityreducemotion[Apple Developer Documentation -- accessibilityReduceMotion]
and https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/environmentvalues/colorschemecontrast[Apple Developer Documentation -- colorSchemeContrast].

UIKit and AppKit expose the same settings as `UIAccessibility.isReduceTransparencyEnabled` (with a matching
change notification) and `NSWorkspace.shared.accessibilityDisplayShouldReduceTransparency`:

[source,swift]
----
#if canImport(UIKit)
let captionAlpha: CGFloat = UIAccessibility.isReduceTransparencyEnabled ? 1.0 : 0.75
#elseif canImport(AppKit)
let captionAlpha: CGFloat = NSWorkspace.shared.accessibilityDisplayShouldReduceTransparency ? 1.0 : 0.75
#endif
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uiaccessibility/isreducetransparencyenabled[Apple Developer Documentation -- isReduceTransparencyEnabled]
and https://developer.apple.com/documentation/appkit/nsworkspace/accessibilitydisplayshouldreducetransparency[Apple Developer Documentation -- accessibilityDisplayShouldReduceTransparency].

The simplest way to test is the Accessibility Inspector's settings panel and the Simulator's accessibility
overrides in Xcode, which toggle these settings on a running app without leaving Xcode; both are described on
xref:apps/apple/accessibility.adoc[Accessibility], together with contrast requirements for text placed on glass.

== App Icons with Icon Composer

The new design extends to app icons. Icons are now **layered**: instead of a single flattened image, an icon is a
stack of up to four groups of layers (background to foreground), and the system renders Liquid Glass effects on
them (specular highlights, blur, translucency, shadow and, on the 27 releases, refraction), responding to
lighting and motion. On iOS, iPadOS and macOS each icon has **appearance variants**: *default* (light), *dark*,
*clear* and *tinted*, which people choose for their Home Screen. The system applies the mask: a rounded
rectangle on iPhone, iPad and Mac, and a circle on Apple Watch.

The tool for building them is **Icon Composer**, which ships with Xcode (**Xcode > Open Developer Tool > Icon
Composer**) and is also available from Apple Design Resources. It produces one file that represents the icon on
iOS, iPadOS, macOS and watchOS, and on the App Store.

=== The Workflow

. **Design in layers** in any vector editor, starting from Apple's icon template: a 1024 x 1024 canvas for iPhone,
  iPad and Mac, and 1088 x 1088 for Apple Watch. Use simple, solid, overlapping shapes, convert text to outlines,
  and name layers with increasing numbers from back to front.
. **Leave effects out**: no blurs, shadows, specular highlights, background colors or gradients, and no mask.
  Icon Composer adds all of these in a way that adapts to each appearance.
. **Export each layer** as SVG (or PNG for features SVG cannot express) and drag them, or folders of them, into
  Icon Composer; folders become groups.
. **Organize into at most four groups**, set the background (solid or gradient), and adjust per group the
  Liquid Glass properties: *Individual* or *Combined* mode, specular, blur, translucency, shadow and refraction.
. **Customize variants** per platform and per appearance (dark, mono for clear and tinted) where the defaults
  do not work, and preview them over your own backgrounds.
. **Add the file to the Xcode project** and set the target's *App Icon* field to its name.

*FieldNotes*' artwork is exported like this before it is imported:

[source,text]
----
FieldNotesIcon-layers/
  Background/               -> group 1 (Icon Composer adds the gradient fill)
  Page/
    1-page.svg              -> group 2: the folded notebook page
  Mark/
    2-contour-lines.svg     -> group 3: map contour lines, semi-transparent
    3-pencil.svg            -> group 4: the pencil, foreground
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode/creating-your-app-icon-using-icon-composer[Apple Developer Documentation -- Creating your app icon using Icon Composer]
and https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons[Human Interface Guidelines -- App icons].

In the project, the Icon Composer file replaces the `AppIcon` set of the asset catalog, and the target's *App
Icon* setting must match its name without the extension. That setting is the `+ASSETCATALOG_COMPILER_APPICON_NAME+`
build setting, which you can check from the command line (build settings and configuration files are covered on
xref:apps/apple/signing-capabilities-and-configuration.adoc[Membership, Signing, Capabilities and Info.plist]):

[source,shell]
----
xcodebuild -project FieldNotes.xcodeproj -target FieldNotes -showBuildSettings \
  | grep ASSETCATALOG_COMPILER_APPICON_NAME
#     ASSETCATALOG_COMPILER_APPICON_NAME = FieldNotesIcon
----

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode/build-settings-reference[Apple Developer Documentation -- Build settings reference].

Two platform details affect *FieldNotes*. First, because its deployment minimum is 26, Xcode also generates flat
icon images at build time for any older releases the target supports, from the same file, so there is no
separate legacy icon to maintain. Second, **visionOS (and tvOS) targets still use an asset catalog** app icon,
so the visionOS build of *FieldNotes* keeps its layered visionOS image stack in the asset catalog alongside the
Icon Composer file used by the other platforms.

=== Refraction and the 27 Icon Rendering (New in 27)

The icons of the 27 releases are rendered more sharply and with more depth. **Icon Composer 2.0**, included with
Xcode 27, adds a new rendering mode for them with **refraction** (layers pick up and bend the color and shape of
what is behind them), an **outside specular** option and deeper shadows. The group inspector exposes the new
properties: turn *Refraction* on and drag its strength in the 2D control, and choose whether specular highlights
sit *Inside*, *Outside* or *Automatic*.

Because *FieldNotes* also runs on the 26 releases, one icon file must look right in **both design
generations**. Icon Composer's toolbar lets you switch the preview between them (for example macOS 26 and
macOS 27 rendering), and toggling *Effects* off shows the flat artwork. On versions earlier than 27, refraction
settings have no visible effect and inside/outside specular settings just turn highlights on, so do not rely on
refraction to carry the design. Once the icon looks good in both generations, the same file serves every OS
version the app supports.

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode-release-notes/xcode-27-release-notes[Apple Developer Documentation -- Xcode 27 Release Notes].

== Adoption Checklist

Adopting the design in an existing app is an audit more than a rewrite. For *FieldNotes*, and for any app
moving to the 27 SDK:

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
| Area | Check

| Build
| Build with Xcode 27 and run on the 27 releases. Remove `UIDesignRequiresCompatibility`, which is ignored.

| Bars and sheets
| Remove custom backgrounds from navigation bars, toolbars, tab bars, split view columns, sheets and popovers.

| Layers
| Glass only on functional elements; standard materials for separation inside content.

| Custom glass
| Few elements, grouped in a `GlassEffectContainer`, with `glassEffectID` for morphs. Prefer `.glass` button
  styles over hand-built glass buttons.

| Custom bars
| Register them with `safeAreaBar` so the scroll edge effect covers them; use `backgroundExtensionEffect()` for
  hero images next to sidebars and inspectors.

| Shapes and color
| Use concentric shapes and standard spacing; use color sparingly in controls, with light, dark and
  increased-contrast variants.

| Accessibility
| Test with Reduce Transparency, Increase Contrast and Reduce Motion, and with both preferred glass looks.

| Performance
| Limit how many glass effects are on screen at once and profile with
  Instruments (xref:apps/apple/performance-and-instruments.adoc[Performance and Instruments]).

| App icon
| One Icon Composer file with layered artwork, checked in both the 26 and the 27 design generations.
|===

See https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/adopting-liquid-glass[Apple Developer Documentation -- Adopting Liquid Glass].
