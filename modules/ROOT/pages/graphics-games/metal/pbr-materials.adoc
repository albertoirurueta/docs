include::partial$ai-disclaimer.adoc[]

Physically based rendering (PBR) is not a different kind of lighting, it is a different way of describing a surface. Instead of an ambient, a diffuse and a specular colour tuned by eye
as in xref:graphics-games/metal/lighting-fundamentals.adoc[Lighting Fundamentals], a PBR material has a base colour, a metallic value and a roughness value. The shader derives how much light the surface
reflects and how it spreads from those three numbers using a model that conserves energy, so materials keep a believable look when the lighting changes. This page explains the model, shows the
Cook-Torrance BRDF in MSL, covers the texture maps that carry the parameters per pixel, normal mapping, and gives an overview of skeletal skinning, the other half of the character pipeline that Metal by Tutorials teaches next to materials.

The facts were checked in November 2026. The code is Swift 6 and MSL 4.0 (MSL 4.1 belongs to the 27 operating systems and nothing here needs it), written against the 26 SDKs with the Metal 3 encoder as the main path; the
Metal 4 binding differences are the ones shown on the lighting page and in a short section below. Metal 4 needs an A14 Bionic or later or a Mac with Apple silicon; the maths and the Metal 3 path run on every Metal-capable device.
The model is the metallic-roughness model of glTF 2.0, the same one stored in xref:attachment$bookshelf.glb[bookshelf.glb] and in its PNG textures
(xref:attachment$bookshelf-textures/bookshelf-wood.png[wood], xref:attachment$bookshelf-textures/bookshelf-cover.png[cover], xref:attachment$bookshelf-textures/bookshelf-spine.png[spine] and
xref:attachment$bookshelf-textures/bookshelf-pages.png[pages]), so parameters transfer directly between glTF, Model I/O assets and this shader.

This is step 5 of the Metal Bookshelf running example, after the shadowed shelf of xref:graphics-games/metal/shadows.adoc[Shadows]: the varnished wood of the shelf, the paper of the pages and the cloth of the covers get
distinct materials, and the desk lamp gets a brass base. The concept page is xref:graphics-games/lighting-and-shading-models.adoc[Lighting and Shading Models]; the same material in other libraries is in
xref:graphics-games/opengl/pbr-basics.adoc[PBR Basics in OpenGL], xref:graphics-games/opengl/materials-and-normal-mapping.adoc[Materials and Normal Mapping in OpenGL] and
xref:graphics-games/webgl/threejs-materials-and-pbr.adoc[three.js Materials and PBR]. Light that arrives from the whole environment instead of from a few lamps is
xref:graphics-games/metal/image-based-lighting-and-hdr.adoc[Image-Based Lighting and HDR].

== What PBR changes

An empirical model such as Blinn-Phong has free parameters (ambient, diffuse, specular, shininess) that are not independent: a surface can be given a bright diffuse colour and a bright specular colour at once and reflect more light than it receives. PBR removes that
freedom with four ideas.

* *Energy conservation.* A surface cannot reflect more light than it receives. The light that is reflected specularly is not available for diffuse reflection, so the diffuse term is scaled by one minus the specular reflectance.
* *Two kinds of material.* *Dielectrics* (wood, paper, plastic, cloth) reflect a small, colourless fraction, about 4 percent at normal incidence, and scatter the rest diffusely with the base colour. *Metals* reflect almost all of the light specularly, tinted by the base colour, and have no diffuse
  term. The *metallic* parameter blends between the two, and in practice is either 0 or 1 with fractional values only at texture boundaries such as rust or paint edges.
* *Roughness.* Every surface is made of microscopic facets. Roughness is how much they scatter around the average normal, from a mirror (0) to chalk (1). It controls the width of the specular lobe, as the figure shows.
* *Fresnel.* Reflectance grows as the viewing angle approaches the surface plane, for every material: even matte paper becomes mirror-like at a grazing angle.

.BRDF lobes: the specular lobe widens and dims as roughness grows, the diffuse lobe stays a hemisphere
image::graphics-games-metal-pbr-materials-brdf-lobes.svg["Three panels showing a light ray hitting a surface for roughness 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9 with a narrow, medium and wide specular lobe next to a diffuse hemisphere",width=720]

The four numbers of a material in the glTF model are `baseColor` (an sRGB colour, or the colour of a metal), `metallic`, `roughness` and an optional `occlusion` that darkens crevices where ambient light does not reach. A fifth map, the normal map,
adds detail to the surface orientation without adding triangles.

== The Cook-Torrance BRDF

A BRDF (bidirectional reflectance distribution function) gives, for a light direction L and a view direction V, the fraction of light reflected. The Cook-Torrance specular BRDF models the surface as microfacets and multiplies three terms:

\[f_{ spec } = \frac{D(\mathbf\{H})\,F(\mathbf\{V},\mathbf\{H})\,G(\mathbf\{L},\mathbf\{V})}{4\,(\mathbf\{N}\cdot\mathbf\{L})(\mathbf\{N}\cdot\mathbf\{V})}\]

.The three Cook-Torrance terms and how diffuse and specular combine
image::graphics-games-metal-pbr-materials-cook-torrance-terms.svg["Flow from the GGX distribution D, Fresnel-Schlick F and Smith geometry G through the specular formula and the diffuse term into the final radiance",width=720]

|===
|Term |Question it answers |Common choice |Formula

|D, normal distribution
|How many microfacets are oriented along H?
|GGX (Trowbridge-Reitz), with \(\alpha = \text{ roughness }^2\)
|\(D = \dfrac{\alpha^2}{\pi\left((\mathbf\{N}\cdot\mathbf\{H})^2(\alpha^2 - 1) + 1\right)^2}\)

|F, Fresnel
|How much light is reflected at this angle?
|Schlick's approximation
|\(F = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \mathbf\{V}\cdot\mathbf\{H})^5\)

|G, geometry
|How much do facets shadow and mask each other?
|Smith with Schlick-GGX
|\(G = G_1(\mathbf\{L})\,G_1(\mathbf\{V}),\ G_1(x) = \dfrac{\mathbf\{N}\cdot x}{(\mathbf\{N}\cdot x)(1 - k) + k}\)
|===

The base reflectance \(F_0\) is the Fresnel value at normal incidence: a fixed 0.04 for dielectrics, and the base colour for metals, so \(F_0 = \operatorname\{mix}(0.04, \rho, \text\{metallic})\). The remaining light is diffuse, scaled to conserve energy:

\[\mathbf\{c}_{ diffuse } = (1 - F)\,(1 - \text\{metallic})\,\frac{\rho}{\pi}\]

and the final radiance of one light is \((f_{ diffuse } + f_{ spec })\,\mathbf\{c}_l\,\max(\mathbf\{N}\cdot\mathbf\{L},0)\). Here the division by \(\pi\) is part of the model, so light intensities are physical-style (a lamp of 3 to 30 units, not 1) and the sum of a scene's lights should be tone mapped.
For \(k\), direct lighting uses \((r + 1)^2/8\) and image-based lighting uses \(r^2/2\), with \(r\) the roughness; using the wrong one darkens specular noticeably. The clamp of \(\alpha\) to a small minimum (0.002 or so) prevents a division by zero for a mirror
and a one-texel-wide highlight that flickers.

=== The shader

The structures extend the `ShaderTypes.h` of the lighting page; add two buffer indices, four texture indices and a material structure.

[source,c]
----
// Additions to ShaderTypes.h
typedef NS_ENUM(EnumBackingType, TextureIndex) {
    TextureIndexBaseColor  = 0,
    TextureIndexNormal     = 1,
    TextureIndexMetalRough = 2,     // glTF packing: G = roughness, B = metallic
    TextureIndexOcclusion  = 3
};

typedef struct {
    vector_float3 baseColorFactor;      // linear, multiplied with the texture
    float         metallicFactor;
    float         roughnessFactor;
    float         occlusionStrength;
    float         normalScale;
} PBRMaterial;
----

[source,cpp]
----
// PBR.metal
#include <metal_stdlib>
#include "ShaderTypes.h"
using namespace metal;

constant bool hasNormalMap [[function_constant(0)]];
constant bool useNormalMap = is_function_constant_defined(hasNormalMap) ? hasNormalMap : false;

struct PBRVertexIn {
    float3 position [[attribute(0)]];
    float3 normal   [[attribute(1)]];
    float2 uv       [[attribute(2)]];
    float3 tangent  [[attribute(3)]];
};

struct PBRVertexOut {
    float4 position [[position]];
    float3 worldPosition;
    float3 worldNormal;
    float3 worldTangent;
    float2 uv;
};

vertex PBRVertexOut vertex_pbr(PBRVertexIn in [[stage_in]],
                               constant FrameUniforms &frame [[buffer(BufferIndexFrame)]],
                               constant ModelUniforms &model [[buffer(BufferIndexModel)]])
{
    float4 world = model.model * float4(in.position, 1.0);
    PBRVertexOut out;
    out.position      = frame.viewProjection * world;
    out.worldPosition = world.xyz;
    out.worldNormal   = model.normalMatrix * in.normal;
    out.worldTangent  = model.normalMatrix * in.tangent;
    out.uv            = in.uv;
    return out;
}

// --- BRDF terms -------------------------------------------------------------
static float D_GGX(float NdotH, float alpha)
{
    float a2 = alpha * alpha;
    float d  = NdotH * NdotH * (a2 - 1.0) + 1.0;
    return a2 / (M_PI_F * d * d);
}

static float G1_SchlickGGX(float NdotX, float k)
{
    return NdotX / (NdotX * (1.0 - k) + k);
}

static float3 F_Schlick(float3 F0, float VdotH)
{
    return F0 + (1.0 - F0) * pow(1.0 - VdotH, 5.0);
}

fragment float4 fragment_pbr(PBRVertexOut in [[stage_in]],
                             constant FrameUniforms &frame  [[buffer(BufferIndexFrame)]],
                             constant PBRMaterial &mat       [[buffer(BufferIndexMaterial)]],
                             constant Light *lights          [[buffer(BufferIndexLights)]],
                             constant uint &lightCount       [[buffer(BufferIndexLightCount)]],
                             texture2d<float> baseColorMap   [[texture(TextureIndexBaseColor)]],
                             texture2d<float> normalMap      [[texture(TextureIndexNormal), function_constant(useNormalMap)]],
                             texture2d<float> metalRoughMap  [[texture(TextureIndexMetalRough)]],
                             texture2d<float> occlusionMap   [[texture(TextureIndexOcclusion)]])
{
    constexpr sampler s(filter::linear, mip_filter::linear, address::repeat, max_anisotropy(4));

    // Material parameters (colour texture is sRGB, so the sample is already linear).
    float3 albedo   = mat.baseColorFactor * baseColorMap.sample(s, in.uv).rgb;
    float3 mr       = metalRoughMap.sample(s, in.uv).rgb;
    float  roughness = clamp(mat.roughnessFactor * mr.g, 0.045, 1.0);
    float  metallic  = saturate(mat.metallicFactor * mr.b);
    float  ao        = mix(1.0, occlusionMap.sample(s, in.uv).r, mat.occlusionStrength);

    // Shading normal.
    float3 N = normalize(in.worldNormal);
    if (useNormalMap) {
        float3 T = normalize(in.worldTangent - dot(in.worldTangent, N) * N);   // Gram-Schmidt
        float3 B = cross(N, T);
        float3 tn = normalMap.sample(s, in.uv).xyz * 2.0 - 1.0;
        tn.xy *= mat.normalScale;
        N = normalize(float3x3(T, B, N) * tn);
    }
    float3 V = normalize(frame.cameraPosition - in.worldPosition);
    float NdotV = max(dot(N, V), 1e-4);

    float  alpha = roughness * roughness;
    float  k     = (roughness + 1.0) * (roughness + 1.0) / 8.0;        // direct lighting
    float3 F0    = mix(float3(0.04), albedo, metallic);

    float3 color = frame.ambientColor * albedo * ao;                    // constant ambient until IBL
    for (uint i = 0; i < lightCount; ++i) {
        Light light = lights[i];
        float3 L = normalize(light.type == LightTypeDirectional ? -light.position : light.position - in.worldPosition);
        float  att = 1.0;
        if (light.type == LightTypePoint) {
            float d = length(light.position - in.worldPosition);
            float x = saturate(1.0 - pow(d / light.radius, 4.0));
            att = x * x / (d * d + 0.01);
        }
        float3 H = normalize(L + V);
        float NdotL = saturate(dot(N, L));
        if (NdotL <= 0.0) { continue; }
        float NdotH = saturate(dot(N, H));
        float VdotH = saturate(dot(V, H));

        float  D = D_GGX(NdotH, alpha);
        float  G = G1_SchlickGGX(NdotL, k) * G1_SchlickGGX(NdotV, k);
        float3 F = F_Schlick(F0, VdotH);

        float3 specular = (D * G) * F / max(4.0 * NdotL * NdotV, 1e-4);
        float3 diffuse  = (1.0 - F) * (1.0 - metallic) * albedo / M_PI_F;
        color += (diffuse + specular) * light.color * light.intensity * att * NdotL;
    }
    return float4(color, 1.0);
}
----

Source: https://developer.apple.com/metal/Metal-Shading-Language-Specification.pdf[Metal Shading Language Specification]

Notes on the shader. `M_PI_F` is MSL's single-precision constant. The `function_constant(useNormalMap)` on the `normalMap` argument means the texture parameter does not exist in the pipeline when the constant is false, so a material without a normal map does not need a dummy texture
(function constants are explained in xref:graphics-games/metal/msl-shading-language.adoc[The Metal Shading Language]). The roughness is clamped to 0.045 so the highlight of a mirror keeps a visible size; real-time engines commonly use a value between
0.03 and 0.1. The `continue` for back-facing lights skips the whole BRDF for that light, which is cheaper than multiplying zero.

== Material maps

A uniform material (one colour, one metallic and one roughness for the whole mesh) is the `PBRMaterial` factors alone. Detail comes from textures that supply the same parameters per pixel. The map set, as the figure shows, has four members, and the colour space of each is not negotiable.

.The material maps, their colour spaces and how the fragment function uses them
image::graphics-games-metal-pbr-materials-texture-maps.svg["Four texture boxes (base colour, normal map, metallic and roughness, occlusion) feeding the fragment function together with vertex tangent data",width=720]

|===
|Map |Stores |Colour space |Notes

|Base colour (albedo)
|Diffuse colour of dielectrics, tint of metals
|sRGB
|Load with the sRGB option or an `+_sRGB+` pixel format. Do not bake lighting or shadows into it.

|Normal
|Tangent-space normal remapped to 0..1
|Linear
|Green channel up (OpenGL, glTF) or down (DirectX); invert when the source uses the other convention. Do not compress with a format that distorts blue; ASTC with a normal-map profile or BC5 with the third component rebuilt are the usual choices.

|Metallic-roughness
|G = roughness, B = metallic (glTF packing)
|Linear
|Other tools use separate maps or put metallic in R; remap in the importer, not per pixel.

|Occlusion
|R = ambient visibility
|Linear
|Often stored in the R channel of the same texture as roughness and metallic, which saves one sample.
|===

Loading is where the colour-space rule is enforced. `MTKTextureLoader` applies the sRGB conversion according to the `SRGB` option:

[source,swift]
----
import MetalKit

struct PBRTextureSet {
    let baseColor: MTLTexture
    let normal: MTLTexture?
    let metalRough: MTLTexture
    let occlusion: MTLTexture

    init(device: MTLDevice, baseName: String, hasNormal: Bool) throws {
        let loader = MTKTextureLoader(device: device)

        func load(_ name: String, srgb: Bool) throws -> MTLTexture {
            let options: [MTKTextureLoader.Option: Any] = [
                .SRGB: srgb,                                              // false for data textures
                .generateMipmaps: true,
                .textureUsage: MTLTextureUsage.shaderRead.rawValue,
                .textureStorageMode: MTLStorageMode.private.rawValue
            ]
            return try loader.newTexture(name: name, scaleFactor: 1, bundle: nil, options: options)
        }

        baseColor  = try load("\(baseName)-basecolor", srgb: true)
        if hasNormal {
            normal = try load("\(baseName)-normal", srgb: false)
        } else {
            normal = nil
        }
        metalRough = try load("\(baseName)-metalrough", srgb: false)
        occlusion  = try load("\(baseName)-occlusion", srgb: false)
    }
}
----

Source: https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metalkit/mtktextureloader[MetalKit: MTKTextureLoader]

Loading a data texture with `SRGB` left to its default can silently apply the sRGB decode, which brightens roughness and tilts normals; always set it explicitly. Textures can also come from Model I/O materials:
`MDLMaterial` stores properties by semantic (`MDLMaterialSemantic.baseColor`, `.tangentSpaceNormal`, `.roughness`, `.metallic`, `.ambientOcclusion`), and a property's `textureSamplerValue` or `urlValue` gives the image to load, covered in xref:graphics-games/metal/models-with-model-io-and-usd.adoc[Models with Model I/O and USD]. Mipmaps, filtering and compressed formats (ASTC, BC) are in xref:graphics-games/metal/textures-and-samplers.adoc[Textures and Samplers]; the one rule specific to PBR
is that roughness maps should be mipmapped carefully (average the normal map's variance into the roughness when generating mips) or distant surfaces shimmer, a refinement that engines apply with offline tools.

=== Pipeline and drawing with optional maps

[source,swift]
----
// Build two pipeline variants from one shader: with and without a normal map.
func makePBRPipeline(device: MTLDevice, library: MTLLibrary, vertexDescriptor: MTLVertexDescriptor,
                     colorFormat: MTLPixelFormat, depthFormat: MTLPixelFormat,
                     withNormalMap: Bool) throws -> MTLRenderPipelineState {
    let constants = MTLFunctionConstantValues()
    var hasNormalMap = withNormalMap
    constants.setConstantValue(&hasNormalMap, type: .bool, index: 0)

    let desc = MTLRenderPipelineDescriptor()
    desc.vertexFunction = try library.makeFunction(name: "vertex_pbr", constantValues: constants)
    desc.fragmentFunction = try library.makeFunction(name: "fragment_pbr", constantValues: constants)
    desc.vertexDescriptor = vertexDescriptor
    desc.colorAttachments[0].pixelFormat = colorFormat
    desc.depthAttachmentPixelFormat = depthFormat
    return try device.makeRenderPipelineState(descriptor: desc)
}

// In the render loop, for each material:
func bind(_ textures: PBRTextureSet, factors: PBRMaterial, to encoder: MTLRenderCommandEncoder) {
    var factors = factors
    encoder.setFragmentBytes(&factors, length: MemoryLayout<PBRMaterial>.stride, index: BufferIndex.material.rawValue)
    encoder.setFragmentTexture(textures.baseColor,  index: TextureIndex.baseColor.rawValue)
    if let normal = textures.normal {
        encoder.setFragmentTexture(normal, index: TextureIndex.normal.rawValue)
    }
    encoder.setFragmentTexture(textures.metalRough, index: TextureIndex.metalRough.rawValue)
    encoder.setFragmentTexture(textures.occlusion,  index: TextureIndex.occlusion.rawValue)
}
----

Source: https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/mtlfunctionconstantvalues[Metal: MTLFunctionConstantValues]

== Normal mapping

A normal map stores, per texel, the direction the surface normal should have, expressed in *tangent space*: a coordinate frame glued to the surface, with Z along the geometric normal and X and Y along the texture's U and V directions. This makes the map independent of the object's
orientation, so one brick texture works on any wall. To use it the shader needs the frame, the TBN matrix, whose columns are the tangent T, the bitangent B and the normal N, all in world space; multiplying the unpacked tangent-space normal by it gives the world-space shading normal.

* *Tangents.* The vertex needs a tangent. Model I/O computes it from positions, normals and texture coordinates with `MDLMesh.addTangentBasis(forTextureCoordinateAttributeNamed:normalAttributeNamed:tangentAttributeNamed:)`, giving a tangent attribute (`MDLVertexAttributeTangent`) that the vertex descriptor then exposes as attribute 3. glTF assets carry a tangent with a fourth
  component, the *handedness* (1 or -1), which flips the bitangent for mirrored UVs: `pass:c[B = cross(N, T) * handedness]`.
* *Orthogonalise.* Interpolated vectors are not exactly perpendicular, so the example removes the part of T along N (Gram-Schmidt) before building B.
* *Unpack.* The stored value is in 0..1; \(n = 2\,\text{ texel } - 1\). A `normalScale` factor on the X and Y components strengthens or flattens the effect without leaving unit length once renormalised.
* *Green channel.* OpenGL and glTF maps have Y pointing up in texture space; DirectX maps point down. A wrongly oriented map lights bumps as if they were dents.
* *Without tangents.* A fragment function can build the frame from screen-space derivatives (`dfdx`, `dfdy` of the position and UV); it avoids a tangent attribute but costs derivatives and shows seams on mirrored UVs, so precomputed tangents are the default.

The normal map changes only the lighting, not the silhouette or the shadows, which is why it is combined with the shadow map of the next page and, for large relief, replaced by displacement through tessellation or a mesh shader
(xref:graphics-games/metal/tessellation.adoc[Tessellation], xref:graphics-games/metal/mesh-shaders.adoc[Mesh Shaders]).

== Skinning overview

Skeletal animation deforms a mesh by moving *joints* (bones) and blending each vertex between the joints that influence it. The Bookshelf's books are rigid and need no skinning, but characters, cloth-like props and anything bendy do, and Metal by Tutorials treats it alongside materials,
so this section is the overview you need to connect an animated asset to a Metal pipeline.

.A vertex blended between joints, with the data each stage needs
image::graphics-games-metal-pbr-materials-skinning-overview.svg["A chain of four joints with a vertex influenced by two of them, next to four boxes: per-vertex joint indices and weights, per-frame joint matrices, the blend in the vertex function, and the final position",width=720]

The data has two parts.

* *Per vertex*, from the asset: up to four joint indices and four weights that sum to 1. In Model I/O these are the attributes `MDLVertexAttributeJointIndices` and `MDLVertexAttributeJointWeights`; in a Metal vertex descriptor they are typically `.ushort4` and `.float4`. More than four influences per vertex are reduced to the four heaviest and renormalised by the exporter.
* *Per frame*, from the skeleton and the animation: one 4x4 matrix per joint, the *palette*. Each is the joint's current world transform multiplied by its *inverse bind matrix*, the transform that brings the mesh from the bind pose to the joint's local space:
  \(P_j = W_j \cdot B_j^{ -1 }\). Model I/O provides the skeleton (`MDLSkeleton`), packed joint animation (`MDLPackedJointAnimation`) and `MDLAnimationBindComponent` that binds an animation to a mesh; the same data from a glTF or USD file arrives through the loader you choose.

The palette is cheap to compute on the CPU for one character; for crowds, evaluate it in a compute kernel. The joints are stored parent before child, so a single pass computes the world transforms:

[source,swift]
----
import simd

struct Skeleton {
    var parents: [Int]                     // -1 for the root; parents come before their children
    var inverseBind: [simd_float4x4]
}

struct JointPose {
    var translation: simd_float3
    var rotation: simd_quatf
    var scale: simd_float3
    var matrix: simd_float4x4 {
        let r = simd_float4x4(rotation)
        let s = simd_float4x4(diagonal: simd_float4(scale, 1))
        var m = r * s
        m.columns.3 = simd_float4(translation, 1)
        return m
    }
}

/// Blend two keyframes, then build the palette written to the GPU each frame.
func jointPalette(skeleton: Skeleton, from a: [JointPose], to b: [JointPose], t: Float) -> [simd_float4x4] {
    var world = [simd_float4x4](repeating: matrix_identity_float4x4, count: skeleton.parents.count)
    var palette = world
    for j in 0..<skeleton.parents.count {
        let pose = JointPose(translation: simd_mix(a[j].translation, b[j].translation, simd_float3(repeating: t)),
                             rotation: simd_slerp(a[j].rotation, b[j].rotation, t),
                             scale: simd_mix(a[j].scale, b[j].scale, simd_float3(repeating: t)))
        let local = pose.matrix
        world[j] = skeleton.parents[j] < 0 ? local : world[skeleton.parents[j]] * local
        palette[j] = world[j] * skeleton.inverseBind[j]
    }
    return palette
}

// Per frame: copy the palette into this frame's slot of a ring buffer and bind it.
// jointBuffer.contents().advanced(by: slotOffset).copyMemory(from: palette, byteCount: palette.count * MemoryLayout<simd_float4x4>.stride)
// encoder.setVertexBuffer(jointBuffer, offset: slotOffset, index: BufferIndex.joints.rawValue)
----

Source: https://developer.apple.com/documentation/modelio/mdlskeleton[Model I/O: MDLSkeleton]

Rotations are interpolated as quaternions with `simd_slerp`; translations and scales are interpolated linearly. Interpolating matrices directly shears the mesh, which is why the pose is kept as translation, rotation and scale until the last step. The ring buffer is the usual
frames-in-flight pattern of xref:graphics-games/metal/metalkit-mtkview-and-swiftui.adoc[MetalKit, MTKView and SwiftUI]: the CPU writes the next frame's palette while the GPU still reads the previous one. The vertex function does the blend:

[source,cpp]
----
// Skinning.metal  (BufferIndexJoints = 6 added to ShaderTypes.h)
struct SkinnedVertexIn {
    float3  position [[attribute(0)]];
    float3  normal   [[attribute(1)]];
    float2  uv       [[attribute(2)]];
    float3  tangent  [[attribute(3)]];
    ushort4 joints   [[attribute(4)]];
    float4  weights  [[attribute(5)]];
};

vertex PBRVertexOut vertex_skinned(SkinnedVertexIn in [[stage_in]],
                                   constant FrameUniforms &frame  [[buffer(BufferIndexFrame)]],
                                   constant ModelUniforms &model  [[buffer(BufferIndexModel)]],
                                   constant float4x4 *palette     [[buffer(BufferIndexJoints)]])
{
    float4x4 skin = in.weights.x * palette[in.joints.x]
                  + in.weights.y * palette[in.joints.y]
                  + in.weights.z * palette[in.joints.z]
                  + in.weights.w * palette[in.joints.w];

    float4 world = model.model * (skin * float4(in.position, 1.0));
    float3x3 skin3 = float3x3(skin[0].xyz, skin[1].xyz, skin[2].xyz);   // correct for rotation and uniform scale

    PBRVertexOut out;
    out.position      = frame.viewProjection * world;
    out.worldPosition = world.xyz;
    out.worldNormal   = model.normalMatrix * (skin3 * in.normal);
    out.worldTangent  = model.normalMatrix * (skin3 * in.tangent);
    out.uv            = in.uv;
    return out;
}
----

Source: https://developer.apple.com/metal/Metal-Shading-Language-Specification.pdf[Metal Shading Language Specification]

The skinned vertex function returns the same `PBRVertexOut` as the static one, so the fragment function is shared. Points to know:

* *Weights must sum to 1.* If they do not, the mesh shrinks toward the origin; renormalise on import.
* *Normals.* Using the upper 3x3 of the blended matrix is exact for rotation and uniform scale. Non-uniform scale in a joint needs the inverse transpose of each matrix, which is rare in practice and expensive.
* *Shadow and ray-tracing passes.* The shadow pass of the next page must run the same skinning, or the shadow shows the bind pose. When a skinned mesh must also be ray traced, skin it into a buffer with a compute kernel
  (see xref:graphics-games/metal/compute-kernels.adoc[Compute Kernels]) and refit the acceleration structure from that buffer.
* *Linear blend skinning artefacts.* Large twists collapse the mesh (the "candy wrapper" effect). Corrective blend shapes, extra twist joints or dual-quaternion skinning address it; the data layout above stays the same except for what the palette holds.
* *Cost.* Four matrix multiply-adds per vertex are inexpensive on Apple GPUs; the palette upload is small. Pre-skinning once in compute, then reusing the result for the shadow and main passes, saves work when the mesh is drawn several times per frame.

== Common mistakes

|===
|Symptom |Likely cause

|Metals look grey or dim
|`F0` not tinted by the base colour, or an ambient term that ignores metallic (the diffuse of a metal is zero, so ambient must come from IBL specular)

|Plastic look on everything
|Roughness map read in sRGB, or `roughnessFactor` left at 0

|Normal-mapped bumps look inverted
|Green channel convention mismatch, or a mirrored UV without the handedness flip

|Specular is black at the edges
|`NdotV` not clamped away from zero before the division

|Bright pixels flicker on small highlights
|Roughness near zero without a clamp, or no mipmapped roughness; raise the minimum roughness

|Colour looks too dark overall
|Light intensities still in Blinn-Phong scale (about 1 instead of tens), or the render target not sRGB

|Skinned mesh collapses to a point
|Joint weights not normalised, or the palette missing the inverse bind matrix
|===

== Performance notes

* The shader is dominated by texture fetches and a few `pow` calls. Pack roughness, metallic and occlusion into one texture, as in glTF, so a material is three fetches instead of four.
* Use half precision for the colours, the roughness and the dot products; keep positions and the final divisions in `float`. Apple GPUs run `half` arithmetic at higher throughput; the visible risk is in the specular denominator, which is why the example clamps it.
* Replace `pow(1 - VdotH, 5)` with repeated multiplication if the compiler does not already do so; the exponent is a literal and the cost is small either way.
* Compile material variants through function constants (as with the normal map) instead of branching on a runtime flag per pixel; a uniform branch is cheap but a removed texture binding and a smaller register footprint are cheaper.
* Block-compress the textures (ASTC on every Apple GPU since A8, BC on Mac): a 2048 x 2048 RGBA8 base colour map is 16 MB, in ASTC 6x6 about 2 MB, which matters more for bandwidth than any shader trick. See xref:graphics-games/metal/textures-and-samplers.adoc[Textures and Samplers].

== What changed since the older guides

|===
|Older guide |Today

|Metal by Tutorials, 4th edition (Metal 3, Xcode 15), chapters 10, 11 and 21 to 24 on lighting, PBR and animation: a new `MTLCommandBuffer` every frame
|The model is unchanged. Metal 4 (26 OSes) binds data through an `MTL4ArgumentTable` and a residency set, reuses command buffers and compiles pipelines with `MTL4Compiler`; the old path still works.

|Metal by Tutorials, 4th edition: textures bound one by one per material
|Argument buffers, `gpuResourceID` and argument tables let a material be a single pointer; see the argument-buffer page. `MTLArgumentEncoder` is not deprecated.

|`makeDefaultLibrary()`, Metal 4 on A14 or an Apple-silicon Mac, glTF-style metallic-roughness materials and ASTC textures are the norm; PVRTC, among the compressed formats of chapter 6, is deprecated since iOS 18 and macOS 15.

|Both books: skeleton data from Model I/O
|Model I/O still provides `MDLSkeleton` and `MDLPackedJointAnimation`, but projects also load USD or glTF with their own code and play the animation themselves; the palette technique above is independent of the loader.
