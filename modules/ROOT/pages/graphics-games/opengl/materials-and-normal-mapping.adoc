= Materials and Normal Mapping in OpenGL
:description: How to give the Bookshelf surfaces detail without more triangles in OpenGL: tangent space and the TBN matrix, computing tangents on the CPU or with derivatives, normal and specular maps in GLSL 4.10, texture formats and filtering for normal maps, material uniforms and the glTF conventions.
:keywords: OpenGL, GLSL, normal mapping, bump mapping, tangent space, TBN, tangent, bitangent, MikkTSpace, specular map, gloss map, material, dFdx, BC5, RG11, glTF, Bookshelf GL

include::partial$ai-disclaimer.adoc[]

A *material* is the set of numbers and textures that tell a lighting shader how one surface reacts to light: its colour, how
glossy it is, how bumpy it looks. Blinn-Phong needs a diffuse colour, a specular colour and a shininess; a
xref:graphics-games/opengl/pbr-basics.adoc[physically based material] needs different inputs, but both can be driven by textures
so that each texel of the surface behaves differently. *Normal mapping* is the most effective of these textures: it stores a
perturbed surface normal in every texel, so a flat wooden board catches the lamp as if it had grain, and a book cover as if it
had embossed lettering, without adding a single triangle.

The facts were checked in November 2026. The versions documented are OpenGL 4.6 with GLSL 4.60 and OpenGL ES 3.2 with GLSL ES
3.20; the examples target a 4.1 core context (`#version 410 core`), which runs on every desktop platform including macOS, and note
the differences for ES 3.0 and later. Nothing on this page needs an extension, but the compressed texture formats mentioned
have different availability on desktop and mobile hardware. The lighting vocabulary (N, L, V, H, the model terms) is defined in
xref:graphics-games/opengl/lighting-blinn-phong.adoc[Lighting with Blinn-Phong] and the general explanation of normal maps is in
xref:graphics-games/lighting-and-shading-models.adoc[Lighting and Shading Models].

== Why tangent space

A normal map could in principle store a normal in object space: the texel colour is the world direction the surface should
face. That only works for an object that is never deformed, because rotating or animating the mesh changes what "up" means for
the texture, and the same texture cannot be reused on different faces. Almost all normal maps are therefore stored in *tangent
space*, relative to the surface itself. In tangent space the unperturbed normal is always \( (0, 0, 1) \); a texel that leans
to the right of the surface stores a positive x, a texel that leans towards the top of the texture stores a positive y.

The three axes of that frame at a vertex are:

* *T*, the *tangent*, the direction on the surface in which the texture coordinate \( u \) increases.
* *B*, the *bitangent* (also called binormal), the direction in which \( v \) increases.
* *N*, the geometric normal of the surface.

.The tangent-space basis of a triangle
image::graphics-games-opengl-materials-and-normal-mapping-tbn.svg["A triangle with its tangent, bitangent and normal arrows beside the matching u and v axes of the texture",width=720]

The matrix with the three vectors as columns, \( TBN = [\,\mathbf\{T}\;\mathbf\{B}\;\mathbf\{N}\,] \), rotates a vector from tangent
space into world space (or into whatever space T, B and N are expressed in). Sampling a normal map therefore takes three steps:
fetch the texel, decode it to a vector, rotate it by the TBN matrix, and use the result as the N of the lighting equations.

.How a normal map stores directions as colours
image::graphics-games-opengl-materials-and-normal-mapping-encoding.svg["Four colour swatches of a normal map with the decoded vectors and the decode formula",width=720]

A colour channel stores values in \( [0, 1] \), a direction component lies in \( [-1, 1] \), so the decode is
\( \mathbf\{n} = 2\,\mathrm\{rgb} - 1 \), followed by a normalisation because 8-bit quantisation and filtering leave the vector
slightly short. A flat area is \( (0.5, 0.5, 1.0) \), the pale blue that every normal map is recognisable by.

== Where the tangents come from

The tangent frame must be consistent with how the texture is mapped, so it is derived from the UV coordinates. There are three
common sources, and they should be chosen once per asset pipeline.

[mermaid]
....
flowchart TD
    A["Asset has tangents?"] -->|"yes, glTF TANGENT attribute"| B["Use the stored vec4 (xyz tangent, w handedness)"]
    A -->|"no"| C{"Can you preprocess?"}
    C -->|"yes"| D["Compute with MikkTSpace at load time and upload as an attribute"]
    C -->|"no or procedural mesh"| E{"Per-triangle accuracy enough?"}
    E -->|"yes"| F["Compute from edges and UV deltas on the CPU"]
    E -->|"no, shader only"| G["Derive in the fragment shader with dFdx and dFdy"]
....

*Stored tangents.* The glTF 2.0 format has an optional `TANGENT` attribute: a `vec4` whose xyz is the tangent and whose w is
`+1.0+` or `+-1.0+`, the handedness used to rebuild the bitangent as \( \mathbf\{B} = w\,(\mathbf\{N} \times \mathbf\{T}) \).
When the attribute is absent the specification says that clients must compute tangents with the MikkTSpace algorithm, which is
the one most baking tools use, so that the shaded result matches the baker.

*CPU computation per triangle.* From a triangle with positions \( p_0, p_1, p_2 \) and texture coordinates
\( uv_0, uv_1, uv_2 \), the edge vectors \( e_1 = p_1 - p_0 \), \( e_2 = p_2 - p_0 \) and the UV differences
\( (\Delta u_1, \Delta v_1) \), \( (\Delta u_2, \Delta v_2) \) satisfy \( e_i = \Delta u_i\,\mathbf\{T} + \Delta v_i\,\mathbf\{B} \).
Solving the 2x2 system gives:

\[ \mathbf\{T} = \frac\{ \Delta v_2\,e_1 - \Delta v_1\,e_2 }{ \Delta u_1 \Delta v_2 - \Delta u_2 \Delta v_1 } \qquad
   \mathbf\{B} = \frac\{ \Delta u_1\,e_2 - \Delta u_2\,e_1 }{ \Delta u_1 \Delta v_2 - \Delta u_2 \Delta v_1 } \]

Per-vertex tangents are the sum of the tangents of the triangles that share the vertex, orthogonalised against the normal.

*Fragment-shader derivatives.* When there is no tangent attribute, the screen-space derivatives `dFdx` and `dFdy` of the world
position and of the UV give T and B per pixel. This needs no vertex data and works on procedural geometry, at the cost of
a little arithmetic, a less accurate frame on thin triangles and discontinuities at triangle boundaries.

=== Computing tangents on the CPU

[source,cpp]
----
#include <glm/glm.hpp>
#include <cmath>
#include <vector>

struct Vertex {
    glm::vec3 pos;
    glm::vec3 normal;
    glm::vec2 uv;
    glm::vec4 tangent;   // xyz = tangent, w = handedness (+1 or -1), like glTF
};

// Adds the tangent of every triangle to its three vertices, then orthogonalises.
void computeTangents(std::vector<Vertex>& v, const std::vector<unsigned>& idx)
{
    std::vector<glm::vec3> tan(v.size(), glm::vec3(0.0f));
    std::vector<glm::vec3> bit(v.size(), glm::vec3(0.0f));

    for (size_t i = 0; i + 2 < idx.size(); i += 3) {
        const Vertex& a = v[idx[i]];
        const Vertex& b = v[idx[i + 1]];
        const Vertex& c = v[idx[i + 2]];

        const glm::vec3 e1 = b.pos - a.pos, e2 = c.pos - a.pos;
        const glm::vec2 d1 = b.uv - a.uv,   d2 = c.uv - a.uv;
        const float det = d1.x * d2.y - d2.x * d1.y;
        if (std::abs(det) < 1e-12f) continue;          // degenerate UV mapping
        const float r = 1.0f / det;

        const glm::vec3 t = (e1 * d2.y - e2 * d1.y) * r;
        const glm::vec3 bt = (e2 * d1.x - e1 * d2.x) * r;
        for (int k = 0; k < 3; ++k) { tan[idx[i + k]] += t; bit[idx[i + k]] += bt; }
    }

    for (size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        const glm::vec3 n = v[i].normal;
        glm::vec3 t = tan[i] - n * glm::dot(n, tan[i]);          // Gram-Schmidt
        t = glm::length(t) > 1e-8f ? glm::normalize(t) : glm::vec3(1, 0, 0);
        const float w = glm::dot(glm::cross(n, t), bit[i]) < 0.0f ? -1.0f : 1.0f;
        v[i].tangent = glm::vec4(t, w);
    }
}
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/glTF/specs/2.0/glTF-2.0.html#meshes[glTF 2.0 specification: meshes (tangent attribute)].

The handedness `w` handles mirrored UVs, for example a book model whose left and right sides
share one half of a texture: without it the bumps on the mirrored half look inverted.

== The shaders

The vertex shader builds the world-space frame. N uses the normal matrix, as explained on the
xref:graphics-games/opengl/lighting-blinn-phong.adoc[lighting page]; T is a direction that lies on the surface, so it
transforms with the upper 3x3 of the model matrix.

[source,glsl]
----
#version 410 core

layout(location = 0) in vec3 aPos;
layout(location = 1) in vec3 aNormal;
layout(location = 2) in vec2 aUV;
layout(location = 3) in vec4 aTangent;   // xyz tangent, w handedness

uniform mat4 uModel;
uniform mat4 uView;
uniform mat4 uProj;
uniform mat3 uNormalMatrix;

out VS_OUT {
    vec3 worldPos;
    vec2 uv;
    mat3 tbn;             // columns T, B, N in world space
} vs;

void main()
{
    vec3 N = normalize(uNormalMatrix * aNormal);
    vec3 T = normalize(mat3(uModel) * aTangent.xyz);
    T = normalize(T - dot(T, N) * N);                 // keep T perpendicular to N
    vec3 B = cross(N, T) * aTangent.w;

    vec4 world = uModel * vec4(aPos, 1.0);
    vs.worldPos = world.xyz;
    vs.uv = aUV;
    vs.tbn = mat3(T, B, N);
    gl_Position = uProj * uView * world;
}
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL/specs/gl/GLSLangSpec.4.60.html[OpenGL Shading Language 4.60 specification].

A `mat3` can be passed between stages like any other value; it uses three consecutive input and output locations. After the
rasteriser interpolates the columns they are no longer exactly orthogonal, so the fragment shader may orthonormalise again.

[source,glsl]
----
#version 410 core

in VS_OUT {
    vec3 worldPos;
    vec2 uv;
    mat3 tbn;
} fs;

uniform sampler2D uAlbedoMap;     // sRGB texture (GL_SRGB8_ALPHA8), decoded to linear on sampling
uniform sampler2D uNormalMap;     // linear texture (GL_RGB8 or a two-channel format)
uniform sampler2D uSpecMap;       // linear single channel: how glossy each texel is
uniform float     uNormalScale;   // 1.0 = as baked, smaller flattens the bumps

uniform vec3  uViewPos;
uniform vec3  uLightPos;
uniform vec3  uLightColor;
uniform float uAmbient;
uniform float uShininess;

out vec4 FragColor;

void main()
{
    vec3 albedo = texture(uAlbedoMap, fs.uv).rgb;
    float gloss = texture(uSpecMap, fs.uv).r;

    vec3 t = texture(uNormalMap, fs.uv).xyz * 2.0 - 1.0;
    t.xy *= uNormalScale;                              // glTF normalTexture.scale
    vec3 N = normalize(fs.tbn * normalize(t));

    vec3 L = normalize(uLightPos - fs.worldPos);
    vec3 V = normalize(uViewPos - fs.worldPos);
    vec3 H = normalize(L + V);

    float ndotl = max(dot(N, L), 0.0);
    float spec  = ndotl > 0.0 ? pow(max(dot(N, H), 0.0), uShininess) : 0.0;

    vec3 color = uAmbient * albedo * uLightColor
               + ndotl * albedo * uLightColor
               + spec * gloss * uLightColor;
    FragColor = vec4(color, 1.0);
}
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/texture.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: texture].

The statement `+t.xy *= uNormalScale+` scales the x and y components before the re-normalisation, which is how the glTF
specification defines the scale of a normal texture. With a scale of 0 the surface looks flat; with a scale above 1 the bumps are
exaggerated.

=== A fragment-shader alternative without tangent attributes

When a mesh has no tangents, a cotangent frame can be built from derivatives. The function below returns a `mat3` that
replaces `fs.tbn` in the shader above:

[source,glsl]
----
mat3 cotangentFrame(vec3 N, vec3 worldPos, vec2 uv)
{
    vec3 dp1 = dFdx(worldPos);
    vec3 dp2 = dFdy(worldPos);
    vec2 duv1 = dFdx(uv);
    vec2 duv2 = dFdy(uv);

    vec3 dp2perp = cross(dp2, N);
    vec3 dp1perp = cross(N, dp1);
    vec3 T = dp2perp * duv1.x + dp1perp * duv2.x;
    vec3 B = dp2perp * duv1.y + dp1perp * duv2.y;

    float invmax = inversesqrt(max(dot(T, T), dot(B, B)));
    return mat3(T * invmax, B * invmax, N);
}
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/dFdx.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: dFdx, dFdy, fwidth].

The derivatives are computed on 2x2 pixel quads, so they must be evaluated in uniform control flow. Do not call
`cotangentFrame` inside a branch that only some pixels of a quad take.

== The normal map texture

Create the map as a *linear* texture: `GL_RGB8` rather than `GL_SRGB8`, because the values are directions, not colours, and an
sRGB decode would bend them. The Bookshelf asset set ships base colour textures only, so the wood normal map is produced at
load time from the brightness of the wood texture with a Sobel filter, which is also how bump maps from height maps are
converted:

[source,cpp]
----
#include <glad/gl.h>
#include <glm/glm.hpp>
#include <algorithm>
#include <cstdint>
#include <vector>

// height: w*h bytes, 0..255. strength: larger means steeper bumps.
std::vector<std::uint8_t> normalMapFromHeight(const std::uint8_t* height, int w, int h, float strength)
{
    auto at = [&](int x, int y) {
        x = (x + w) % w;  y = (y + h) % h;                 // wrap: the wood tiles
        return height[y * w + x] / 255.0f;
    };
    std::vector<std::uint8_t> out(static_cast<size_t>(w) * h * 3);
    for (int y = 0; y < h; ++y) {
        for (int x = 0; x < w; ++x) {
            const float dx = (at(x + 1, y - 1) + 2 * at(x + 1, y) + at(x + 1, y + 1))
                           - (at(x - 1, y - 1) + 2 * at(x - 1, y) + at(x - 1, y + 1));
            const float dy = (at(x - 1, y + 1) + 2 * at(x, y + 1) + at(x + 1, y + 1))
                           - (at(x - 1, y - 1) + 2 * at(x, y - 1) + at(x + 1, y - 1));
            // Image rows grow downward but v grows upward, hence +dy for the green channel.
            glm::vec3 n = glm::normalize(glm::vec3(-dx * strength, dy * strength, 1.0f));
            const size_t o = (static_cast<size_t>(y) * w + x) * 3;
            for (int c = 0; c < 3; ++c)
                out[o + c] = static_cast<std::uint8_t>(std::clamp(n[c] * 0.5f + 0.5f, 0.0f, 1.0f) * 255.0f + 0.5f);
        }
    }
    return out;
}

GLuint createNormalMap(const std::vector<std::uint8_t>& rgb, int w, int h)
{
    GLuint tex;
    glGenTextures(1, &tex);
    glBindTexture(GL_TEXTURE_2D, tex);
    glPixelStorei(GL_UNPACK_ALIGNMENT, 1);                  // RGB rows are not 4-byte aligned for odd widths
    glTexImage2D(GL_TEXTURE_2D, 0, GL_RGB8, w, h, 0, GL_RGB, GL_UNSIGNED_BYTE, rgb.data());
    glGenerateMipmap(GL_TEXTURE_2D);
    glTexParameteri(GL_TEXTURE_2D, GL_TEXTURE_MIN_FILTER, GL_LINEAR_MIPMAP_LINEAR);
    glTexParameteri(GL_TEXTURE_2D, GL_TEXTURE_MAG_FILTER, GL_LINEAR);
    glTexParameteri(GL_TEXTURE_2D, GL_TEXTURE_WRAP_S, GL_REPEAT);
    glTexParameteri(GL_TEXTURE_2D, GL_TEXTURE_WRAP_T, GL_REPEAT);
    return tex;
}
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glTexImage2D.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: glTexImage2D] and https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glGenerateMipmap.xhtml[glGenerateMipmap].

The Bookshelf wood and cover textures are in the repository as xref:attachment$bookshelf.glb[bookshelf.glb], with the
individual images in the `bookshelf-textures` folder next to it. On OpenGL 4.2 and later and on ES 3.0, immutable storage
with `glTexStorage2D` is the recommended allocation; the textures page covers it in
xref:graphics-games/opengl/textures-and-samplers.adoc[Textures and Samplers].

=== Texture formats for normal maps

[cols="1,2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Format |Where |Notes

|`GL_RGB8`
|everywhere
|Simple, three 8-bit channels, 3 bytes per texel. Decode with `+* 2.0 - 1.0+` and normalise.

|`GL_RG8` plus reconstruction
|GL 3.0+, ES 3.0+
|Store only x and y; rebuild \( z = \sqrt\{ 1 - x^\{2} - y^\{2} } \) in the shader since z is never negative in tangent space.
Saves 33 percent of memory.

|`GL_COMPRESSED_RG_RGTC2` (BC5)
|desktop GL 3.0+ (RGTC core in 3.0)
|Two independent compressed channels, 8 bits per texel, the best desktop choice. Rebuild z in the shader.

|`GL_COMPRESSED_RG11_EAC`
|ES 3.0+ and GL 4.3+
|Two-channel compressed format, the ES 3.0 counterpart of BC5.

|ASTC (for example `GL_COMPRESSED_RGBA_ASTC_4x4_KHR`)
|core (LDR profile) in ES 3.2; an extension on earlier ES versions and on desktop GPUs that support it
|Flexible block sizes. Normal maps are compressed with a two-channel mode; check the extension at run time.
|===

Never compress a normal map with a format designed for colour such as DXT1: the blocks cause visible faceting in the lighting.

Mipmaps of a normal map average neighbouring directions, so distant texels get shorter, less-perturbed vectors. That is the
correct behaviour (the surface looks smoother as it recedes) and is why the shader re-normalises after sampling. For specular
shimmer on distant bumpy surfaces, techniques such as Toksvig factors reduce the shininess with the length of the filtered
normal.

== Material data in the application

A material is a bundle of textures and uniforms bound before a draw call. Keep it as plain data and bind it in one function so
that draws can be sorted by material:

[source,cpp]
----
struct Material {
    GLuint albedoMap = 0;      // sRGB
    GLuint normalMap = 0;      // linear
    GLuint specMap   = 0;      // linear
    float  shininess = 64.0f;
    float  normalScale = 1.0f;
};

void bindMaterial(GLuint prog, const Material& m)
{
    glActiveTexture(GL_TEXTURE0);  glBindTexture(GL_TEXTURE_2D, m.albedoMap);
    glActiveTexture(GL_TEXTURE1);  glBindTexture(GL_TEXTURE_2D, m.normalMap);
    glActiveTexture(GL_TEXTURE2);  glBindTexture(GL_TEXTURE_2D, m.specMap);
    glUniform1i(glGetUniformLocation(prog, "uAlbedoMap"), 0);
    glUniform1i(glGetUniformLocation(prog, "uNormalMap"), 1);
    glUniform1i(glGetUniformLocation(prog, "uSpecMap"), 2);
    glUniform1f(glGetUniformLocation(prog, "uShininess"), m.shininess);
    glUniform1f(glGetUniformLocation(prog, "uNormalScale"), m.normalScale);
}
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glActiveTexture.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: glActiveTexture] and https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glBindTexture.xhtml[glBindTexture].

A sampler uniform holds the number of a texture unit, set with `glUniform1i`; it is not a texture name. The sampler uniforms and
their unit assignment can also be written once in GLSL with `layout(binding = 1)` on GL 4.2 and later, which does not exist
in the 4.1 baseline of this page. The Bookshelf scene uses four materials from the glTF file: wood for the shelf, and cover,
spine and pages for the books.

== Specular, gloss and other material maps

The same per-texel idea applies to every other material input.

* A *specular map* (or gloss map) multiplies the highlight, so that the glossy cover shows a highlight and the cloth spine does
  not, as in the shader above. It is a single linear channel.
* A *shininess map* changes the exponent per texel; in a physically based pipeline this becomes the roughness map.
* An *ambient occlusion map* darkens crevices, applied to the ambient term only. In glTF it is the red channel of the
  occlusion texture.
* An *emissive map* adds light that the surface itself emits, for example the glowing lamp.
* An *opacity map* or the alpha channel of the base colour drives transparency or alpha testing, see
  xref:graphics-games/opengl/blending-depth-and-stencil.adoc[Blending, Depth and Stencil].
* A *height map* can drive parallax mapping, which shifts the texture coordinate along the view direction by the height to fake
  depth. It costs extra texture reads and breaks at grazing angles; use it sparingly.

Pack several single-channel maps into one texture (occlusion, roughness and metallic in the red, green and blue channels, the
glTF layout) to reduce texture reads and binds; the packed texture must be linear.

== Common mistakes

* *Sampling the normal map as sRGB.* The decoded values are bent and the lighting looks lumpy. Use a linear internal format.
* *Wrong green channel.* OpenGL and glTF maps have green pointing up (+Y); some DirectX-oriented tools bake green inverted. If
  bumps look dented where they should protrude, flip green (`t.y = -t.y`) or re-export.
* *Skipping the normalisation.* Decoded, filtered and interpolated vectors are not unit length.
* *Normals from the mesh instead of the map.* Passing the geometric normal into the lighting equations leaves the map unused.
  Check that the mapped normal reaches the lighting code.
* *Mirror seams.* Texture coordinates that are mirrored without a handedness sign give inverted bumps on one half; keep `w`.
* *Non-orthogonal frames.* T and N that are not perpendicular shear the lighting. Orthogonalise with Gram-Schmidt.
* *Transforming T with the normal matrix.* The normal matrix is for normals; tangents use the model matrix.
* *Sharing tangents across UV seams.* Vertices at a seam need separate tangents because their UV derivatives differ;
  duplicate them as you do for normals at hard edges.
* *Compressing with the wrong codec.* Use two-channel formats or a normal-map-aware encoder.

== Performance notes

* A normal-mapped fragment costs one more texture read, a matrix multiply and a normalisation compared to plain Blinn-Phong,
  which is cheap on any GPU of the last decade.
* Tangent attributes add 16 bytes per vertex. Quantising them to `GL_BYTE` or `GL_SHORT` normalised components saves
  memory bandwidth; see xref:graphics-games/opengl/buffers-and-vertex-specification.adoc[Buffers and Vertex Specification].
* Packing normal, roughness and occlusion into as few textures as possible reduces texture units and cache pressure, and
  compressed formats cut memory bandwidth by 4 to 8 times.
* On mobile tile-based GPUs, fetches from compressed textures are particularly valuable; ASTC and ETC2 are the usual choices.
* Mipmaps are mandatory for minified normal maps; without them the bump detail shimmers.

== What the books cover and where they are outdated

The classic books introduce bump mapping and normal mapping with GLSL 1.10-era shaders, `gl_MultiTexCoord0`,
`gl_NormalMatrix`, `gl_TextureMatrix` and the `attribute` and `varying` keywords, and often compute the tangent in the vertex
shader from custom attribute slots. The technique is unchanged, but all of those built-ins and keywords are gone from the core
profile.

== Recent changes

* ASTC (LDR) compression is part of OpenGL ES 3.2 (2015, revision 2022); on desktop it remains an
  extension, with BC5 (RGTC) the portable choice.
* OpenGL 4.5 (2014) and ARB_direct_state_access make the texture creation shown above shorter with `glCreateTextures`
  and `glTextureStorage2D`; see xref:graphics-games/opengl/direct-state-access.adoc[Direct State Access].
* glTF 2.0 standardised the normal-texture scale, the tangent attribute and the MikkTSpace fallback, so a model from any tool
  agrees on the frame.
* The shading itself moves to a physically based model in xref:graphics-games/opengl/pbr-basics.adoc[PBR Basics]; the normal map
  and TBN code of this page carry over unchanged.
