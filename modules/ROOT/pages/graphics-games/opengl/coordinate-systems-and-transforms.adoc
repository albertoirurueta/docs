= Coordinate Systems and Transforms in OpenGL
:description: The model, view and projection chain in OpenGL with GLM: object, world, view, clip, NDC and window coordinates, column-major matrices, the perspective divide, the viewport and depth-range transforms, depth precision, glClipControl and reverse-Z, with complete C++20 code that rotates the Bookshelf book.
:keywords: OpenGL, coordinate systems, model matrix, view matrix, projection matrix, MVP, GLM, NDC, clip space, perspective divide, viewport, glViewport, glDepthRange, glClipControl, reverse-Z, column-major, glUniformMatrix4fv, depth buffer

include::partial$graphics-games-opengl-disclaimer.adoc[]

Every vertex that OpenGL draws travels through a fixed sequence of coordinate systems, and the vertex shader you write performs
the part that you control: three matrices that take a position from the book's own coordinates to *clip space*. Everything after
that, the perspective divide and the mapping to pixels and depth values, is fixed-function and configured by two calls,
`glViewport` and `glDepthRange` (or `glClipControl` on newer contexts). This page follows one vertex of the Bookshelf book from
object space to a pixel, defines each space with the exact conventions of OpenGL, explains the column-major storage that
confuses most newcomers, and finishes with a complete {cpp}20 program that spins the book with GLM matrices.

The facts were checked in November 2026. The versions documented are OpenGL 4.6 core (4.1 as the macOS ceiling, which the program
targets), OpenGL ES 3.2 where it differs, and GLM for the maths. `glClipControl` needs OpenGL 4.5 (or `GL_ARB_clip_control`) and
is not available on macOS; on OpenGL ES the equivalent is the extension `GL_EXT_clip_control`. For the mathematics that is
common to all APIs see xref:graphics-games/coordinate-spaces-and-transforms.adoc[Coordinate Spaces and Transforms] and
xref:graphics-games/vectors-matrices-and-homogeneous-coordinates.adoc[Vectors, Matrices and Homogeneous Coordinates]; rotations
in depth are in xref:graphics-games/rotations-and-quaternions.adoc[Rotations and Quaternions]. The camera side (lookAt, field of
view, the orbit camera) is in xref:graphics-games/opengl/cameras-and-projection.adoc[Cameras and Projection].

== The chain from object space to window

.The six spaces a Bookshelf vertex passes through
image::graphics-games-opengl-coordinate-systems-and-transforms-chain.svg["Six boxes: object, world, view, clip, NDC and window space, with the model matrix, the view matrix, the projection matrix, the divide by w and the viewport and depth range transform between them",width=720]

1. *Object space* (also model or local space): the coordinates stored in the vertex buffer. The Bookshelf book is a box whose
   centre is the origin.
2. *World space*: the shared space of the scene. The *model matrix* \( M \) places the book on the shelf.
3. *View space* (also eye or camera space): the world as seen from the camera, which sits at the origin and looks down the
   negative Z axis. The *view matrix* \( V \) is the inverse of the camera's own placement.
4. *Clip space*: homogeneous coordinates \( (x, y, z, w) \) produced by the *projection matrix* \( P \). The vertex shader
   writes them to the built-in `+gl_Position+`. A point is inside the view volume when \( -w \le x, y, z \le w \).
5. *Normalised device coordinates* (NDC): the result of dividing by \( w \). The visible volume becomes the cube from
   \( -1 \) to \( 1 \) on every axis.
6. *Window coordinates*: pixels and a depth value, produced by the viewport and depth-range transform.

The whole shader-side chain for one vertex is

\[ \mathbf\{p}_\{clip} = P \, V \, M \, \mathbf\{p}_\{object} \]

with all four operands in homogeneous form, \( \mathbf\{p}_\{object} = (x, y, z, 1)^T \). Matrices are applied right to left: the
vector is multiplied by \( M \) first. OpenGL has used column vectors from the start, and so does GLM, which is why the product
reads \( P V M \) and not \( M V P \).

=== Why the chain is split in three

You could multiply one matrix per object on the CPU and send a single `uMvp` uniform, and the example below does exactly that.
The three-way split exists because the three matrices change at different rates:

* \( M \) changes per object per frame (the book rotates).
* \( V \) changes per camera per frame (the orbit camera moves).
* \( P \) changes only when the window is resized or the field of view changes.

Lighting needs the intermediate spaces too: a Blinn-Phong shader computes light and view vectors in view or world space, so it
needs \( M \) (or \( V M \)) separately. See xref:graphics-games/opengl/lighting-blinn-phong.adoc[Lighting: Blinn-Phong].

== Column-major storage

OpenGL and GLSL store matrices in *column-major* order: the 16 floats of a `mat4` are column 0, then column 1, column 2 and
column 3. GLM follows the same rule, so a `glm::mat4` can be passed to OpenGL without any conversion.

.How a 4 by 4 matrix sits in memory
image::graphics-games-opengl-coordinate-systems-and-transforms-column-major.svg["Left: a 4 by 4 grid labelled m00 to m33 with its array indices, the translation column highlighted. Right: the 16 floats in memory, grouped by column",width=720]

The element at row \( r \) and column \( c \) is at index \( 4c + r \). The translation \( (t_x, t_y, t_z) \) of a transformation
matrix sits in the *last column*, so it occupies array elements 12, 13 and 14. In GLM the same element is written `m[col][row]`:
`m[3]` is the fourth column, `m[3][0]` is \( t_x \). That `m[a][b]` means "column a, row b" is the second most common source of
confusion, after the next one.

=== Uploading matrices

[source,cpp]
----
#include <glm/glm.hpp>
#include <glm/gtc/matrix_transform.hpp>
#include <glm/gtc/type_ptr.hpp>

// A translation by (2, 3, 4): the last column of the matrix holds the offset.
glm::mat4 t = glm::translate(glm::mat4(1.0f), glm::vec3(2.0f, 3.0f, 4.0f));
const float* a = glm::value_ptr(t);
// a[12] == 2, a[13] == 3, a[14] == 4

// GLM and GLSL are both column-major, so no transpose is needed.
glUniformMatrix4fv(location, 1, GL_FALSE, glm::value_ptr(t));
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glUniform.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: glUniform].

The third argument of `glUniformMatrix4fv` is `transpose`. Pass `GL_FALSE` when the data is column-major, which is the case for
GLM, for matrices you write by hand as `float m[16]` in column order and for the output of the Android helper class
`android.opengl.Matrix`. In OpenGL ES 2.0 passing `GL_TRUE` is an error; the parameter exists in OpenGL ES 3.0 and later. Another
reason to leave it `GL_FALSE`: the GPU would have to transpose every upload.

In GLSL, `m[1]` is column 1 and `m[1][2]` is element row 2 of that column, the same convention. The multiplication `+M * v+` treats
`v` as a column vector. Reversing the order, `+v * M+`, treats it as a row vector and multiplies by the transpose; it is occasionally
used on purpose and is a bug otherwise.

== Building the model matrix

A model matrix is a product of translation \( T \), rotation \( R \) and scale \( S \), in the order \( T R S \): scale first, then
rotate about the object's origin, then move.

\[
T =
\begin\{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 & t_x \\
0 & 1 & 0 & t_y \\
0 & 0 & 1 & t_z \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end\{pmatrix},
\quad
S =
\begin\{pmatrix}
s_x & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & s_y & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & s_z & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end\{pmatrix},
\quad
R_y(\alpha) =
\begin\{pmatrix}
\cos\alpha & 0 & \sin\alpha & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
-\sin\alpha & 0 & \cos\alpha & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end\{pmatrix}
\]

In code, the matrix is built by multiplying from the left, so the line that reads first applies last:

[source,cpp]
----
// Bookshelf book: 0.60 wide, 0.90 tall, 0.18 thick; a unit cube scaled to that size.
glm::mat4 bookModel(float angleRadians, const glm::vec3& position) {
    glm::mat4 m(1.0f);
    m = glm::translate(m, position);                               // applied last
    m = glm::rotate(m, angleRadians, glm::vec3(0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f)); // applied second
    m = glm::scale(m, glm::vec3(0.60f, 0.90f, 0.18f));             // applied first
    return m;
}
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glUniform.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: glUniform] (matrix upload) and
https://github.com/g-truc/glm[GLM] for the matrix functions.

Swapping the order produces a different, often surprising, result: rotating *after* translating swings the book around the world
origin instead of spinning it in place. A non-uniform scale after a rotation shears the object. When normals are transformed for
lighting, the matrix to use is the inverse transpose of the upper-left 3 by 3 block of \( M \), not \( M \) itself; the lighting
page explains why.

GLM angles are in radians (recent releases make this the default; define `GLM_FORCE_RADIANS` in older ones), so convert degrees with
`glm::radians`.

== The view matrix

The view matrix is the inverse of the transform that places the camera in the world. `glm::lookAt(eye, target, up)` builds it
directly from a camera position, the point it looks at and an up direction; the maths and an orbit camera are in
xref:graphics-games/opengl/cameras-and-projection.adoc[Cameras and Projection]. After \( V \) the camera is at the origin
looking along \( -Z \), with \( +X \) to the right and \( +Y \) up: view space is *right-handed*.

== The projection matrix

The projection matrix maps the view frustum to the clip-space volume. For a symmetric perspective frustum with vertical field of
view \( \theta \), aspect ratio \( a \) (width over height), near distance \( n \) and far distance \( f \), the OpenGL matrix is

\[
P =
\begin\{pmatrix}
\frac\{1}{a \tan(\theta/2)} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \frac\{1}{\tan(\theta/2)} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \frac{f + n}{n - f} & \frac{2 f n}{n - f} \\
0 & 0 & -1 & 0
\end\{pmatrix}
\]

The bottom row \( (0, 0, -1, 0) \) copies the negated view-space \( z \) into \( w \): for a point at distance \( d \) in front of the
camera, \( w = d \). That is what makes the later division a perspective division. The third row maps view-space depth into
\( z_\{clip} \), and after the division the NDC depth of a point at distance \( d \) is

\[ z_\{ndc} = \frac{f + n}{f - n} - \frac{2 f n}{(f - n)\, d} \]

which is \( -1 \) at \( d = n \) and \( +1 \) at \( d = f \). The orthographic matrix, used for 2D, shadow maps and technical views,
has \( w = 1 \) for every point and the form

\[
O =
\begin\{pmatrix}
\frac\{2}{r - l} & 0 & 0 & -\frac{r + l}{r - l} \\
0 & \frac\{2}{t - b} & 0 & -\frac{t + b}{t - b} \\
0 & 0 & -\frac\{2}{f - n} & -\frac{f + n}{f - n} \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end\{pmatrix}
\]

for a box with left, right, bottom, top, near and far planes. GLM builds them with `glm::perspective(fovy, aspect, n, f)` and
`glm::ortho(l, r, b, t, n, f)`, both of which target the OpenGL convention of depth \( -1 \) to \( 1 \) unless a configuration macro
says otherwise (see below).

== Clipping, the perspective divide and NDC

The vertex shader output \( (x_c, y_c, z_c, w_c) \) is *clipped* against the view volume: a primitive is cut so that every
remaining point satisfies \( -w_c \le x_c, y_c, z_c \le w_c \). Clipping happens in clip space, before the divide, because dividing
a point with \( w \le 0 \) (behind the camera) would produce garbage. The surviving points are divided:

\[ (x_\{ndc}, y_\{ndc}, z_\{ndc}) = \left( \frac\{x_c}\{w_c}, \frac\{y_c}\{w_c}, \frac\{z_c}\{w_c} \right) \]

This is the perspective divide; it is where far objects get smaller. All other vertex attributes are interpolated across the
triangle *perspective-correctly*, using the \( 1/w \) of each vertex, unless the fragment input is declared `noperspective`.
The details are in xref:graphics-games/opengl/rendering-pipeline.adoc[The OpenGL Rendering Pipeline].

== The viewport and depth-range transform

NDC is resolution-independent. The last transform maps it to the framebuffer. With `glViewport(x0, y0, w, h)` and
`glDepthRange(n, f)` (the depth-range values, not the projection planes), the window coordinates are

\[
x_w = \frac\{w}\{2}\,(x_\{ndc} + 1) + x_0, \qquad
y_w = \frac\{h}\{2}\,(y_\{ndc} + 1) + y_0, \qquad
z_w = \frac{f - n}\{2}\, z_\{ndc} + \frac{n + f}\{2}
\]

With the default depth range \( [0, 1] \) the depth written to the depth buffer is \( z_w = (z_\{ndc} + 1)/2 \). The window origin is
the *lower-left* corner and \( y \) grows upwards, unlike Vulkan, Metal, Direct3D and the web, which put the origin at the upper
left.

[source,cpp]
----
// On resize, use the framebuffer size in pixels, not the window size in screen coordinates.
glfwSetFramebufferSizeCallback(window, [](GLFWwindow*, int width, int height) {
    glViewport(0, 0, width, height);
});
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glViewport.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: glViewport] and
https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glDepthRange.xhtml[glDepthRange].

On a high-DPI display the framebuffer has more pixels than the window has screen coordinates, so `glfwGetWindowSize` gives the wrong
number to `glViewport` and the picture fills a quarter of the window. Always use the framebuffer size, and recompute the
projection's aspect ratio from it. `glViewport` also accepts an offset, which is how split screen and render-to-texture sub-rectangles
are drawn; it is clamped to `GL_MAX_VIEWPORT_DIMS`. A *scissor* rectangle (`glScissor`) is separate: it discards fragments, the viewport
scales the image.

=== The conventions of other APIs

.Clip-space and window conventions of OpenGL against other APIs
image::graphics-games-opengl-coordinate-systems-and-transforms-api-conventions.svg["Table comparing NDC z range, NDC y direction, window origin, handedness and depth-range call for OpenGL, OpenGL with glClipControl, Vulkan and Metal, Direct3D and WebGPU",width=720]

When a shader or a matrix library from another API is reused, three things change: the depth range \( 0 \) to \( 1 \) instead of
\( -1 \) to \( 1 \), the direction of NDC \( y \), and the window origin. The porting consequences are discussed in
xref:graphics-games/opengl/porting-to-vulkan-metal-webgpu.adoc[Porting to Vulkan, Metal and WebGPU]. The same pitfalls
exist when rendering into a texture in OpenGL and sampling it with image coordinates that start at the top: the image looks upside
down. The cure is to flip \( v \) or the projection, not to flip the data.

== Depth precision, glClipControl and reverse-Z

The depth value is not linear in distance. From the formula above, the NDC depth of a point at distance \( d \) behaves like
\( -1/d \): most of the depth range is used up close to the camera.

.Window depth against distance for standard and reverse-Z projections
image::graphics-games-opengl-coordinate-systems-and-transforms-depth-precision.svg["Graph with distance on a logarithmic axis and window depth from 0 to 1: the standard curve rises quickly from 0 to nearly 1, the reverse-Z curve falls from 1 to nearly 0",width=720]

With a near plane of 0.1 and a far plane of 100, a point only one metre away already has a window depth of about 0.90: the
remaining 99 metres share the last 10 percent of the range. A fixed-point 24-bit depth buffer then cannot tell apart surfaces a few
centimetres apart at distance, and the result is *z-fighting*.

The standard remedies are to push the near plane out (a near of 0.1 instead of 0.01 gives ten times more precision at distance),
to use a 32-bit floating-point depth buffer, and *reverse-Z*. Reverse-Z maps the near plane to depth 1 and the far plane to depth 0.
A floating-point number has the most precision close to zero, so this gives the far distances, where the standard mapping is worst,
the finest steps, and the two non-uniformities largely cancel. The recipe has four parts:

1. Use a depth-range \( [0, 1] \) clip space, selected with `glClipControl(GL_LOWER_LEFT, GL_ZERO_TO_ONE)` (OpenGL 4.5,
   `GL_ARB_clip_control`; `GL_EXT_clip_control` on OpenGL ES).
2. Build the projection with the near and far arguments swapped: `glm::perspectiveRH_ZO(fovy, aspect, far, near)`.
3. Clear the depth buffer to 0 and test with `GL_GREATER` (or `GL_GEQUAL`).
4. Use a floating-point depth attachment, `GL_DEPTH_COMPONENT32F`, in a framebuffer object; see
   xref:graphics-games/opengl/framebuffers-and-render-to-texture.adoc[Framebuffers and Render to Texture].

Reverse-Z does *not* help with the default \( -1 \) to \( 1 \) clip space. There the depth is first stored as a float in
\( [-1, 1] \), where the resolution is highest near zero (the middle of the range) and then mapped to \( [0, 1] \), which wastes the
benefit: step 1 is not optional. On macOS and on OpenGL ES without the extension `glClipControl` does not exist, and reverse-Z is
not available.

[source,cpp]
----
// Reverse-Z depth set-up for a context that exposes OpenGL 4.5 (check GLAD_GL_VERSION_4_5 first).
void setDepthMode(bool reverseZ) {
    if (reverseZ) {
        glClipControl(GL_LOWER_LEFT, GL_ZERO_TO_ONE);
        glClearDepth(0.0);
        glDepthFunc(GL_GREATER);
    } else {
        glClipControl(GL_LOWER_LEFT, GL_NEGATIVE_ONE_TO_ONE);
        glClearDepth(1.0);
        glDepthFunc(GL_LESS);
    }
}

glm::mat4 makeProjection(bool reverseZ, float fovy, float aspect, float zNear, float zFar) {
    return reverseZ ? glm::perspectiveRH_ZO(fovy, aspect, zFar, zNear)   // near -> 1, far -> 0
                    : glm::perspectiveRH_NO(fovy, aspect, zNear, zFar);  // near -> -1, far -> 1
}
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glClipControl.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: glClipControl].

With reverse-Z the far plane can also be pushed to infinity: the matrix then needs no far parameter and nothing at distance is
clipped by the far plane. The `glClipControl` call also accepts `GL_UPPER_LEFT`, which moves the window origin to the top left so
that an OpenGL renderer can match the Vulkan and Direct3D image orientation. Be aware that this can change which faces count as
front-facing, so check face culling and `glFrontFace` after switching the origin.

== Storyboard: one vertex of the book

.A Bookshelf book through the five visible stages
image::graphics-games-opengl-coordinate-systems-and-transforms-storyboard.svg["Five panels: the book at the origin in object space, on the shelf in world space, in front of the camera in view space, inside the NDC square, and as a pixel rectangle in window coordinates",width=720]

Follow one corner of the book through the numbered panels. In object space the corner is \( (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) \) of the unit cube.
\( M \) scales it to a 0.6 by 0.9 by 0.18 box and moves it onto the shelf. \( V \) re-expresses it relative to the camera, so a
corner 2.5 units in front of the camera has \( z = -2.5 \) in view space. \( P \) produces clip coordinates whose \( w \) is the
distance, 2.5. The divide gives NDC coordinates between \( -1 \) and \( 1 \), and the viewport transform multiplies by half the
framebuffer size to get pixels.

== A complete program: the rotating book box

This is Bookshelf GL step 3 without lighting (lighting comes in
xref:graphics-games/opengl/lighting-blinn-phong.adoc[Lighting: Blinn-Phong]): a box with the proportions of the book, rotating
about its vertical axis, seen by a perspective camera, with depth testing and back-face culling. Press `R` to toggle reverse-Z when
the context offers OpenGL 4.5. The vertex colour is derived from the position so that the faces are distinguishable without textures,
which come in xref:graphics-games/opengl/textures-and-samplers.adoc[Textures and Samplers].

[source,cmake]
----
cmake_minimum_required(VERSION 3.20)
project(bookshelf_gl_transforms CXX)
set(CMAKE_CXX_STANDARD 20)
set(CMAKE_CXX_STANDARD_REQUIRED ON)

find_package(glfw3 CONFIG REQUIRED)
find_package(glm CONFIG REQUIRED)
# glad2 generated with: --api gl:core=4.6  (the 4.5 entry points compile; the code checks at run time)
add_subdirectory(external/glad/cmake)
glad_add_library(glad REPRODUCIBLE API gl:core=4.6)

add_executable(transforms main.cpp)
target_link_libraries(transforms PRIVATE glfw glm::glm glad)
----

Source: https://www.glfw.org/docs/latest/build_guide.html[GLFW: Building applications] and https://gen.glad.sh/[glad2 generator].
The full project set-up is in xref:graphics-games/opengl/getting-started-desktop.adoc[Getting Started on the Desktop]; for CMake and
vcpkg see xref:programming-languages/cpp/build-and-tooling.adoc[{cpp} Build and Tooling].

[source,cpp]
----
// main.cpp: the Bookshelf book as a rotating box with a model-view-projection matrix.
#include <glad/gl.h>
#include <GLFW/glfw3.h>
#include <glm/glm.hpp>
#include <glm/gtc/matrix_transform.hpp>
#include <glm/gtc/type_ptr.hpp>

#include <array>
#include <cstdint>
#include <cstdio>
#include <cstdlib>

static const char* kVertexShader = R"GLSL(#version 410 core
layout(location = 0) in vec3 aPos;
uniform mat4 uMvp;
out vec3 vColor;
void main() {
    vColor = aPos + 0.5;              // unit cube corners map to 0..1: a colour per corner
    gl_Position = uMvp * vec4(aPos, 1.0);
}
)GLSL";

static const char* kFragmentShader = R"GLSL(#version 410 core
in vec3 vColor;
out vec4 fragColor;
void main() { fragColor = vec4(vColor, 1.0); }
)GLSL";

static GLuint makeShader(GLenum stage, const char* source) {
    GLuint s = glCreateShader(stage);
    glShaderSource(s, 1, &source, nullptr);
    glCompileShader(s);
    GLint ok = GL_FALSE;
    glGetShaderiv(s, GL_COMPILE_STATUS, &ok);
    if (!ok) {
        char log[1024];
        glGetShaderInfoLog(s, sizeof(log), nullptr, log);
        std::fprintf(stderr, "shader error: %s\n", log);
        std::exit(1);
    }
    return s;
}

static GLuint makeProgram() {
    GLuint vs = makeShader(GL_VERTEX_SHADER, kVertexShader);
    GLuint fs = makeShader(GL_FRAGMENT_SHADER, kFragmentShader);
    GLuint p = glCreateProgram();
    glAttachShader(p, vs);
    glAttachShader(p, fs);
    glLinkProgram(p);
    GLint ok = GL_FALSE;
    glGetProgramiv(p, GL_LINK_STATUS, &ok);
    if (!ok) {
        char log[1024];
        glGetProgramInfoLog(p, sizeof(log), nullptr, log);
        std::fprintf(stderr, "link error: %s\n", log);
        std::exit(1);
    }
    glDeleteShader(vs);
    glDeleteShader(fs);
    return p;
}

static void setDepthMode(bool reverseZ) {
    if (reverseZ) {
        glClipControl(GL_LOWER_LEFT, GL_ZERO_TO_ONE);
        glClearDepth(0.0);
        glDepthFunc(GL_GREATER);
    } else {
        glClipControl(GL_LOWER_LEFT, GL_NEGATIVE_ONE_TO_ONE);
        glClearDepth(1.0);
        glDepthFunc(GL_LESS);
    }
}

int main() {
    if (!glfwInit()) return 1;
    glfwWindowHint(GLFW_CONTEXT_VERSION_MAJOR, 4);
    glfwWindowHint(GLFW_CONTEXT_VERSION_MINOR, 1);
    glfwWindowHint(GLFW_OPENGL_PROFILE, GLFW_OPENGL_CORE_PROFILE);
    glfwWindowHint(GLFW_OPENGL_FORWARD_COMPAT, GLFW_TRUE);
    GLFWwindow* window = glfwCreateWindow(960, 600, "Bookshelf GL: transforms", nullptr, nullptr);
    if (!window) { glfwTerminate(); return 1; }
    glfwMakeContextCurrent(window);
    if (!gladLoadGL(glfwGetProcAddress)) return 1;
    glfwSwapInterval(1);

    const bool canClipControl = GLAD_GL_VERSION_4_5 != 0;
    bool reverseZ = false;
    bool rWasDown = false;

    // Unit cube, 8 corners, 12 triangles, counter-clockwise when seen from outside.
    const std::array<float, 24> corners = {
        -0.5f, -0.5f,  0.5f,   0.5f, -0.5f,  0.5f,   0.5f,  0.5f,  0.5f,  -0.5f,  0.5f,  0.5f,
        -0.5f, -0.5f, -0.5f,   0.5f, -0.5f, -0.5f,   0.5f,  0.5f, -0.5f,  -0.5f,  0.5f, -0.5f};
    const std::array<std::uint8_t, 36> indices = {
        0, 1, 2,  0, 2, 3,    // front  (+z)
        5, 4, 7,  5, 7, 6,    // back   (-z)
        1, 5, 6,  1, 6, 2,    // right  (+x)
        4, 0, 3,  4, 3, 7,    // left   (-x)
        3, 2, 6,  3, 6, 7,    // top    (+y)
        4, 5, 1,  4, 1, 0};   // bottom (-y)

    GLuint vao = 0, vbo = 0, ebo = 0;
    glGenVertexArrays(1, &vao);
    glGenBuffers(1, &vbo);
    glGenBuffers(1, &ebo);
    glBindVertexArray(vao);
    glBindBuffer(GL_ARRAY_BUFFER, vbo);
    glBufferData(GL_ARRAY_BUFFER, sizeof(corners), corners.data(), GL_STATIC_DRAW);
    glBindBuffer(GL_ELEMENT_ARRAY_BUFFER, ebo);
    glBufferData(GL_ELEMENT_ARRAY_BUFFER, sizeof(indices), indices.data(), GL_STATIC_DRAW);
    glVertexAttribPointer(0, 3, GL_FLOAT, GL_FALSE, 3 * sizeof(float), nullptr);
    glEnableVertexAttribArray(0);

    const GLuint program = makeProgram();
    const GLint mvpLocation = glGetUniformLocation(program, "uMvp");

    glEnable(GL_DEPTH_TEST);
    glEnable(GL_CULL_FACE);        // back faces are those with clockwise winding
    glFrontFace(GL_CCW);
    setDepthMode(false);

    while (!glfwWindowShouldClose(window)) {
        // Toggle reverse-Z on the rising edge of R.
        const bool rDown = glfwGetKey(window, GLFW_KEY_R) == GLFW_PRESS;
        if (rDown && !rWasDown) {
            if (canClipControl) {
                reverseZ = !reverseZ;
                setDepthMode(reverseZ);
                std::printf("reverse-Z %s\n", reverseZ ? "on" : "off");
            } else {
                std::printf("glClipControl needs OpenGL 4.5: not available here\n");
            }
        }
        rWasDown = rDown;

        int width = 0, height = 0;
        glfwGetFramebufferSize(window, &width, &height);
        if (width == 0 || height == 0) { glfwPollEvents(); continue; }   // minimised
        glViewport(0, 0, width, height);
        const float aspect = static_cast<float>(width) / static_cast<float>(height);

        const float t = static_cast<float>(glfwGetTime());
        glm::mat4 model(1.0f);
        model = glm::rotate(model, t, glm::vec3(0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f));
        model = glm::scale(model, glm::vec3(0.60f, 0.90f, 0.18f));

        const glm::mat4 view = glm::lookAt(glm::vec3(0.0f, 0.6f, 2.5f),   // eye
                                           glm::vec3(0.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f),   // target
                                           glm::vec3(0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f));  // up
        const float fovy = glm::radians(45.0f);
        const glm::mat4 proj = reverseZ ? glm::perspectiveRH_ZO(fovy, aspect, 50.0f, 0.1f)
                                        : glm::perspectiveRH_NO(fovy, aspect, 0.1f, 50.0f);
        const glm::mat4 mvp = proj * view * model;

        glClearColor(0.10f, 0.11f, 0.15f, 1.0f);
        glClear(GL_COLOR_BUFFER_BIT | GL_DEPTH_BUFFER_BIT);
        glUseProgram(program);
        glUniformMatrix4fv(mvpLocation, 1, GL_FALSE, glm::value_ptr(mvp));
        glBindVertexArray(vao);
        glDrawElements(GL_TRIANGLES, 36, GL_UNSIGNED_BYTE, nullptr);

        glfwSwapBuffers(window);
        glfwPollEvents();
    }
    glDeleteProgram(program);
    glfwDestroyWindow(window);
    glfwTerminate();
    return 0;
}
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glDrawElements.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: glDrawElements],
https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glFrontFace.xhtml[glFrontFace] and
https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glDepthFunc.xhtml[glDepthFunc].

Notes on the program:

* The depth-mode switch only changes the order of the depth test. Real precision gains need a float depth attachment, which the
  default framebuffer does not guarantee.
* A cube needs only 8 positions here because the colour is computed from the position; a lit cube needs 24 vertices, one per corner per
  face, since the normals differ (see xref:graphics-games/meshes-and-vertex-data.adoc[Meshes and Vertex Data]).
* If the window shows a black screen, the first checks are the winding order and culling (swap `GL_CCW` for `GL_CW`), the depth
  function and the clear value, then `glGetError` and the debug callback of
  xref:graphics-games/opengl/debugging-and-tools.adoc[Debugging and Tools].

=== Placing many books on the shelf

A later Bookshelf GL step lays the books along a shelf. Only the model matrices differ, so a loop is enough; the instanced version
that draws them in one call is in xref:graphics-games/opengl/instancing-and-indirect-drawing.adoc[Instancing and Indirect Drawing].

[source,cpp]
----
#include <glm/glm.hpp>
#include <glm/gtc/matrix_transform.hpp>
#include <vector>

// Nine books standing on a shelf board at height shelfY, left to right, with a little random lean.
std::vector<glm::mat4> shelfModels(float shelfY, int count = 9) {
    std::vector<glm::mat4> models;
    models.reserve(static_cast<size_t>(count));
    const float spacing = 0.22f;
    const float startX = -0.5f * spacing * static_cast<float>(count - 1);
    for (int i = 0; i < count; ++i) {
        const float lean = glm::radians(((i * 37) % 7 - 3) * 1.5f);   // deterministic variation
        glm::mat4 m(1.0f);
        m = glm::translate(m, glm::vec3(startX + spacing * static_cast<float>(i), shelfY + 0.45f, 0.0f));
        m = glm::rotate(m, lean, glm::vec3(0.0f, 0.0f, 1.0f));
        m = glm::scale(m, glm::vec3(0.18f, 0.90f, 0.60f));   // spine facing the viewer
        models.push_back(m);
    }
    return models;
}
----

Source: https://github.com/g-truc/glm[GLM: OpenGL Mathematics].

== Common mistakes

* *Wrong multiplication order.* `+model * view * proj+` instead of `+proj * view * model+` collapses the picture or flips it. In GLSL write
  `+uMvp * vec4(aPos, 1.0)+`, with the matrix on the left.
* *Transposing by accident.* Passing `GL_TRUE` for a GLM matrix, or building a matrix row by row into a `float[16]`. Symptom: a
  rotation looks like a shear, or the translation disappears.
* *`w` forgotten.* A direction (a normal, a light vector) has `w = 0` so the translation does not move it. A position has `w = 1`.
* *Using the window size for the viewport.* Wrong by a factor of two on retina and many Android displays; use the framebuffer size.
* *Aspect ratio not updated.* The book looks stretched after a resize because \( P \) still uses the old aspect. The cure is in
  xref:graphics-games/opengl/cameras-and-projection.adoc[Cameras and Projection].
* *Near plane at or below zero.* \( n = 0 \) makes the projection singular and \( n < 0 \) is invalid; use 0.1 or larger and raise it if
  z-fighting appears.
* *`GLM_FORCE_DEPTH_ZERO_TO_ONE` without `glClipControl`.* The macro changes `glm::perspective` to the 0 to 1 depth range. The default
  OpenGL clip volume is \( -1 \) to \( 1 \), so the near half of the depth range is clipped away.
* *Negative scale and culling.* A mirroring matrix reverses the winding, so the object disappears with back-face culling on. Flip
  `glFrontFace` for those draws.
* *Large worlds.* A 32-bit float has about 24 bits of significand: at 10 kilometres from the origin the spacing between values is about a
  millimetre, so geometry jitters. Render relative to the camera (subtract the camera position in double precision on the CPU) or keep
  the world small.

== Performance notes

* Multiply the three matrices once per object on the CPU and upload a single `mat4`, or upload \( P V \) once per frame and \( M \) per
  object, instead of multiplying three matrices per vertex in the shader. Lighting that needs separate matrices can use uniform
  blocks; see xref:graphics-games/opengl/uniforms-ubos-and-ssbos.adoc[Uniforms, UBOs and SSBOs].
* Per-frame data (camera matrices) belongs in one uniform buffer bound to all programs; per-object matrices belong in an instance
  buffer or a shader storage buffer when there are many objects.
* The perspective divide and clipping are free of charge on the GPU. What costs is overdraw, which a good front-to-back draw order and
  early depth testing reduce, as discussed in xref:graphics-games/performance-and-profiling.adoc[Performance and Profiling] and
  xref:graphics-games/opengl/performance.adoc[Performance].

== Where older guides are outdated

[cols="2,3,3",options="header"]
|===
|Older material |What it teaches |Current practice

|OpenGL SuperBible, 4th edition (2007), Chapter 4
|The matrix stack: `glMatrixMode`, `glLoadIdentity`, `glTranslatef`, `glRotatef`, `glPushMatrix`, `glPopMatrix`; `gluPerspective`,
`gluLookAt` and `glFrustum`.
|All of these were removed from the core profile in OpenGL 3.1 and 3.2 (2009) and are absent from core contexts on macOS. Build the
matrices with GLM and upload them as uniforms.

|OpenGL ES 2.0 Programming Guide (2008) and OpenGL ES 3.0 Programming Guide (2014), Chapter 8
|Transform maths implemented in the companion sample code with the books' own small matrix helpers and conventions.
|GLM, or `android.opengl.Matrix` on Android, both column-major; `GL_FALSE` for `transpose`.

|OpenGL ES 2 for Android (2013)
|`Matrix.frustumM`, `Matrix.setLookAtM`, `Matrix.multiplyMM` with a Java `float[16]` and manual viewport handling in
`onSurfaceChanged`.
|The same helper class still exists; use the framebuffer size and rebuild the projection on resize.

|Course notes that use `glOrtho` and `gluPerspective`
|Fixed-function projection set-up with `glMatrixMode(GL_PROJECTION)`.
|A matrix uniform computed on the CPU.

|Any guide that stops at depth range \( -1 \) to \( 1 \)
|The only clip-space convention.
|`glClipControl` (OpenGL 4.5, 2014) selects \( 0 \) to \( 1 \) and the window origin; reverse-Z with a float depth buffer is now the
recommended precision strategy where it is available.

== Recent changes

* OpenGL 4.5 (2014) added `glClipControl`, which OpenGL 4.6 (2017) keeps in core; it gives OpenGL the depth-range and origin
  conventions of the other APIs. On OpenGL ES the extension `GL_EXT_clip_control` offers the same.
* OpenGL 4.6 is the last desktop version; the specification revision is dated 5 May 2022.
* Current GLM releases take angles in radians by default; older ones need `GLM_FORCE_RADIANS`.
