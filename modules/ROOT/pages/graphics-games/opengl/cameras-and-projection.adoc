= Cameras and Projection in OpenGL
:description: Perspective and orthographic projection, the view frustum, glm::lookAt, keeping the aspect ratio correct when the window is resized, an orbit camera driven by the mouse, camera uniforms, picking rays and the Android Matrix helpers, with a complete C++20 program that orbits the Bookshelf shelf.
:keywords: OpenGL, camera, projection, perspective, orthographic, view frustum, lookAt, field of view, aspect ratio, orbit camera, GLM, near plane, far plane, resize, unProject, android.opengl.Matrix

include::partial$ai-disclaimer.adoc[]

OpenGL has no camera. There is a viewport, a clip-space cube and whatever matrices your vertex shader multiplies; the "camera" is a
pair of those matrices that your program builds: a *view matrix* that places the world in front of an eye, and a *projection
matrix* that squeezes a frustum into the clip-space cube. This page defines both, shows how to keep the picture correct when the
window changes shape, and builds the camera that Bookshelf GL keeps for the rest of the section: an orbit camera that circles the
shelf under mouse control.

The facts were checked in November 2026. The versions documented are OpenGL 4.6 core (the program runs on a 4.1 core context, the
macOS ceiling), OpenGL ES 3.0 to 3.2 for the Android snippet, GLM for the maths and GLFW 3 for the window and input. The meaning
of the individual spaces and the clip-space conventions are in
xref:graphics-games/opengl/coordinate-systems-and-transforms.adoc[Coordinate Systems and Transforms]; the API-independent maths is in
xref:graphics-games/coordinate-spaces-and-transforms.adoc[Coordinate Spaces and Transforms] and
xref:graphics-games/vectors-matrices-and-homogeneous-coordinates.adoc[Vectors, Matrices and Homogeneous Coordinates].

== The view frustum

A perspective camera sees a truncated pyramid, the *view frustum*, bounded by six planes: left, right, bottom, top, near and far.
Anything outside it is clipped away.

.The view frustum of a perspective camera
image::graphics-games-opengl-cameras-and-projection-frustum.svg["Side view of a view frustum: the eye on the left, the near plane, the far plane, the vertical field of view angle and the distances near and far measured from the eye",width=720]

Four numbers define a symmetric frustum:

* the *vertical field of view* \( \theta \) (`fovy`), typically 45 to 75 degrees for a game camera;
* the *aspect ratio* \( a \), the framebuffer width divided by its height;
* the *near distance* \( n \) and the *far distance* \( f \), both positive and measured from the eye along the viewing direction.

The horizontal field of view follows from them: \( \tan(\theta_h / 2) = a \tan(\theta / 2) \). A 45 degree vertical field of view on a 16:9
window is a horizontal field of view of about 74 degrees.

The corresponding matrix is built by `glm::perspective(fovy, aspect, n, f)` with the formula given on
xref:graphics-games/opengl/coordinate-systems-and-transforms.adoc[Coordinate Systems and Transforms]. Three practical points
decide whether the picture is correct:

. *The angle is the vertical one, in radians.* `+glm::perspective(45.0f, ...)+` instead of `glm::radians(45.0f)` asks for a field of
  view of 45 radians, which is nonsense.
. *Near distance greater than zero.* The projection is undefined for \( n = 0 \); and the smaller \( n \) is, the more the depth
  buffer's precision is squandered on the first few centimetres (see the depth discussion in the previous page). Start with 0.1 for
  a shelf-sized scene and raise it as far as the scene allows.
. *Far distance as small as the scene permits.* The ratio \( f / n \) controls depth precision, not \( f \) alone. A far plane at
  infinity is possible with `glm::infinitePerspective(fovy, aspect, n)`, which uses the limit of the matrix as \( f \to \infty \):

\[
P_\infty =
\begin\{pmatrix}
\frac\{1}{a \tan(\theta/2)} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \frac\{1}{\tan(\theta/2)} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & -1 & -2 n \\
0 & 0 & -1 & 0
\end\{pmatrix}
\]

== Orthographic projection

An orthographic projection keeps parallel lines parallel and makes the size of an object independent of its distance. It is the
projection of choice for 2D games and user interfaces, technical and isometric views, and the light's view in a shadow map. The
frustum degenerates into a box, and `glm::ortho(left, right, bottom, top, n, f)` builds it. Unlike the perspective projection the
near plane may be zero or even negative, because there is no division by distance.

A common 2D set-up maps one world unit to one pixel with the origin at the top left, matching window coordinates:

[source,cpp]
----
// Pixel-perfect 2D projection: x from 0 to width, y from 0 (top) to height (bottom), depth -1 to 1.
glm::mat4 pixelProjection(float widthPixels, float heightPixels) {
    return glm::ortho(0.0f, widthPixels, heightPixels, 0.0f, -1.0f, 1.0f);
}
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glViewport.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: glViewport]; the matrix is built by
https://github.com/g-truc/glm[GLM] `glm::ortho`.

To make an orthographic view show the same area as the perspective view at the point of interest, use the half-height
\( h = d \tan(\theta/2) \), where \( d \) is the distance from the camera to that point, and the half-width \( a h \).

== The view matrix and lookAt

The view matrix places the camera. If the camera has position \( \mathbf\{e} \) (eye), looks at a point \( \mathbf\{c} \) and has the
approximate up direction \( \mathbf\{u} \), the construction of `glm::lookAt(e, c, u)` is:

\[
\mathbf\{f} = \frac{\mathbf\{c} - \mathbf\{e}}{\lVert \mathbf\{c} - \mathbf\{e} \rVert}, \qquad
\mathbf\{s} = \frac{\mathbf\{f} \times \mathbf\{u}}{\lVert \mathbf\{f} \times \mathbf\{u} \rVert}, \qquad
\mathbf\{u}' = \mathbf\{s} \times \mathbf\{f}
\]

\[
V =
\begin\{pmatrix}
s_x & s_y & s_z & -\mathbf\{s} \cdot \mathbf\{e} \\
u'_x & u'_y & u'_z & -\mathbf\{u}' \cdot \mathbf\{e} \\
-f_x & -f_y & -f_z & \mathbf\{f} \cdot \mathbf\{e} \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end\{pmatrix}
\]

The three orthonormal vectors \( \mathbf\{s} \) (right), \( \mathbf\{u}' \) (true up) and \( -\mathbf\{f} \) (backwards) become the rows of the
rotation, so the camera looks along \( -Z \) in view space. The last column translates the world by \( -\mathbf\{e} \) first. Because
\( V \) is a rigid transform, it can be inverted cheaply: the inverse of \( V \) is the camera's world matrix, whose last column is
the eye position.

The cross product \( \mathbf\{f} \times \mathbf\{u} \) is zero when the viewing direction is parallel to the up vector, for
example a camera looking straight down at a flat shelf with \( \mathbf\{u} = (0, 1, 0) \). The result is a matrix full of NaN values and
an empty screen. Cameras that can look straight up or down must clamp their pitch just short of 90 degrees or choose another up
vector for that case.

== Keeping the aspect ratio correct

.The same vertical field of view in windows of different shape
image::graphics-games-opengl-cameras-and-projection-resize-aspect.svg["Three window shapes: 4 to 3, 16 to 9 and a tall portrait window, each with a book of the same height and a note on how the horizontal extent changes",width=720]

The projection matrix contains the aspect ratio. When the window is resized, `glViewport` and the projection must both be updated, or
the book looks stretched. The robust pattern is to read the *framebuffer* size every frame (or in
`glfwSetFramebufferSizeCallback`), set the viewport from it and derive the aspect from it. A minimised window reports a zero size,
which must be skipped to avoid a division by zero.

[source,cpp]
----
// Recompute viewport and aspect from the framebuffer size; returns false if the window is minimised.
bool updateViewport(GLFWwindow* window, float& aspect) {
    int width = 0, height = 0;
    glfwGetFramebufferSize(window, &width, &height);
    if (width == 0 || height == 0) return false;
    glViewport(0, 0, width, height);
    aspect = static_cast<float>(width) / static_cast<float>(height);
    return true;
}

// Alternative: keep a fixed design aspect (for example 16:9) with black bars.
void letterboxViewport(int fbWidth, int fbHeight, float designAspect) {
    int vpWidth = fbWidth;
    int vpHeight = static_cast<int>(static_cast<float>(fbWidth) / designAspect);
    if (vpHeight > fbHeight) {
        vpHeight = fbHeight;
        vpWidth = static_cast<int>(static_cast<float>(fbHeight) * designAspect);
    }
    glViewport((fbWidth - vpWidth) / 2, (fbHeight - vpHeight) / 2, vpWidth, vpHeight);
}
----

Source: https://www.glfw.org/docs/latest/window_guide.html#window_fbsize[GLFW: Framebuffer size] and
https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glViewport.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: glViewport].

The choices when the aspect ratio changes are summarised below.

[cols="2,3,3",options="header"]
|===
|Strategy |Behaviour |Used for

|Fix the vertical field of view ("Hor+")
|A wider window shows more to the sides; a narrower one shows less.
|Most 3D games and the program on this page.

|Fix the horizontal field of view ("Vert-")
|A wider window shows less above and below.
|Rare; some portrait-mode games.

|Fixed design aspect with bars
|The picture never changes; bars fill the rest, drawn by clearing and a restricted viewport (and `glScissor` for the clear).
|Pixel-art, UI-heavy and competitive games.

|Render at a fixed size, then scale
|The scene is drawn into an off-screen texture and stretched; see
xref:graphics-games/opengl/framebuffers-and-render-to-texture.adoc[Framebuffers and Render to Texture].
|Retro rendering, dynamic resolution.
|===

== The orbit camera

An orbit camera keeps a *target* point (here the middle of the shelf) and moves the eye on a sphere around it. It needs three
parameters: *yaw* (rotation about the world up axis), *pitch* (elevation above the horizontal plane) and *distance*.

.An orbit camera described by yaw, pitch and distance
image::graphics-games-opengl-cameras-and-projection-orbit-camera.svg["Diagram of a camera on a sphere around a target: the distance r, the pitch angle above the horizontal plane, the yaw circle, and the formulas that convert spherical coordinates to the eye position",width=720]

The eye position follows from the spherical coordinates

\[
\mathbf\{e} = \mathbf\{c} + r \begin\{pmatrix} \cos\varphi \sin\psi \\ \sin\varphi \\ \cos\varphi \cos\psi \end\{pmatrix}
\]

with pitch \( \varphi \), yaw \( \psi \) and distance \( r \), and the view matrix is `lookAt(e, c, up)`. Mouse dragging changes yaw and pitch,
the scroll wheel changes the distance. Three details separate a good orbit camera from a frustrating one:

* *Clamp the pitch* to about plus or minus 89 degrees. At exactly 90 degrees the forward vector is parallel to the up vector and
  `lookAt` fails as described above, and beyond it the image flips upside down.
* *Zoom multiplicatively* (`+distance *= 0.9^steps+`) so a scroll step feels the same close up and far away, and clamp the
  distance above the near plane.
* *Use relative mouse movement* between frames, and ignore the first frame after the button is pressed, otherwise the view jumps by
  the distance from the last position.

A free-fly (first-person) camera has the same structure with yaw and pitch applied to the *forward* vector and the eye as the
variable: \( \mathbf\{f} = (\cos\varphi \sin\psi, \sin\varphi, -\cos\varphi \cos\psi) \) and `lookAt(e, e + f, up)`. For general rotations,
without gimbal-lock problems when the camera must also roll, use a quaternion; see
xref:graphics-games/rotations-and-quaternions.adoc[Rotations and Quaternions].

== A complete program: orbiting the shelf

The program draws a shelf board and seven books (all the same cube mesh with different model matrices), lets the user orbit with
the left mouse button, zoom with the wheel, switch between perspective and orthographic with `P`, and resizes correctly. It runs
on an OpenGL 4.1 core context. The CMake file is the one from
xref:graphics-games/opengl/coordinate-systems-and-transforms.adoc[Coordinate Systems and Transforms] with the target renamed.

[source,cpp]
----
// main.cpp: Bookshelf GL, orbit camera around a shelf.
#include <glad/gl.h>
#include <GLFW/glfw3.h>
#include <glm/glm.hpp>
#include <glm/gtc/matrix_transform.hpp>
#include <glm/gtc/type_ptr.hpp>

#include <array>
#include <cmath>
#include <cstdint>
#include <cstdio>
#include <cstdlib>
#include <vector>

// ---------------------------------------------------------------- camera
struct OrbitCamera {
    glm::vec3 target{0.0f, 0.45f, 0.0f};
    float yaw = glm::radians(25.0f);
    float pitch = glm::radians(18.0f);
    float distance = 3.2f;
    float fovy = glm::radians(45.0f);
    float zNear = 0.1f;
    float zFar = 50.0f;
    bool orthographic = false;

    glm::vec3 eye() const {
        return target + distance * glm::vec3(std::cos(pitch) * std::sin(yaw),
                                              std::sin(pitch),
                                              std::cos(pitch) * std::cos(yaw));
    }
    glm::mat4 view() const { return glm::lookAt(eye(), target, glm::vec3(0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f)); }
    glm::mat4 projection(float aspect) const {
        if (orthographic) {
            // Same visible height at the target as the perspective camera.
            const float halfHeight = distance * std::tan(fovy * 0.5f);
            const float halfWidth = halfHeight * aspect;
            return glm::ortho(-halfWidth, halfWidth, -halfHeight, halfHeight, zNear, zFar);
        }
        return glm::perspective(fovy, aspect, zNear, zFar);
    }
    void rotate(float dxPixels, float dyPixels) {
        const float limit = glm::radians(89.0f);
        yaw -= dxPixels * 0.006f;
        pitch = glm::clamp(pitch + dyPixels * 0.006f, -limit, limit);
    }
    void zoom(float scrollSteps) {
        distance = glm::clamp(distance * std::pow(0.9f, scrollSteps), zNear * 4.0f, 30.0f);
    }
};

static void onScroll(GLFWwindow* window, double, double yOffset) {
    auto* camera = static_cast<OrbitCamera*>(glfwGetWindowUserPointer(window));
    camera->zoom(static_cast<float>(yOffset));
}

// ---------------------------------------------------------------- shaders
static const char* kVertex = R"GLSL(#version 410 core
layout(location = 0) in vec3 aPos;
uniform mat4 uMvp;
uniform vec3 uTint;
out vec3 vColor;
void main() {
    vColor = uTint * (0.55 + 0.45 * (aPos.y + 0.5));   // a simple vertical gradient
    gl_Position = uMvp * vec4(aPos, 1.0);
}
)GLSL";

static const char* kFragment = R"GLSL(#version 410 core
in vec3 vColor;
out vec4 fragColor;
void main() { fragColor = vec4(vColor, 1.0); }
)GLSL";

static GLuint compile(GLenum stage, const char* src) {
    GLuint s = glCreateShader(stage);
    glShaderSource(s, 1, &src, nullptr);
    glCompileShader(s);
    GLint ok = GL_FALSE;
    glGetShaderiv(s, GL_COMPILE_STATUS, &ok);
    if (!ok) {
        char log[1024];
        glGetShaderInfoLog(s, sizeof(log), nullptr, log);
        std::fprintf(stderr, "shader error: %s\n", log);
        std::exit(1);
    }
    return s;
}

// ---------------------------------------------------------------- main
int main() {
    if (!glfwInit()) return 1;
    glfwWindowHint(GLFW_CONTEXT_VERSION_MAJOR, 4);
    glfwWindowHint(GLFW_CONTEXT_VERSION_MINOR, 1);
    glfwWindowHint(GLFW_OPENGL_PROFILE, GLFW_OPENGL_CORE_PROFILE);
    glfwWindowHint(GLFW_OPENGL_FORWARD_COMPAT, GLFW_TRUE);
    GLFWwindow* window = glfwCreateWindow(1000, 640, "Bookshelf GL: orbit camera", nullptr, nullptr);
    if (!window) { glfwTerminate(); return 1; }
    glfwMakeContextCurrent(window);
    if (!gladLoadGL(glfwGetProcAddress)) return 1;
    glfwSwapInterval(1);

    OrbitCamera camera;
    glfwSetWindowUserPointer(window, &camera);
    glfwSetScrollCallback(window, onScroll);

    // Unit cube (8 corners, 36 indices, counter-clockwise from outside).
    const std::array<float, 24> corners = {
        -0.5f, -0.5f,  0.5f,   0.5f, -0.5f,  0.5f,   0.5f,  0.5f,  0.5f,  -0.5f,  0.5f,  0.5f,
        -0.5f, -0.5f, -0.5f,   0.5f, -0.5f, -0.5f,   0.5f,  0.5f, -0.5f,  -0.5f,  0.5f, -0.5f};
    const std::array<std::uint8_t, 36> indices = {
        0, 1, 2, 0, 2, 3,  5, 4, 7, 5, 7, 6,  1, 5, 6, 1, 6, 2,
        4, 0, 3, 4, 3, 7,  3, 2, 6, 3, 6, 7,  4, 5, 1, 4, 1, 0};
    GLuint vao = 0, vbo = 0, ebo = 0;
    glGenVertexArrays(1, &vao);
    glGenBuffers(1, &vbo);
    glGenBuffers(1, &ebo);
    glBindVertexArray(vao);
    glBindBuffer(GL_ARRAY_BUFFER, vbo);
    glBufferData(GL_ARRAY_BUFFER, sizeof(corners), corners.data(), GL_STATIC_DRAW);
    glBindBuffer(GL_ELEMENT_ARRAY_BUFFER, ebo);
    glBufferData(GL_ELEMENT_ARRAY_BUFFER, sizeof(indices), indices.data(), GL_STATIC_DRAW);
    glVertexAttribPointer(0, 3, GL_FLOAT, GL_FALSE, 3 * sizeof(float), nullptr);
    glEnableVertexAttribArray(0);

    GLuint program = glCreateProgram();
    GLuint vs = compile(GL_VERTEX_SHADER, kVertex);
    GLuint fs = compile(GL_FRAGMENT_SHADER, kFragment);
    glAttachShader(program, vs);
    glAttachShader(program, fs);
    glLinkProgram(program);
    glDeleteShader(vs);
    glDeleteShader(fs);
    const GLint mvpLoc = glGetUniformLocation(program, "uMvp");
    const GLint tintLoc = glGetUniformLocation(program, "uTint");

    // The scene: one board plus seven books standing on it.
    struct Item { glm::mat4 model; glm::vec3 tint; };
    std::vector<Item> scene;
    scene.push_back({glm::scale(glm::translate(glm::mat4(1.0f), glm::vec3(0.0f, -0.025f, 0.0f)),
                                glm::vec3(2.0f, 0.05f, 0.7f)),
                     glm::vec3(0.65f, 0.45f, 0.25f)});
    const glm::vec3 tints[7] = {{0.75f, 0.20f, 0.20f}, {0.20f, 0.45f, 0.75f}, {0.25f, 0.65f, 0.35f},
                                {0.85f, 0.65f, 0.15f}, {0.55f, 0.30f, 0.65f}, {0.20f, 0.65f, 0.65f},
                                {0.85f, 0.45f, 0.20f}};
    float x = -0.72f;
    for (int i = 0; i < 7; ++i) {
        const float thickness = 0.10f + 0.03f * static_cast<float>(i % 3);
        const float height = 0.62f + 0.05f * static_cast<float>((i * 5) % 4);
        glm::mat4 m = glm::translate(glm::mat4(1.0f), glm::vec3(x + thickness * 0.5f, height * 0.5f, 0.0f));
        m = glm::scale(m, glm::vec3(thickness, height, 0.45f));
        scene.push_back({m, tints[i]});
        x += thickness + 0.04f;
    }

    glEnable(GL_DEPTH_TEST);
    glEnable(GL_CULL_FACE);
    double lastX = 0.0, lastY = 0.0;
    bool dragging = false, pWasDown = false;

    while (!glfwWindowShouldClose(window)) {
        // Input: left-drag orbits, P toggles the projection.
        double mx = 0.0, my = 0.0;
        glfwGetCursorPos(window, &mx, &my);
        if (glfwGetMouseButton(window, GLFW_MOUSE_BUTTON_LEFT) == GLFW_PRESS) {
            if (dragging) camera.rotate(static_cast<float>(mx - lastX), static_cast<float>(my - lastY));
            dragging = true;
        } else {
            dragging = false;
        }
        lastX = mx;
        lastY = my;
        const bool pDown = glfwGetKey(window, GLFW_KEY_P) == GLFW_PRESS;
        if (pDown && !pWasDown) camera.orthographic = !camera.orthographic;
        pWasDown = pDown;

        // Viewport and aspect from the framebuffer size.
        int width = 0, height = 0;
        glfwGetFramebufferSize(window, &width, &height);
        if (width == 0 || height == 0) { glfwPollEvents(); continue; }
        glViewport(0, 0, width, height);
        const float aspect = static_cast<float>(width) / static_cast<float>(height);

        const glm::mat4 viewProj = camera.projection(aspect) * camera.view();

        glClearColor(0.10f, 0.11f, 0.15f, 1.0f);
        glClear(GL_COLOR_BUFFER_BIT | GL_DEPTH_BUFFER_BIT);
        glUseProgram(program);
        glBindVertexArray(vao);
        for (const Item& item : scene) {
            const glm::mat4 mvp = viewProj * item.model;
            glUniformMatrix4fv(mvpLoc, 1, GL_FALSE, glm::value_ptr(mvp));
            glUniform3fv(tintLoc, 1, glm::value_ptr(item.tint));
            glDrawElements(GL_TRIANGLES, 36, GL_UNSIGNED_BYTE, nullptr);
        }
        glfwSwapBuffers(window);
        glfwPollEvents();
    }
    glDeleteProgram(program);
    glfwDestroyWindow(window);
    glfwTerminate();
    return 0;
}
----

Source: https://www.glfw.org/docs/latest/input_guide.html[GLFW: Input guide] and
https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glUniform.xhtml[OpenGL 4 reference: glUniform].

== Camera data in the shader

Once there are several programs, the camera should not be a loose uniform in each. The usual structure is a *uniform block* that
holds the matrices and the eye position, filled once per frame and bound to the same binding point for every program. The block
layout rules are covered in xref:graphics-games/opengl/uniforms-ubos-and-ssbos.adoc[Uniforms, UBOs and SSBOs].

[source,glsl]
----
// GLSL 4.10 core (binding point assigned from C++ with glUniformBlockBinding).
layout(std140) uniform Camera {
    mat4 uView;
    mat4 uProj;
    mat4 uViewProj;
    vec4 uEyePosition;   // xyz = eye in world space, w unused
};
----

Source: https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL/specs/gl/GLSLangSpec.4.60.html#interface-blocks[GLSL 4.60: Interface blocks].

The eye position is needed by Blinn-Phong lighting and reflections
(xref:graphics-games/opengl/lighting-blinn-phong.adoc[Lighting: Blinn-Phong]); it is the translation column of the *inverse* of
the view matrix.

== Casting a ray through the mouse position

Picking a book with the mouse needs the inverse of the whole chain: a window position becomes a point on the near plane and a point
on the far plane, and the ray joins them. `glm::unProject` does the arithmetic; the only trap is the vertical flip, because the
cursor origin is the upper left corner and the OpenGL window origin is the lower left.

[source,cpp]
----
#include <glm/gtc/matrix_transform.hpp>
#include <utility>

// Returns the ray origin and a normalised direction in world space for a cursor position.
std::pair<glm::vec3, glm::vec3> pickRay(double cursorX, double cursorY, int fbWidth, int fbHeight,
                                        const glm::mat4& view, const glm::mat4& proj) {
    const glm::vec4 viewport(0.0f, 0.0f, static_cast<float>(fbWidth), static_cast<float>(fbHeight));
    const float x = static_cast<float>(cursorX);
    const float y = static_cast<float>(fbHeight) - static_cast<float>(cursorY);   // flip y
    const glm::vec3 nearPoint = glm::unProject(glm::vec3(x, y, 0.0f), view, proj, viewport);
    const glm::vec3 farPoint = glm::unProject(glm::vec3(x, y, 1.0f), view, proj, viewport);
    return {nearPoint, glm::normalize(farPoint - nearPoint)};
}
----

Source: https://github.com/g-truc/glm[GLM: unProject].

On a high-DPI display the cursor position is in screen coordinates while the framebuffer is in pixels, so multiply the cursor
position by the ratio of framebuffer to window size before calling the function. With reverse-Z the near and far depths are
swapped. An alternative that needs no ray-box maths is the ID-buffer technique of
xref:graphics-games/opengl/text-ui-and-input.adoc[Text, UI and Input].

== The same camera on Android

On Android the helper class `android.opengl.Matrix` provides column-major `float[16]` matrices that can be passed to
`glUniformMatrix4fv` with the `transpose` argument `false`. Its `perspectiveM` takes the vertical field of view in *degrees*.

[source,kotlin]
----
import android.opengl.GLES30
import android.opengl.GLSurfaceView
import android.opengl.Matrix
import javax.microedition.khronos.egl.EGLConfig
import javax.microedition.khronos.opengles.GL10

class ShelfRenderer : GLSurfaceView.Renderer {
    private val projection = FloatArray(16)
    private val view = FloatArray(16)
    private val viewProjection = FloatArray(16)
    private var yawDegrees = 25f
    private var pitchDegrees = 18f
    private var distance = 3.2f

    override fun onSurfaceCreated(unused: GL10?, config: EGLConfig?) {
        GLES30.glClearColor(0.10f, 0.11f, 0.15f, 1f)
        GLES30.glEnable(GLES30.GL_DEPTH_TEST)
    }

    override fun onSurfaceChanged(unused: GL10?, width: Int, height: Int) {
        GLES30.glViewport(0, 0, width, height)
        val aspect = width.toFloat() / height.toFloat()
        Matrix.perspectiveM(projection, 0, 45f, aspect, 0.1f, 50f)   // fovy in degrees
    }

    override fun onDrawFrame(unused: GL10?) {
        GLES30.glClear(GLES30.GL_COLOR_BUFFER_BIT or GLES30.GL_DEPTH_BUFFER_BIT)
        val yaw = Math.toRadians(yawDegrees.toDouble())
        val pitch = Math.toRadians(pitchDegrees.toDouble())
        val ex = (distance * Math.cos(pitch) * Math.sin(yaw)).toFloat()
        val ey = 0.45f + (distance * Math.sin(pitch)).toFloat()
        val ez = (distance * Math.cos(pitch) * Math.cos(yaw)).toFloat()
        Matrix.setLookAtM(view, 0, ex, ey, ez, 0f, 0.45f, 0f, 0f, 1f, 0f)
        Matrix.multiplyMM(viewProjection, 0, projection, 0, view, 0)
        // For each object: multiplyMM(mvp, 0, viewProjection, 0, model, 0), then
        // GLES30.glUniformMatrix4fv(mvpLocation, 1, false, mvp, 0)
    }
}
----

https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/graphics/opengl/projection[Android: Apply projection and camera views].

The renderer runs on the GL thread, not the UI thread, so touch input that rotates the camera must hand the new angles over safely;
that, together with the manifest and the lifecycle, is the subject of
xref:graphics-games/opengl/opengl-es-on-android.adoc[OpenGL ES on Android]. The UI integration and `GLSurfaceView` set-up are in
xref:apps/android/graphics-canvas-and-opengl-es.adoc[Graphics, Canvas and OpenGL ES]. Android's `perspectiveM` and `frustumM` use
the OpenGL depth range of \( -1 \) to \( 1 \).

== Common mistakes

* A stretched picture after a resize: the projection still has the old aspect ratio, or the viewport was not updated.
* An empty screen after pitching to the pole: `lookAt` with a forward vector parallel to up. Clamp the pitch.
* A camera that "moves the wrong way": mixing up the camera's world transform and the view matrix. The view matrix is the *inverse* of the
  camera placement; moving the camera right means translating the world left.
* Degrees where radians are expected (GLM) or radians where degrees are expected (`Matrix.perspectiveM` on Android).
* Near plane too small: z-fighting at distance. Far plane too large for the near plane: the same.
* Using window coordinates for the viewport on a high-DPI display, or the cursor position as if it were in pixels.
* Jumping when the mouse is first pressed because the previous position is stale; skip the delta on the first frame of a drag.
* Reading `uEyePosition` from the view matrix's last column: that column is the translation of the *view*, not the eye position.
  Invert the view matrix, or keep the eye in the camera object.

== Performance notes

* The camera matrices are computed once per frame on the CPU; the cost is negligible. Upload them once in a uniform block rather than once
  per object, and use per-object model matrices from an array or an instance buffer when there are many objects.
* Frustum culling (testing each object's bounding volume against the six planes and skipping the draw call) saves far more than any
  matrix optimisation. The planes can be taken directly from the rows of \( P V \). See
  xref:graphics-games/performance-and-profiling.adoc[Performance and Profiling] and
  xref:graphics-games/opengl/performance.adoc[Performance].
* A tight near/far range improves depth precision for free; adjusting them per frame to the scene's actual bounds is cheap if the bounds
  are known.

== Where older guides are outdated

[cols="2,3,3",options="header"]
|===
|Older material |What it teaches |Current practice

|OpenGL SuperBible, 4th edition (2007)
|`gluPerspective`, `gluLookAt`, `glOrtho` and `glFrustum` filling the projection and modelview matrix stacks, and a frame class in the
companion library.
|None of these exist in a core profile. Compute the matrices with GLM and send them as uniforms; GLU was never part of OpenGL itself and is
no longer maintained.

|OpenGL ES 2 for Android (2013)
|`Matrix.frustumM` and `Matrix.setLookAtM` in Eclipse-era Java samples.
|The same helper class is still current, usually with `perspectiveM` and Kotlin, inside an Android Studio project.

|Mobile and desktop guides that pre-date high-DPI screens
|The window size equals the framebuffer size.
|The framebuffer size in pixels is separate; use `glfwGetFramebufferSize` on the desktop and the width and height passed to
`onSurfaceChanged` on Android.
|===

== Recent changes

* OpenGL 4.5 (2014) and OpenGL 4.6 (2017) offer `glClipControl`, so a reversed or zero-to-one depth projection such as
  `glm::perspectiveRH_ZO` is available on desktop contexts that support it; see
  xref:graphics-games/opengl/coordinate-systems-and-transforms.adoc[Coordinate Systems and Transforms].
* GLFW 3.3 added `glfwGetWindowContentScale` for high-DPI displays; the framebuffer-size callback, not the window-size callback, is the
  place to update the viewport.
