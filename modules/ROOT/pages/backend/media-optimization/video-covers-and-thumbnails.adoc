= Video Covers and Thumbnails
:description: Generating cover images and thumbnails from video frames: seeking with -ss, extracting one frame, the FFmpeg thumbnail filter, scene detection, choosing a representative frame, sprite sheets for scrubbing previews, rotation metadata, BlurHash on the cover, and the managed cloud equivalents.
:keywords: video cover, video thumbnail, FFmpeg thumbnail filter, frame extraction, sprite sheet, scrubbing preview, poster image, scene detection, rotation metadata, BlurHash
:stem: latexmath

include::partial$ai-disclaimer.adoc[]

Baseline date: 2026-10-07. A video needs at least one still image: the *poster* shown before playback starts, a *cover*
in lists and grids, and often a strip of *sprites* for hover previews. All three come from frames of the video, produced
once, by a worker, after the upload. They are then handled as images, see
xref:backend/media-optimization/image-formats-and-encoding.adoc[image formats] and
xref:backend/media-optimization/responsive-images-and-thumbnails.adoc[responsive variants].

== Extracting one frame

The FFmpeg commands on this page were run against FFmpeg 9.0.2 (the `linuxserver/ffmpeg` Docker image), and the Node.js code
against Node.js 24 with sharp 0.35 and blurhash 2.0.5.

[source,bash]
----
# Fast and accurate: -ss before -i seeks to the previous keyframe, then decodes to the exact time.
ffmpeg -ss 00:00:05 -i input.mp4 -frames:v 1 -q:v 2 cover.jpg

# Slow: -ss after -i decodes and discards everything before the time. Rarely needed.
ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -ss 00:00:05 -frames:v 1 -q:v 2 cover.jpg
----

* `-ss` before `-i` is an input option. FFmpeg seeks to the nearest keyframe before the time and then decodes to the
  exact point, which is fast and accurate in current FFmpeg versions. Placing `-ss` after `-i` decodes everything
  before the time and discards it.
* `-frames:v 1` stops after one video frame.
* `-q:v 2` sets JPEG quality (lower is better, 2 to 5 is the useful range). For WebP or AVIF output, give the
  encoder's own quality options instead.
* Apply the rotation: FFmpeg autorotates by default when it re-encodes using the display matrix of the stream, and you
  can force the issue with `-autorotate` or check with ffprobe (`-show_entries stream_side_data=rotation`). Phones
  record landscape pixels and flag the video as rotated, so a cover that ignores this one is sideways. `-noautorotate`
  turns the behaviour off, which is only useful to reproduce the problem.
* Scale in the same command (`-vf scale=720:-2`) rather than producing a full-size frame and resizing it later.

References: https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg.html#Main-options[FFmpeg main options] and the
https://trac.ffmpeg.org/wiki/Seeking[FFmpeg seeking guide].

== Choosing a representative frame

A frame at a fixed time is often black (fade-in), blurry (motion) or a title card. Options, from cheapest to best:

. *Fixed fraction of the duration.* Take the frame at 10 percent of the video. Cheap, and avoids intros.
. *The thumbnail filter.* The https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#thumbnail[`thumbnail` filter] looks at a batch of
  consecutive frames (100 by default) and picks the one that is the most typical, which means the closest to the batch's
  colour histogram average. It avoids black frames and transitions most of the time.
+
[source,bash]
----
ffmpeg -ss 00:00:03 -i input.mp4 -vf "thumbnail=n=300,scale=720:-2" -frames:v 1 cover.jpg
----
. *Scene detection.* The `select` filter can pick frames where the content changes, using the `scene` score:
+
[source,bash]
----
ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -vf "select=gt(scene\,0.4),scale=480:-2" -fps_mode vfr scene_%03d.jpg
----
+
The backslash escapes the comma inside the filter expression. Scene frames are a good source of *candidate* covers.
. *Let the user choose.* Extract eight to twelve candidates, show them in the uploader, and store the choice. A
  user-chosen frame beats any heuristic for editorial content.
. *Analysis.* Score candidates for sharpness (variance of the Laplacian), faces and brightness in the worker, or use
  a managed analysis service such as Azure AI Video Indexer or a vision model.

Remember to skip frames at the very end of the video and to fall back to a fixed time when a clip is shorter than the
`thumbnail` batch.

== Sprite sheets for scrubbing previews

A sprite sheet is one image holding a grid of small frames sampled at regular intervals. A player shows the right tile
when the user hovers or drags on the progress bar, with one request for the whole strip.

[source,bash]
----
# One frame every 10 seconds, 160 px wide, laid out as a grid of 10 columns by 10 rows.
ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -vf "fps=1/10,scale=160:-2,tile=10x10" -frames:v 1 sprites.jpg
----

Use a second small file, usually a WebVTT track with `#xywh=` fragments (the format Video.js and Shaka Player
thumbnails plugins expect), to map time ranges to tiles. Cap the number of tiles, and split into several sheets for
very long videos.

Some services produce sprite sheets for you, for example the Google Cloud Transcoder API
(see xref:cloud/google-cloud/media-optimization.adoc[the Google Cloud page]).

== BlurHash on the cover

Treat the chosen cover like an uploaded image: downscale it to about 64 pixels, compute the BlurHash and store it next to
the cover metadata, see xref:backend/media-optimization/blurhash-algorithm.adoc[BlurHash]. The frontend shows the blurred
cover in the video card and as the poster while the poster image loads, see
xref:web/media-optimization/video-playback-and-streaming.adoc[video playback on the web].

== Worked example: a cover for the Bookshelf trailer

The running example is the trailer of the Bookshelf platform, a 20 second 1080p clip with a hard scene cut at 10 seconds (the
synthetic clip described in xref:backend/media-optimization/video-encoding-fundamentals.adoc[encoding fundamentals]). The
cover worker has to produce a poster, a BlurHash for it and a scrubbing strip, with no human in the loop unless the
uploader wants one:

[mermaid]
....
flowchart TD
    V["Video file"] --> D["ffprobe: duration, rotation"]
    D --> C["Extract N candidate frames, sampled in the middle of N equal slots"]
    C --> S["Score each: brightness and sharpness"]
    S --> F{"Brightness between 30 and 225?"}
    F -- no --> X["Discard: black or blown out"]
    F -- yes --> P["Pick the sharpest remaining candidate"]
    X --> P
    P --> E["Encode poster and cover sizes, see the image pages"]
    P --> H["BlurHash from a 64 px copy"]
    D --> SP["Sprite sheet and WebVTT"]
    E --> ST["Object storage and database row"]
    H --> ST
    SP --> ST
....

Extracting the candidates is one FFmpeg command. Sampling in the middle of each slot keeps the first frame, which is often a
fade from black, out of the set, and `fps=1/<slot>` yields one frame per slot:

[source,bash]
----
# 8 candidates from a 20 s video: slot = 2.5 s, first sample at 1.25 s, 480 px wide
ffmpeg -ss 1.25 -i trailer.mp4 -vf "fps=1/2.5,scale=480:-2" -frames:v 8 cands/cand_%02d.jpg
----

Scoring is a few lines with sharp. Sharpness is the variance of the Laplacian response of the grey image (the classic
focus measure), and brightness is the mean grey level. The `offset: 128` in the kernel keeps negative responses, because an
8 bit result would otherwise clip them to zero and halve the signal:

[source,javascript]
----
import sharp from 'sharp';

const LAPLACIAN = { width: 3, height: 3, kernel: [0, 1, 0, 1, -4, 1, 0, 1, 0], scale: 1, offset: 128 };

export async function score(file) {
  const grey = sharp(file).greyscale();
  const { channels: [tone] } = await grey.clone().stats();
  const { channels: [edges] } = await grey.clone().convolve(LAPLACIAN).stats();
  return { brightness: tone.mean, sharpness: edges.stdev ** 2 };
}

export async function pickCover(candidates) {                      // [{ file, time }]
  const scored = [];
  for (const c of candidates) scored.push({ ...c, ...(await score(c.file)) });
  const usable = scored.filter((c) => c.brightness > 30 && c.brightness < 225);   // not black, not blown out
  return (usable.length ? usable : scored).reduce((a, b) => (b.sharpness > a.sharpness ? b : a));
}
----

On the trailer the eight candidates print:

[source,text]
----
t=1.25 brightness=124.9 sharpness=14960.2
t=3.75 brightness=124.3 sharpness=15126.2
t=6.25 brightness=128.9 sharpness=13225.0
t=8.75 brightness=124.5 sharpness=15149.5
t=11.25 brightness=165.1 sharpness=7383.4
t=13.75 brightness=119.1 sharpness=7386.0
t=16.25 brightness=91.0 sharpness=6204.8
t=18.75 brightness=71.5 sharpness=6583.7
----

and the worker picks the frame at 8.75 seconds. The test pattern of the first half is full of edges and the fractal of the
second half is smooth, so the first half wins, which shows what the measure does and does not mean: it ranks candidates of
one video against each other and favours busy frames. It does not know what is a good composition, which is why the
candidate frames are also what an uploader gets to choose from. Compare sharpness only between frames of the same size, and
treat it as a tie-breaker after the brightness filter, not as the decision. The BlurHash of the picked frame (computed with
the function of the Node.js page, 64 pixel copy, 4 by 3 components) was `LXKn#e|,jX_gxbX3W-rxj[WCbZjb`.

=== What the other extraction methods returned

On the same trailer, every command of the sections above ran as written, and returned:

[cols="2,3", options="header"]
|===
| Command | Result

| `-ss` before `-i`, and `-ss` after `-i`, at 5 seconds
| Two byte-identical 1920 by 1080 JPEG files. The placement changes how FFmpeg gets there (seek and decode, or decode and
  discard), not the frame it returns, so the fast form is the right default.

| `thumbnail=n=300,scale=720:-2`, from 3 seconds
| One 720 by 406 JPEG. The height is 406 because `-2` rounds to an even number.

| `select=gt(scene\,0.4),scale=480:-2`
| Exactly one frame, 480 by 270, and its timestamp is 10 seconds: the scene cut. On footage with many cuts this yields many
  candidates, so cap the count.

| `thumbnail=n=300` from 19 seconds, with fewer than 300 frames left
| Succeeds and picks among the frames that exist, so no special case is needed for the end of a clip.
|===

== Sprite sheets in detail

The `tile` filter fills a grid row by row, so the frame with index stem:[i] sits at:

[stem]
++++
x = (i \bmod C)\,w, \qquad y = \left\lfloor \frac{i}{C} \right\rfloor h
++++

where stem:[C] is the number of columns and stem:[w] by stem:[h] is the size of one tile. The sheet always has the full
grid size: `fps=1/10,scale=160:-2,tile=10x10` on the 20 second trailer holds two frames and still wrote a 1600 by 900 image,
the unused tiles are padding. Compute the grid from the duration instead of fixing it, so a short clip does not ship a
mostly empty sheet:

[source,bash]
----
# one frame every 2 s from a 20 s video is 10 frames: a 5 by 2 grid, and the sheet is 800 by 180
ffmpeg -i trailer.mp4 -vf "fps=1/2,scale=160:-2,tile=5x2" -frames:v 1 sprites.jpg
----

The WebVTT file that maps time to tile is generated from the same numbers. The function below is the one used for the trailer,
and the three first cues it wrote are shown after it (the tile is 160 by 90 pixels):

[source,javascript]
----
export function spriteVtt(url, total, interval, columns, tileW, tileH) {
  const stamp = (s) => new Date(s * 1000).toISOString().slice(11, 23);          // HH:MM:SS.mmm
  const cues = ['WEBVTT', ''];
  for (let i = 0, t = 0; t < total; i++, t += interval) {
    const x = (i % columns) * tileW, y = Math.floor(i / columns) * tileH;
    cues.push(`${stamp(t)} --> ${stamp(Math.min(t + interval, total))}`, `${url}#xywh=${x},${y},${tileW},${tileH}`, '');
  }
  return cues.join('\n');
}

// spriteVtt('sprites.jpg', 20, 2, 5, 160, 90)
----

[source,text]
----
WEBVTT

00:00:00.000 --> 00:00:02.000
sprites.jpg#xywh=0,0,160,90

00:00:02.000 --> 00:00:04.000
sprites.jpg#xywh=160,0,160,90

00:00:04.000 --> 00:00:06.000
sprites.jpg#xywh=320,0,160,90
----

[mermaid]
....
sequenceDiagram
    participant U as Viewer
    participant P as Player
    participant C as CDN
    P->>C: GET sprites.vtt, once
    P->>C: GET sprites.jpg, once
    U->>P: hover at 7 s on the progress bar
    P->>P: find the cue that contains 7 s: 6 to 8 s, the fourth tile
    P->>P: show the 160 by 90 region at x=480, y=0 of the sheet
....

The cue for second 7 is the fourth one, `xywh=480,0,160,90`, which is why the player needs one request for the sheet and
no request per hover. A JPEG side is limited to 65535 pixels and a large sheet costs memory and decode time on the device, so a long video
needs several sheets. At one tile per 10 seconds, a 10 by 10 sheet covers about 17 minutes. Start a new sheet after that, with
the cue URL naming the sheet it belongs to.

== Rotation, measured

A phone holds the camera upright and records landscape pixels, so rotated video is the norm. The container stores the rotation
as display matrix side data and the decoder is expected to apply it. To see the effect, the trailer was remuxed with a
display rotation of 90 degrees (`ffmpeg -display_rotation 90 -i trailer.mp4 -c copy rotated.mp4`, an input option) and the
frame at 1 second extracted three ways:

[cols="2,1,2", options="header"]
|===
| Command | Size | Meaning

| `ffprobe -show_entries stream_side_data=rotation -show_entries stream=width,height rotated.mp4`
| 1920 by 1080, rotation 90
| The pixels are landscape and a flag says to turn them.

| `ffmpeg -ss 1 -i rotated.mp4 -frames:v 1 cover.jpg`
| 1080 by 1920
| The rotation was applied, which is what you want.

| `ffmpeg -noautorotate -ss 1 -i rotated.mp4 -frames:v 1 cover.jpg`
| 1920 by 1080
| The raw pixels: a sideways cover.

| `ffmpeg -ss 1 -i rotated.mp4 -vf scale=720:-2 -frames:v 1 cover.jpg`
| 720 by 1280
| The filters run after the rotation, so `scale=720:-2` fixes the width of the upright picture.
|===

Two consequences: apply size limits to the upright dimensions (read them from ffprobe and swap width and height when the
rotation is 90 or 270), and never re-apply the rotation in your own code after FFmpeg has done it.
