Baseline date: 2026-10-07. Users upload video in whatever format their device produced: HEVC in a MOV container from
an iPhone, H.264 in MP4 from an Android phone, VP9 in WebM from a screen recorder. A platform has to produce a set of
renditions that every device can play, at sizes that fit every connection. This page covers the building blocks.
The bitrate values in the ladder table below were copied from the
https://developer.apple.com/documentation/http-live-streaming/hls-authoring-specification-for-apple-devices[HLS Authoring Specification for Apple Devices]
as it stood on the baseline date.

== Codecs

[cols="1,2,2,2", options="header"]
|===
| Codec | Strengths | Weaknesses | Licensing and support

| H.264 / AVC
| Plays on practically every device and browser. Hardware decoders everywhere. Fast to encode.
| Needs about twice the bit rate of newer codecs for the same quality.
| Patent pool (MPEG LA, now Via LA). Streaming free content is generally not charged for, but check your case. Encoder: x264 (GPL).

| H.265 / HEVC
| Roughly 30 to 50 percent smaller than H.264 at the same quality. Native on Apple devices.
| Patchy browser support outside Safari. Several patent pools with unclear terms.
| Encoder: x265 (GPL, commercial licence available). Often shipped only to Apple clients.

| VP9
| Royalty-free, supported in Chrome, Firefox and Edge and Android. Often used in WebM.
| Slow encode, limited hardware support on Apple devices, weaker than AV1.
| Royalty-free. Encoder: libvpx.

| AV1
| Best compression of the four, royalty-free, supported in current major browsers and a growing number of hardware decoders.
| Slow to encode in software. Many older devices decode it in software only.
| Royalty-free (AOMedia). Encoders: SVT-AV1, libaom, rav1e.
|===

A common production set is H.264 for every device, plus HEVC for Apple clients, or AV1 for clients that report
support. Choose per audience, not per fashion. Check the baseline for the target clients on
https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Media/Guides/Formats/Video_codecs[the MDN video codec guide] before you drop
the H.264 fallback.

== Containers

MP4::
The universal container for H.264, HEVC and AV1 with AAC audio. A normal MP4 stores its index (`moov` atom) at the end
of the file, so a player cannot start until it has the whole file, unless it was written with the index first
(`faststart`, below).

WebM::
A Matroska subset for VP8, VP9 and AV1 with Vorbis or Opus audio. Plays in current desktop browsers and on Android. Check Safari and
iOS support for your target versions before relying on it.

Fragmented MP4 (fMP4) and CMAF::
The file is split into independently decodable fragments. HLS and DASH both accept fMP4 segments. CMAF is a profile of
fMP4 so that one set of segments serves both protocols, see
xref:backend/media-optimization/video-streaming.adoc[video streaming].

MPEG-TS::
The original HLS segment container. Still accepted, but fMP4 is preferred for new work.

== Rate control

CRF (constant rate factor)::
The encoder holds visual quality constant and lets the bit rate vary with the scene. Lower is better quality. Good
starting points: x264 around 21 to 23, x265 around 24 to 28, SVT-AV1 around 30 to 35. The scales differ per encoder,
so tune by eye and with VMAF, not by copying numbers from another codec. CRF is the right choice for a single file,
but its bit rate is unpredictable, which a streaming ladder cannot tolerate.

Capped CRF (constrained quality)::
CRF plus `-maxrate` and `-bufsize`. Quality is constant until a scene would exceed the cap. This is the usual choice
for adaptive streaming renditions.

VBR and two-pass::
Target an average bit rate. Two-pass analyses the whole video in the first pass and spends bits where they help most
in the second. It doubles encode time, and is useful when a hard size target matters.

CBR::
A constant bit rate wastes bits on easy scenes, so it is used for live streams and not for stored video.

QVBR::
AWS Elemental MediaConvert's quality-defined variable bit rate, see
xref:cloud/aws/media-optimization/mediaconvert-video-transcoding.adoc[MediaConvert]. It is the managed counterpart of capped CRF.

The relation between resolution, bit rate and quality is not linear. A rough heuristic is that bit rate for equal
quality grows with the number of pixels latexmath:[W \cdot H] and with the frame rate, and shrinks with codec efficiency, so
for a codec with efficiency factor latexmath:[e] (about 1 for H.264, around 0.5 to 0.7 for AV1 on typical content):

[stem]
++++
b \approx b_{ref} \cdot \frac{W\,H}{W_{ref}\,H_{ref}} \cdot \frac{f}{f_{ref}} \cdot e
++++

Treat this as a way to sanity check a ladder, not as a rule. The ladder table below shows the specification values, which
grow slower than the pixel count because lower rungs target worse connections.

== Presets, GOP and keyframes

* *Preset* trades encode time for compression. x264 and x265 use names from `ultrafast` to `placebo`, with `medium`
  as the default and `slow` or `slower` common for stored video. SVT-AV1 uses a numeric `-preset` from 0 (slowest) to 13
  (fastest).
* *GOP* (group of pictures) is the span between keyframes. A keyframe (IDR frame) can be decoded alone. Streaming
  renditions need a fixed keyframe interval, so that segment boundaries line up in every rendition and a player can
  switch between them. Apple's specification says key frames (IDRs) should be present every two seconds, and that video
  segments must start with an IDR frame.
* *Scene-cut detection* inserts extra keyframes at cuts. Disable it (`-sc_threshold 0` for x264) when you need strictly
  aligned keyframes.

== Audio

AAC-LC is the universal choice (128 kbit/s stereo is a common default). Opus is better at the same bit rate and is the
usual partner of WebM and WebRTC, with growing support in MP4. Apple's specification lists stereo AAC at 32 to 160
kbit/s. Normalise loudness during transcoding if the platform mixes content from different sources.

== FFmpeg commands

https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-codecs.html[FFmpeg] wraps all of these encoders. Check each flag against the
https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg.html[FFmpeg documentation] and the https://trac.ffmpeg.org/wiki/Encode/H.264[encoding wiki] for
your build, since options depend on how FFmpeg was compiled. The commands below were run against FFmpeg 6.1 with libx264, libx265, SVT-AV1, libvpx-vp9 and libopus.

[source,bash]
----
# H.264 for every device. Index at the front so playback can start early.
ffmpeg -i input.mov -c:v libx264 -preset slow -crf 22 -pix_fmt yuv420p \
  -c:a aac -b:a 128k -movflags +faststart output-h264.mp4
----

`-movflags +faststart` moves the `moov` atom to the beginning of the file on the second pass over the output, which is
what lets a browser start playing an MP4 served by plain HTTP before it has the whole file.

[source,bash]
----
# H.265 for Apple devices. The hvc1 tag is needed for QuickTime and Safari.
ffmpeg -i input.mov -c:v libx265 -preset medium -crf 26 -tag:v hvc1 -pix_fmt yuv420p \
  -c:a aac -b:a 128k -movflags +faststart output-hevc.mp4

# AV1 with SVT-AV1, in MP4.
ffmpeg -i input.mov -c:v libsvtav1 -preset 6 -crf 32 -pix_fmt yuv420p10le \
  -c:a libopus -b:a 96k output-av1.mp4

# VP9 in WebM, constant quality, single pass.
ffmpeg -i input.mov -c:v libvpx-vp9 -crf 32 -b:v 0 -row-mt 1 \
  -c:a libopus -b:a 96k output-vp9.webm
----

For capped CRF, and for renditions that must be switchable, add the cap and a fixed keyframe interval:

[source,bash]
----
ffmpeg -i input.mov -vf "scale=-2:720" -c:v libx264 -preset slow -crf 22 \
  -maxrate 3000k -bufsize 6000k -g 48 -keyint_min 48 -sc_threshold 0 \
  -c:a aac -b:a 128k -movflags +faststart 720p.mp4
----

Here `-g 48` at 24 fps is a keyframe every 2 seconds. `scale=-2:720` keeps the aspect ratio and makes the width even.
Always probe the input first (`ffprobe -v error -show_streams -of json input.mov`) to read the codec, frame rate,
rotation and colour range, so that the pipeline can reject files that are unreasonable, see
xref:backend/media-optimization/security-and-operations.adoc[security and operations].

== Bitrate ladder per device class

A bitrate ladder is the set of renditions offered to the player. The player starts with a middle rung and moves up or
down as bandwidth changes. The tables list the 16:9 variants from Apple's specification, with average bit rate in
kbit/s. Apple says the default variant should be the one with 2000 kbit/s average bit rate and that, for VOD content,
the peak bit rate should be no more than 200 percent of the average.

.H.264/AVC variants (kbit/s)

| Resolution | Video bit rate | Frame rate

| 416 x 234 | 145 | up to 30 fps
| 640 x 360 | 365 | up to 30 fps
| 768 x 432 | 730 | up to 30 fps
| 768 x 432 | 1100 | up to 30 fps
| 960 x 540 | 2000 | same as source
| 1280 x 720 | 3000 | same as source
| 1280 x 720 | 4500 | same as source
| 1920 x 1080 | 6000 | same as source
| 1920 x 1080 | 7800 | same as source
|===

image::media-abr-ladder.svg["Chart of average video bit rate against frame height for the H.264 and HEVC rungs in the Apple HLS Authoring Specification",width=800]

How to turn the tables into a ladder for a platform:

[cols="1,2,2", options="header"]
|===
| Device class | Rungs to offer | Notes

| Phones on mobile networks
| 360p to 720p, H.264 or HEVC
| Include a low rung (around 145 kbit/s) so playback survives poor coverage.

| Tablets and laptops
| 540p to 1080p
| Make the 2000 kbit/s H.264 rung the default, as the specification recommends.

| Large screens and TVs
| 1080p up to 2160p, HEVC or AV1 where supported
| Offer 4K only when the source has it. Never upscale.

| Audio only
| One AAC rung
| Apple's specification gives audio-only variants in the tens of kbit/s. Useful for background play and for very poor links.
|===

Values that are not in the specification (for example VP9 or AV1 rungs) are assumptions: start from the HEVC column,
lower by the codec's measured gain on your content, and verify with VMAF. A platform that does not want to design a
ladder by hand can let a service choose it per title, for example
xref:cloud/aws/media-optimization/mediaconvert-video-transcoding.adoc[MediaConvert Automated ABR].
