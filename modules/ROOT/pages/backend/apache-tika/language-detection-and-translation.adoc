== Why the intake service wants a language

The Bookshelf intake service receives reviews and catalogue descriptions from readers and publishers in several
languages. The language of a text is not a curiosity: it drives decisions further down the pipeline.

* *Search analysis.* A Solr or Elasticsearch field needs a language-specific analyzer (stemming, stop words); choosing it
  needs the language of the text. See the existing pages
  xref:database/solr/indexing-and-updates.adoc[Solr indexing and updates] for where the analyzer is chosen.
* *RAG and embeddings.* Chunks of a multilingual corpus are tagged with their language so retrieval can filter or
  re-rank by it, and so a monolingual embedding model is not fed text it was not trained on. See
  xref:ai/rag-systems/document-parsing.adoc[Document parsing for RAG].
* *Routing and review.* A French review can go to the French-speaking editor; text in an unexpected language can be
  flagged for a human.
* *Quality control.* A file that claims to be an English manuscript but whose extracted text is detected as another
  language, or as nothing at all, is a good candidate for an extraction problem (an encoding mistake or garbled OCR).
* *Translation.* You must know the source language before you ask a translator for a target one (see
  <<the-translator-api>>).

The other thing language is *not* is a property of the file format. A Word document can carry a language tag in its
styles, an HTML page can say `lang="es"` and a PDF can have a `/Lang` entry, but those are declarations made by the
author or the software that produced the file. They are frequently missing, frequently wrong (a template left in
English around Spanish text) and always a single value for a document that may contain several languages. Language
*detection* looks at the text itself. The two are complementary and a good intake pipeline stores both, as shown in
<<declared-versus-detected>>.

== N-gram language identification: the theory

This section is the idea behind every detector below. It is the content of chapter 7 of _Tika in Action_, whose
listings (`LanguageIdentifier`, `LanguageProfile`, `ProfilingHandler`) no longer exist, brought up to date.

=== Character n-grams and profiles

An _n-gram_ is a run of _n_ consecutive characters. The text `the cat` contains the trigrams `the`, `he ` (with the
space), `e c`, ` ca` and `cat`. Every language has characteristic n-grams: `th`, `the`, `ing` and `ion` are frequent in
English, `ción` and `que` in Spanish, `eau` and `ent ` in French, `sch` and `ung` in German, and whole scripts have their
own character ranges (Cyrillic, Hangul, Devanagari). Counting how often each n-gram occurs gives a _profile_, a sort of
fingerprint, of a language.

The classic method, from the 1994 paper by Cavnar and Trenkle that both the book and the 4.1.x documentation cite, builds
a ranked profile of the most frequent n-grams for each language from training text, builds the same profile for the
unknown text, and picks the language whose profile is nearest by an "out-of-place" distance (how far each n-gram has
moved in the ranking). It is simple, needs no dictionaries, works on any script and is robust to spelling errors, which
is why it became the standard first approach to the problem.

=== From rank distance to a trained classifier

Modern detectors keep the n-gram idea but replace the hand-built ranking by a trained statistical model. Instead of
comparing ranked lists, the detector turns the text into a numeric feature vector (how often each hashed n-gram
occurs), and a linear classifier trained on a large multilingual corpus turns the vector into one score per language.
The 4.1.x documentation describes this for the default detector: character n-grams, short complete words, word
bigrams and per-script counts are hashed into a fixed-size vector, normalised, and multiplied by a weight matrix with
one row per language.

The scores are raw logits. They have no absolute meaning on their own; what matters is how far the best language is
from the runner-up. This leads to the confidence definition that the 4.1.x documentation gives for the default detector:

[stem]
++++
\text{confidence} = \sigma(z_1 - z_2) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(z_1 - z_2)}}
++++

where \(z_1\) and \(z_2\) are the scores of the best and second-best languages (after groups of confusable
languages are merged). The value is close to 0.5 when two languages are almost equally likely and approaches 1 when
one clearly wins. Because it depends on a *difference*, the same threshold means the same thing for a one-line snippet
and for a whole document, and for a detector that knows 100 or 200 languages.

=== The pipeline in one picture

[mermaid]
....
flowchart LR
    text["Text: characters from a handler, a reader or a string"]
    pre["Preprocessing: truncate to 100,000 characters, strip URLs and emails, NFC normalise, skip marks, case fold"]
    feat["Feature extraction: character n-grams, short words, word bigrams, script counts"]
    hash["Hashing into a fixed vector: 32,768 buckets, L2 normalised"]
    model["Model: linear classifier with INT8 weights, one score per language"]
    merge["Merge confusable groups: best score minus runner-up"]
    res["LanguageResult: language, LanguageConfidence, raw score"]
    text --> pre --> feat --> hash --> model --> merge --> res
    res --> use{"isReasonablyCertain?"}
    use -- yes --> keep["Store the language and route by it"]
    use -- no --> flag["Store as unknown or flag for review"]
....

Source: https://tika.apache.org/docs/4.1.x/advanced/language-detection.html[Apache Tika 4.1.x, Language Detection] (the
stages, the 100,000-character truncation, the 32,768 buckets and the confidence definition are from this page; the
final decision box is the intake service's own policy, shown in <<routing-by-language>>).

=== What the theory predicts, and where it breaks

* *Short text is hard.* An n-gram model needs evidence. A single word, a name or a code has few n-grams and many of them
  occur in several languages. The 4.1.0 run below detects `Hola` as Tetum (`tet`, `LOW`) rather than Spanish, and the
  catalogue number `ISBN 978-84-376-0494-7` as Maltese (`mlt`, `LOW`). Both are "reasonable" guesses for a statistical
  model and both are wrong for a human; the confidence is the signal that tells you so.
* *Closely related languages are hard.* Spanish and Galician, Serbian and Croatian, Malay and Indonesian, Norwegian
  Bokmål and Danish differ in few n-grams. The CharSoup detector ships a list of confusable groups
  (`confusables.txt` inside the `tika-langdetect-charsoup` jar) and merges them for confidence purposes, so that a
  genuinely ambiguous pair gets a low confidence instead of a falsely sharp one.
* *Mixed text has no single answer.* A page that contains a French paragraph, a Spanish one and an English one has no
  one language. `detect` returns the best; `detectAll` returns the ranked list so you can see the mixture (an example
  is below).
* *Boilerplate pollutes the sample.* Menus, legal footers and cookie banners are written in the site's language, not the
  article's. Detect on the main text where you can.
* *Text that is not language.* Numbers, code, tables of ISBNs and lists of names produce unstable results.
* *Script is a strong, cheap signal.* A text in Hangul or Thai is almost never confused with a Latin-script language,
  which is why the model gives the classifier explicit per-script counts.

Detector discovery uses the service mechanism, so what is on the classpath decides what is found. With both detector

single-module case was not run), and with neither there is no implementation to load.

== The LanguageDetector SPI

`org.apache.tika.language.detect.LanguageDetector` is an abstract class in `tika-core`. A detector accumulates text and
then answers.

[cols="3,6",options="header"]
|===
|Member |Purpose

|`static LanguageDetector getDefaultLanguageDetector()` |The first detector found by service discovery. Not loaded yet.
|`static List<LanguageDetector> getLanguageDetectors()` |All detectors found on the classpath, in discovery order.
|`loadModels()` |Loads the model for every language the detector supports. Must be called once before use. Returns the detector, so it chains.
|`loadModels(Set<String> languages)` |Loads only the listed languages. This restricts the candidate set and can shrink memory for detectors that load a model per language.
|`hasModel(String language)` |Whether the detector has a model for a language code.
|`reset()` and `reset(ParseContext)` |Clears the accumulated text so the detector can be reused. Detectors are stateful; reset between documents.
|`addText(char[], int, int)` and `addText(CharSequence)` |Adds text to the buffer. Can be called repeatedly (incremental use).
|`hasEnoughText()` |`true` once enough text has been added for a reliable answer. A streaming caller can stop feeding then.
|`detect()` |The best match for the accumulated text, as a `LanguageResult`.
|`detectAll()` |All candidates, best first.
|`detect(CharSequence)` and `detectAll(String)` |Convenience: reset, add the text, detect.
|`setMixedLanguages(boolean)` and `isMixedLanguages()` |A hint that the text may mix languages.
|`setShortText(boolean)` and `isShortText()` |A hint that the text is short.
|`setPriors(Map<String, Float>)` |Language priors, where the implementation supports them (see the warning below).
|===

Two details matter in practice. First, `detect(CharSequence)` calls `reset()` for you, whereas `addText` followed by
`detect()` does not, so a long-lived detector shared between documents must be reset explicitly. Second, the detector
object is not thread safe: it holds the accumulated text. Create one detector per thread, or one per task, or guard it.
The models are loaded per detector instance and are the expensive part (the CharSoup model is a 6.4 MB file), so create
detectors once and reuse them for many documents rather than per file.

WARNING: The 4.1.x documentation lists `setPriors(Map)` as a tuning knob of `CharSoupLanguageDetector`. In the 4.1.0 run
for this page, `+CharSoupLanguageDetector.setPriors(...)+` threw `UnsupportedOperationException: Priors are not supported`.
The method exists because the base class declares it, not because the CharSoup implementation honours it. The tuning
that works for CharSoup is `setMaxLength(int)` (the size of the rolling text buffer) and the choice of `loadModels(Set)`.
Priors on `OpenNLPDetector` were not tested.

=== LanguageResult and LanguageConfidence

`LanguageResult` is what a detector returns.

[cols="3,6",options="header"]
|===
|Member |Meaning

|`getLanguage()` |The language code. For the bundled detectors these are ISO 639-3 codes (`eng`, `spa`, `fra`), apart from three orthography variants of the CharSoup model that carry a private-use subtag (`cdo-x-rom`, `hak-x-rom`, `be-x-old`). An empty string for no answer. tika-server `/language` converts these codes to two-letter ISO 639-1 codes (see below).
|`getConfidence()` |A coarse `LanguageConfidence`: `HIGH`, `MEDIUM`, `LOW` or `NONE`.
|`getRawScore()` |The detector's raw probability-like score for the language, between 0 and 1.
|`getConfidenceScore()` |A normalised score, derived from how far ahead of the runner-up this language is.
|`isReasonablyCertain()` |A convenience that is true for the confidence levels the library considers reliable. In the runs here it was `true` for `HIGH` and `false` for `LOW`.
|`isUnknown()` |`true` for the "no language" result (for example from empty text).
|`isLanguage(String)` |Compares with a code, using `LanguageNames` to treat macro-languages and variants as equal where appropriate.
|===

Two facts about the numbers. `getRawScore()` and `getConfidence()` are not the same quantity: in the French run below the
raw score is 0.998 and the confidence score is 0.886, while for the very short text `Hola, buenos días` the raw score
is 0.517 and the confidence is `LOW`. And scores are comparable only within one detector: the OpenNLP detector
reports much smaller raw scores for the same texts (see <<charsoup-versus-opennlp>>), so never apply one detector's
threshold to the other.

=== A first detection

[source,java]
----
import java.io.IOException;
import java.nio.charset.StandardCharsets;
import java.nio.file.Files;
import java.nio.file.Path;
import java.util.Locale;
import org.apache.tika.language.detect.LanguageDetector;
import org.apache.tika.language.detect.LanguageResult;

public class DetectText {

    public static void main(String[] args) throws IOException {
        LanguageDetector detector = LanguageDetector.getDefaultLanguageDetector().loadModels();
        System.out.println("detector: " + detector.getClass().getName());

        String french = Files.readString(Path.of("reseñas-fr.txt"), StandardCharsets.UTF_8);
        String english = Files.readString(Path.of("review-en.txt"), StandardCharsets.UTF_8);

        for (String text : new String[] {french, english, "Hola, buenos días", "ok"}) {
            LanguageResult result = detector.detect(text);
            System.out.printf(Locale.ROOT, "%-6s %-7s raw=%.3f score=%.3f certain=%s%n",
                    result.getLanguage(), result.getConfidence(), result.getRawScore(),
                    result.getConfidenceScore(), result.isReasonablyCertain());
        }
        for (LanguageResult r : detector.detectAll(french).subList(0, 3)) {
            System.out.println("candidate " + r);
        }
    }
}
----

Output (Tika 4.1.0):

[source,text]
----
detector: org.apache.tika.langdetect.charsoup.CharSoupLanguageDetector
fra    HIGH    raw=0.998 score=0.886 certain=true
eng    HIGH    raw=0.995 score=0.664 certain=true
spa    LOW     raw=0.517 score=0.254 certain=false
diq    LOW     raw=0.779 score=0.184 certain=false
candidate fra: HIGH (0.997937)
candidate wln: NONE (0.002063)
candidate cat: NONE (0.001633)
----

Notice what the last two lines of the first block say. The four-line French review and the English review are detected
with `HIGH` confidence. The three-word Spanish greeting is correct but `LOW`. And the two-letter text `ok` is detected as
Zazaki (`diq`) with a `raw` of 0.779 that looks impressive and is meaningless: the raw score of a short text is not a
reason to trust it. The decision belongs to `getConfidence()` or `isReasonablyCertain()`, not to the raw number. The
`candidate` lines show `detectAll`: the top candidate, then the runners-up with tiny scores. The `toString` of a result is
`language: CONFIDENCE (rawScore)`.

Source: https://github.com/apache/tika/blob/main/tika-example/src/main/java/org/apache/tika/example/LanguageDetectorExample.java[`LanguageDetectorExample` in `tika-example`]
and https://tika.apache.org/4.1.0/api/org/apache/tika/language/detect/LanguageDetector.html[`LanguageDetector`, Javadoc 4.1.0].

=== Mixed-language text with detectAll

`mixed.txt` holds one English, one Spanish and one French sentence. `detect` returns a single answer, and `detectAll`
shows how the evidence is spread.

[source,java]
----
LanguageDetector detector = new CharSoupLanguageDetector().loadModels();
String mixed = Files.readString(Path.of("mixed.txt"));

System.out.println("detect : " + detector.detect(mixed));
for (LanguageResult r : detector.detectAll(mixed).subList(0, 4)) {
    System.out.println("candidate " + r + " raw=" + r.getRawScore());
}
----

Output (Tika 4.1.0):

[source,text]
----
detect : fra: LOW (0.636762)
candidate fra: LOW (0.636762) raw=0.6367616
candidate eng: NONE (0.363238) raw=0.3632384
candidate spa: NONE (0.353880) raw=0.35388032
candidate glg: NONE (0.297766) raw=0.2977663
----

The text has three languages and the answer is "French, low confidence", with English and Spanish next in the list.
That is a correct reading of a mixed text: the detector is telling you that it is not a single-language text. The fourth
candidate, Galician, is the Spanish confusable showing up (Galician and Spanish share many n-grams). If your intake
service must handle mixed documents, split the text into paragraphs, detect each, and store a language per chunk (see
<<language-per-chunk>>), instead of forcing one language on the whole file. `setMixedLanguages(true)` exists as a hint on
the base class; its effect on CharSoup was not tested, and the safe approach for mixed text is to split it yourself.

Source: https://tika.apache.org/4.1.0/api/org/apache/tika/language/detect/LanguageDetector.html[`LanguageDetector`, Javadoc 4.1.0].

== Detecting from a parse: LanguageHandler and LanguageWriter

In the book (and for as long as `ProfilingHandler` existed) the idiom was "feed the language identifier from a SAX
handler". The 4.x equivalents are `LanguageHandler`, a content handler, and `LanguageWriter`, a `java.io.Writer`. Both
hand the characters straight to a `LanguageDetector` as they stream past, so no complete text is built in memory just
for language detection.

=== LanguageHandler: language as a by-product of the parse

`LanguageHandler` extends `WriteOutContentHandler`. Construct it with no argument (it uses the default detector and
loads its models), with a `LanguageDetector` you prepared, or with a `LanguageWriter`.

[source,java]
----
import java.io.IOException;
import java.nio.file.Path;
import org.apache.tika.exception.TikaException;
import org.apache.tika.io.TikaInputStream;
import org.apache.tika.language.detect.LanguageHandler;
import org.apache.tika.language.detect.LanguageResult;
import org.apache.tika.metadata.Metadata;
import org.apache.tika.parser.AutoDetectParser;
import org.apache.tika.parser.ParseContext;
import org.xml.sax.SAXException;

public class Handler {

    public static void main(String[] args) throws IOException, SAXException, TikaException {
        AutoDetectParser parser = new AutoDetectParser();
        LanguageHandler handler = new LanguageHandler();
        Metadata metadata = new Metadata();
        try (TikaInputStream in = TikaInputStream.get(Path.of("reviews-es.html"))) {
            parser.parse(in, handler, metadata, new ParseContext());
        }
        LanguageResult result = handler.getLanguage();
        System.out.println("detector : " + handler.getDetector().getClass().getSimpleName());
        System.out.println("language : " + result.getLanguage() + " " + result.getConfidence());
        System.out.println("declared : " + metadata.get("Content-Language"));
    }
}
----

Output (Tika 4.1.0):

[source,text]
----
detector : CharSoupLanguageDetector
language : spa HIGH
declared : es
----

The handler consumed the characters of the page as the HTML parser produced them, and then `getLanguage()` returned the
detected result. The `declared` line is the other source of language information: the HTML parser copied the
`lang="es"` attribute from the `<html>` element into the metadata key `Content-Language`. Note that the declared value
is the two-letter ISO 639-1 code (`es`) and the detected value is the three-letter ISO 639-3 code (`spa`); compare them
through `LanguageNames` or a mapping table, not with `equals`.

Because the handler is the only handler passed to the parse, the extracted text is not kept; you get the language and
the metadata but no text. If you need both the text and the language, either parse once into a text handler and detect
on the string, or tee the content to two handlers. The metadata filter (next section) is the cleaner way to get both in
one pass.

Source: https://tika.apache.org/4.1.0/api/org/apache/tika/language/detect/LanguageHandler.html[`LanguageHandler`, Javadoc 4.1.0].

=== LanguageWriter: language from any stream of characters

`LanguageWriter` is the writer-shaped version. It is useful when the characters do not come from a Tika parse at all (a
`Reader` over a database column, a message queue payload, a decoded stream).

[source,java]
----
import java.io.IOException;
import java.io.Reader;
import java.nio.charset.StandardCharsets;
import java.nio.file.Files;
import java.nio.file.Path;
import org.apache.tika.language.detect.LanguageDetector;
import org.apache.tika.language.detect.LanguageResult;
import org.apache.tika.language.detect.LanguageWriter;

public class StreamingDetect {

    public static void main(String[] args) throws IOException {
        LanguageDetector detector = LanguageDetector.getDefaultLanguageDetector().loadModels();
        try (LanguageWriter writer = new LanguageWriter(detector);
                Reader reader = Files.newBufferedReader(Path.of("reseñas-fr.txt"), StandardCharsets.UTF_8)) {
            char[] buffer = new char[64];
            int read;
            int chunks = 0;
            while ((read = reader.read(buffer)) != -1) {
                writer.write(buffer, 0, read);
                chunks++;
                if (detector.hasEnoughText()) {
                    break;
                }
            }
            LanguageResult result = writer.getLanguage();
            System.out.println("chunks=" + chunks + " enough=" + detector.hasEnoughText()
                    + " -> " + result.getLanguage() + " " + result.getConfidence());
        }
    }
}
----

Output (Tika 4.1.0):

[source,text]
----
chunks=6 enough=false -> fra HIGH
----

The loop stops early if `hasEnoughText()` becomes true. For CharSoup that threshold is 10,000 buffered characters (from
the 4.1.x documentation), so this small file is read to the end (`enough=false`) and the answer is still `HIGH`: enough
text for a confident answer is much less than the early-stop threshold. On a large document the early stop means the
reader does not need to consume the whole file, which is the point of the incremental API. The detector also truncates
its input at 100,000 characters regardless, so a gigabyte-long text does not cost a gigabyte of work.

Source: https://tika.apache.org/4.1.0/api/org/apache/tika/language/detect/LanguageWriter.html[`LanguageWriter`, Javadoc 4.1.0].

== CharSoup: the recommended detector

`org.apache.tika.langdetect.charsoup.CharSoupLanguageDetector` (module `tika-langdetect-charsoup`) is the detector the
4.1.x documentation recommends for new deployments. Everything below, apart from what is marked as observed, is from the
4.1.x page "Language Detection" and the CharSoup supported-languages page.

[cols="3,6",options="header"]
|===
|Property |Value

|Languages |204, ISO 639-3 codes (apart from `cdo-x-rom`, `hak-x-rom` and `be-x-old`). The full list is on the "CharSoup Supported Languages" page. Observed: `CharSoupLanguageDetector.getSupportedLanguages()` returned 204 entries.
|Training data |Wikipedia dumps, supplemented by the MADLAD-400 corpus where Wikipedia coverage is thin.
|Model |One model for all text lengths: `langdetect-20260320.bin` inside `tika-langdetect-charsoup-core`, about 6.4 MB, 32,768 hash buckets, INT8 quantised weights.
|Runtime dependencies |None beyond `tika-core`.
|Preprocessing |Truncate to 100,000 characters, strip URLs and emails, NFC normalise, skip transparent characters, case fold.
|Transparent characters |Unicode nonspacing marks (Arabic harakat, Hebrew niqqud), the Arabic tatweel, and the zero-width joiners, so that Arabic, Hebrew and Persian words are not broken into fragments.
|Features |Character bigrams, trigrams and 4-grams with a word-position salt, short complete words, word bigrams, CJK and kana unigrams, per-script counts.
|Confidence |`sigmoid(top - second)` of the raw logits after confusable groups are collapsed; the per-language raw score is `sigmoid(logit - best_competitor_logit)`.
|Early stop |`hasEnoughText()` is true at 10,000 buffered characters.
|Friendly name |`charsoup-language-detector` (a `@TikaComponent`, so it resolves by name in the JSON configuration).
|Tuning knobs |`setMaxLength(int)` (rolling buffer cap). `setPriors(Map)` is documented but threw `UnsupportedOperationException` in 4.1.0 (see the warning above). Also `getDistributionEntropy()`, `compareLanguageSignal(Map)` and the static `topShortTextLanguages(String, int)`, whose uses are not documented.
|===

=== Strengths and limits

CharSoup's strengths are the breadth (204 languages from one 6.4 MB file), the absence of dependencies, the handling of
right-to-left and joining scripts, and a confidence that means the same thing for short and long text. Its limits are
those of every n-gram detector: very short strings are guesses (the `Hola` run above), closely related languages
overlap (mitigated by the confusable groups, not removed), and the model knows exactly its 204 languages. A text in a
language outside that set is assigned to the nearest known one, usually with a low confidence. The detector also does not
detect *dialects* (Brazilian versus European Portuguese is the same `por`).

The model is trained from Wikipedia and MADLAD-400, which means formal and web register is better covered than slang,
chat abbreviations or historical spelling. Test it on a sample of your own documents (the labelled sample in
<<evaluating-on-your-own-documents>>) before relying on it for anything that matters.

=== Restricting the candidate languages

If the Bookshelf catalogue only ever contains English, Spanish and French, you can tell the detector to consider only
those, which both speeds up the scoring and removes a whole class of absurd answers (Tetum for `Hola`).

[source,java]
----
LanguageDetector restricted = new CharSoupLanguageDetector()
        .loadModels(Set.of("eng", "spa", "fra"));

System.out.println(restricted.detect("Hola"));
----

This fragment was not run for this page. Whether and how `loadModels(Set)` restricts the candidates for the single-matrix CharSoup
model (as opposed to detectors with a model per language) is not described in the 4.1.x documentation, so test the
behaviour on your own samples before depending on it, and prefer the explicit post-filter shown in
<<routing-by-language>> (accept only a known set of languages with sufficient confidence), which behaves the same way
with every detector.

[#charsoup-versus-opennlp]
== CharSoup versus OpenNLP

`org.apache.tika.langdetect.opennlp.OpenNLPDetector` (module `tika-langdetect-opennlp`) wraps the language-detection
model of Apache OpenNLP. The model inside the 4.1.0 jar is dated 2021-04-13 (`opennlp-langdetect-20210413.bin`). The
CharSoup model is dated 2026-03-20. The observed comparison on the Bookshelf samples (Tika 4.1.0, both detectors in the
same JVM, one warm-up not performed so the times are only indicative):

[source,java]
----
List<LanguageDetector> detectors = List.of(
        new CharSoupLanguageDetector().loadModels(),
        new OpenNLPDetector().loadModels());

for (String file : List.of("reviews-es.html", "reseñas-fr.txt", "review-en.txt", "mixed.txt")) {
    String text = Files.readString(Path.of(file));
    if (file.endsWith(".html")) {
        text = text.replaceAll("<[^>]+>", " ");
    }
    for (LanguageDetector d : detectors) {
        LanguageResult r = d.detect(text);
        System.out.printf(Locale.ROOT, "%-16s %-24s %s %-6s raw=%.3f%n",
                file, d.getClass().getSimpleName(), r.getLanguage(), r.getConfidence(), r.getRawScore());
    }
}
----

reseñas-fr.txt   OpenNLPDetector          fra NONE   raw=0.179

review-en.txt    OpenNLPDetector          eng NONE   raw=0.047

[cols="3,4,4",options="header"]
|===
|Aspect |CharSoup |OpenNLP

|Module |`tika-langdetect-charsoup` |`tika-langdetect-opennlp`
|Languages |204 (observed via `getSupportedLanguages()`) |148 (observed: the array returned by `getSupportedLanguages()`)
|Model date |2026-03-20 |2021-04-13
|Dependencies |None beyond `tika-core` |Apache OpenNLP
|Position in service discovery |First (the default) |Second
|Confidence on these samples |`HIGH` for every clear single-language text |Raw scores 0.05 to 0.27 and `NONE` or `LOW` even when the language is right
|Short text |Wrong more often than it looks but flagged `LOW` or `MEDIUM` |`Hola` and `Bonjour tout le monde` as Malagasy (`mlg`), flagged `NONE`

Both detectors returned the correct language on every clear sample. The difference is in how sure each one is. The
OpenNLP detector's scores are low even on correct answers, which means that, with the confidence mapping used by Tika,
you cannot use `isReasonablyCertain()` as a gate with it on texts of this size: everything would be rejected. That is a
property of how the two models and Tika's score mapping behave on these samples, not a statement about the quality of
OpenNLP in general. The practical conclusions are: use CharSoup unless you have a reason not to; never mix their
scores; and, if you run both as a second opinion, compare the *languages* they return, not their confidences.

Source: https://tika.apache.org/4.1.0/api/org/apache/tika/langdetect/opennlp/OpenNLPDetector.html[`OpenNLPDetector`, Javadoc 4.1.0] and
https://tika.apache.org/4.1.0/api/org/apache/tika/langdetect/charsoup/CharSoupLanguageDetector.html[`CharSoupLanguageDetector`, Javadoc 4.1.0].

=== The other detector modules

The 4.1.x language-detection page documents two implementations. The earlier detector modules are not in that page, but
they were still being published at the time of writing: Maven Central lists `tika-langdetect-optimaize`,
`tika-langdetect-lingo24` and `tika-langdetect-mitll-text` at version 4.1.0 (the `maven-metadata.xml` of each says the
release is 4.1.0). The Optimaize detector (a Java port of a well-known n-gram detector) was the usual choice in the 2.x
and 3.x lines. None of these three modules was loaded or tested for this page, and the 4.x documentation does not describe them, so
treat them as legacy: they are not part of the 4.x recommendation, which is CharSoup (default) or OpenNLP. If you are
upgrading from a 3.x deployment that selected Optimaize, plan to move to CharSoup and to re-validate thresholds, because
the confidence numbers are not comparable.

== Language in the metadata pipeline: charsoup-metadata-filter

The `LanguageHandler` approach has a limitation: it takes over the content handler. The 4.x answer is a *metadata
filter*: a component that runs after the parse and reads the extracted content that is already in the metadata. The
filter detects the language of the text and writes the result back as metadata keys, so the content is still available.

The `tika-langdetect-charsoup` module provides `CharSoupMetadataFilter` under the friendly name
`charsoup-metadata-filter`, and the OpenNLP module provides `OpenNLPMetadataFilter` as `open-nlp-metadata-filter`.
Metadata filters are configured in `tika-config.json` under the top-level `metadata-filters` array and applied through
`TikaLoader`. The configuration below keeps only the keys the Bookshelf indexer needs, plus the three language keys.

[source,json]
----
{
  "metadata-filters": [
    "charsoup-metadata-filter",
    {
      "include-field-metadata-filter": {
        "include": [
          "tk:content",
          "Content-Type",
          "dc:title",
          "tk:detected-language",
          "tk:detected-language-confidence",
          "tk:detected-language-confidence-raw"
        ]
      }
    }
  ]
}
----

The filter has to run before the include filter, because the include filter drops everything it does not list, and the
language keys do not exist until the language filter has run. The order of the array is the order of execution.

[source,java]
----
import java.nio.file.Path;
import java.util.List;
import org.apache.tika.config.loader.TikaLoader;
import org.apache.tika.io.TikaInputStream;
import org.apache.tika.metadata.Metadata;
import org.apache.tika.parser.ParseContext;
import org.apache.tika.parser.RecursiveParserWrapper;
import org.apache.tika.sax.BasicContentHandlerFactory;
import org.apache.tika.sax.RecursiveParserWrapperHandler;

public class FilterDemo {

    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        TikaLoader loader = TikaLoader.load(Path.of("tika-config.json"));
        RecursiveParserWrapper wrapper = new RecursiveParserWrapper(loader.loadAutoDetectParser());
        RecursiveParserWrapperHandler handler = new RecursiveParserWrapperHandler(
                new BasicContentHandlerFactory(BasicContentHandlerFactory.HANDLER_TYPE.TEXT, -1));
        try (TikaInputStream in = TikaInputStream.get(Path.of("reviews-es.html"))) {
            wrapper.parse(in, handler, new Metadata(), new ParseContext());
        }
        List<Metadata> list = handler.getMetadataList();
        loader.loadMetadataFilters().filter(list);
        for (Metadata m : list) {
            for (String name : m.names()) {
                String v = m.get(name);
                System.out.println(name + " = " + (v.length() > 60 ? v.substring(0, 60).replace('\n', ' ') + "..." : v));
            }
        }
    }
}
----

Output (Tika 4.1.0):

[source,text]
----
tk:content =             Reseñas de lectores   Reseñas de lectores  El gu...
dc:title = Reseñas de lectores
tk:detected-language = spa
tk:detected-language-confidence-raw = 0.9610374569892883
Content-Type = text/html; charset=UTF-8
tk:detected-language-confidence = HIGH
----

The key names are verified: they are the constants `TikaCoreProperties.TIKA_DETECTED_LANGUAGE`,
`TIKA_DETECTED_LANGUAGE_CONFIDENCE` and `TIKA_DETECTED_LANGUAGE_CONFIDENCE_RAW`, written in the 4.x `tk:` namespace. The 4.x
migration material says metadata keys moved from the `X-TIKA:` prefix to `tk:`, so a 3.x deployment will have stored
these as `X-TIKA:detected-language` and so on (3.x was not run for this page to confirm the exact 3.x key names); the
`LegacyKeyMigrationFilter` mentioned in
xref:backend/apache-tika/metadata.adoc[Metadata] can restore the 3.x names for downstream consumers that were not
updated. The key `tk:detected-language-confidence` is the coarse `HIGH`/`MEDIUM`/`LOW`/`NONE`, and
`tk:detected-language-confidence-raw` is the raw score. The filter processes the extracted content, and the filter class
contains the message "no content to detect language from; filter is a no-op for this document", so a document without
text simply gets no language keys rather than a made-up one.

Because the filter works on the content of each `Metadata` in the list, it applies to every embedded document too when
you use the recursive wrapper. In the `publisher-email.eml` and `samples.zip` walkthrough of
xref:backend/apache-tika/embedded-documents-and-recursive-parsing.adoc[Embedded Documents & Recursive Parsing], each
embedded file therefore gets its own language, which is what you want for a zip that contains a French review, a Spanish
review and an English manuscript.

Source: https://tika.apache.org/docs/4.1.x/configuration/metadata-filters.html[Apache Tika 4.1.x, Metadata filters],
https://tika.apache.org/4.1.0/api/org/apache/tika/langdetect/charsoup/CharSoupMetadataFilter.html[`CharSoupMetadataFilter`, Javadoc 4.1.0]
and https://tika.apache.org/docs/4.1.x/migration-to-4x/metadata-changes-4x.html[Apache Tika 4.1.x, Metadata changes in 4.x].

[#declared-versus-detected]
== Declared versus detected language

The Handler example already showed both sources: `Content-Language = es` from the HTML and `spa` from the detector. The
metadata the parsers produce for the declared side varies by format (an HTML `lang` attribute or `Content-Language` meta
tag, the `dc:language` of an EPUB, a Word language tag, a PDF `/Lang`), so the intake service should store both in
separate fields and compare them:

[cols="3,5",options="header"]
|===
|Situation |What to do

|Declared and detected agree |Store the detected value with its confidence; high trust.
|Declared is missing |Use the detected value if its confidence is `HIGH`; mark the source as "detected".
|Declared and detected disagree, detection is `HIGH` |Trust the detection, log the disagreement (templates left in English around Spanish text are common). Keep the declared value for audit.
|Declared and detected disagree, detection is `LOW` or `NONE` |Keep the declared value if present, flag for review. A short text is not evidence against a declaration.
|Detection is `NONE` on a long text |Suspect the extraction (an encoding error, an OCR failure, a scanned page with no text layer), not the language.
|===

The comparison needs a normalisation step because the vocabularies differ: `es` against `spa`, `fr` against `fra`. The
`LanguageNames` class in `tika-core` (`normalizeName`, `equals`, `isMacroLanguage`, `getMacroLanguage`) provides the
helpers Tika itself uses; a small explicit map for the handful of languages your service accepts is also perfectly
reasonable.

[#routing-by-language]
== Routing by language

The decision box of the pipeline figure, in code: accept a language only when the detector is confident *and* the
language is one the service handles; otherwise fall back to a neutral treatment.

[source,java]
----
import java.util.Locale;
import java.util.Map;
import org.apache.tika.language.detect.LanguageDetector;
import org.apache.tika.language.detect.LanguageResult;

public class Route {

    private static final Map<String, String> ANALYZERS =
            Map.of("eng", "english", "spa", "spanish", "fra", "french");

    public static String analyzerFor(LanguageDetector detector, String text) {
        LanguageResult result = detector.detect(text);
        if (result.isReasonablyCertain() && ANALYZERS.containsKey(result.getLanguage())) {
            return ANALYZERS.get(result.getLanguage());
        }
        return "standard";
    }

    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        LanguageDetector detector = LanguageDetector.getDefaultLanguageDetector().loadModels();
        for (String s : new String[] {
                "El guardián del faro es una novela pausada y luminosa.",
                "Le gardien du phare est un roman lent et lumineux.",
                "The Lighthouse Keeper is a slow, luminous novel.",
                "Hola", "ISBN 978-84-376-0494-7"}) {
            LanguageResult r = detector.detect(s);
            System.out.printf(Locale.ROOT, "%-58s %-4s %-6s -> %s%n",
                    s, r.getLanguage(), r.getConfidence(), analyzerFor(detector, s));
        }
    }
}
----

Output (Tika 4.1.0):

[source,text]
----
El guardián del faro es una novela pausada y luminosa.     spa  LOW    -> standard
Le gardien du phare est un roman lent et lumineux.         fra  HIGH   -> french
The Lighthouse Keeper is a slow, luminous novel.           eng  HIGH   -> english
Hola                                                       tet  LOW    -> standard
ISBN 978-84-376-0494-7                                     mlt  LOW    -> standard
----

The first row is the instructive one. A correct, single-sentence Spanish text comes back as `spa` with `LOW`
confidence, so the policy sends it to the neutral `standard` analyzer even though the answer was right. That is the
cost of a conservative gate: a sentence of about nine words is not enough for `HIGH`, whereas the two-paragraph review in
`reviews-es.html` is (`spa HIGH`). Two ways to reduce the loss are to detect on the whole document's text rather than
on a sentence, and to accept `spa` at `LOW` when the document's declared language also says Spanish (the combined rule
in <<declared-versus-detected>>). Do not lower the threshold globally: the last two rows show what comes through if you
do.

Source: https://tika.apache.org/4.1.0/api/org/apache/tika/language/detect/LanguageResult.html[`LanguageResult`, Javadoc 4.1.0].

[#language-per-chunk]
== Language per chunk for mixed documents and RAG

When a document is mixed, or when the RAG pipeline chunks text anyway, detect the language per chunk and keep it as chunk
metadata. The chunk is the unit that is embedded and retrieved, so it is the unit whose language matters. The same
`Route`-style code applies to each chunk; the practical rules are:

* Detect after chunking, not before, and skip chunks shorter than a threshold (a few hundred characters is a sensible
  start; below that, inherit the language of the neighbouring chunks or of the document).
* Store the language and the confidence as chunk metadata so retrieval can filter (`language == "spa"`) or boost.
* Do not translate before chunking unless you have to: a translation pass is slow, costs money (see
  <<translation-status>>) and loses the original wording.

The chunking and metadata design is in xref:ai/rag-systems/document-parsing.adoc[Document parsing for RAG] and in
xref:database/vector-rag/document-ingestion-and-chunking.adoc[document ingestion and chunking].

== From the server: the /language endpoint

tika-server 4.1.x lists a `/language` endpoint (config name `language`) described as "Language detection". Two facts from
the 4.1.x server page matter operationally.

* *It runs in the server's own JVM, not in a fork.* The parsing endpoints (and `/detect` and `/unpack`) run in forked
  JVMs through Tika Pipes, so a crashing or runaway parse kills only a fork. `/language` is outside that isolation: a crash
  or memory exhaustion in it takes the whole server down. The page says so in a warning, and adds that if you do not
  need `/language` you should omit it from the list of enabled endpoints.
* *It caps the work.* Detection stops after the first 100,000 characters, and the server does not read more than that from
  the body into memory. The request size limit (`maxRequestSizeBytes`) still bounds what a client may send.

The endpoint takes the raw body (`PUT` or `POST`) and detects on it as text, without parsing it first:

[source,bash]
----
curl -T reviews-es.html http://localhost:9998/language
curl -T reseñas-fr.txt http://localhost:9998/language
curl -X POST -d 'The catalogue lists forty titles.' http://localhost:9998/language
----

Output, from the 4.1.0 `tika-server-standard` zip:

[source,text]
----
es
fr
en
----

Unlike tika-app, the server answers with two-letter ISO 639-1 codes. Its `LanguageResource` runs the same default
detector and then maps the three-letter code to ISO 639-1 "for backward compatibility with existing API consumers",
keeping the three-letter code only for a language that has no two-letter one. The 3.x `/language/stream` and
`/language/string` paths answer `404`. Normalise the codes before you compare server answers with the
`tk:detected-language` values of the metadata filter, which stay ISO 639-3. Prefer that filter anyway (language computed
inside the pipes fork, as part of the same parse) because it keeps the isolation. The endpoint-by-endpoint reference is
on the xref:backend/apache-tika/tika-server-rest-api.adoc[Tika Server (REST API)] page.

Source: https://tika.apache.org/docs/4.1.x/using-tika/server/index.html[Apache Tika 4.1.x, Tika Server] and the 4.1.0
`LanguageResource` source in https://github.com/apache/tika/tree/main/tika-server[`tika-server` on GitHub].

Readers of _Tika in Action_ (written for Tika 1.0) and of the 3.3.2 documentation's `examples.html` page will find this

----
// Tika 1.x. Removed in 2.0; shown here only so you can recognise it.
LanguageIdentifier identifier = new LanguageIdentifier(text);

----

`org.apache.tika.language.LanguageIdentifier`, `LanguageProfile` and `ProfilingHandler` were removed in Tika 2.0 and
replaced by the `LanguageDetector` SPI described above. Observed in this run: the 4.1.0 `tika-core` jar contains only
`+org/apache/tika/language/detect/*+` and `+org/apache/tika/language/translate/*+` in the `language` package, so the 1.x
classes are definitely gone. The mapping is mechanical:

[cols="3,3,4",options="header"]
|===
|Tika 1.x (the book, chapter 7) |Tika 2.x, 3.x, 4.x |Notes

|`new LanguageIdentifier(text).getLanguage()` |`LanguageDetector.getDefaultLanguageDetector().loadModels().detect(text).getLanguage()` |Models are loaded explicitly. Codes become ISO 639-3 (`eng`) rather than the two-letter codes of the old profiles.
|`identifier.isReasonablyCertain()` |`result.isReasonablyCertain()` on `LanguageResult` |Same name, different object; the criterion is the detector's.
|`LanguageProfile` (n-gram frequency table) |Internal to each detector's model |You no longer build or load profiles yourself. To train a new CharSoup model see "Building the Language Detector" in the 4.1.x docs.
|`ProfilingHandler` |`LanguageHandler` |Both are content handlers fed during the parse.
|Profiles for about 18 languages in the book's era |204 (CharSoup) or 148 (OpenNLP, observed) |The coverage is a different order of magnitude.
|`X-TIKA:language` style keys |`tk:detected-language` in 4.x (`X-TIKA:detected-language` in 3.x) |See the metadata filter section above.
|===

[#the-translator-api]
== The Translator API

Tika's translation support is a small, pluggable abstraction: given text, a source language and a target language, a
`Translator` returns the translated text, usually by calling an external service. It is deliberately *not* a translation
engine. The API lives in `tika-core` and is intentionally tiny.

[cols="3,6",options="header"]
|===
|Member |Meaning

|`String translate(String text, String sourceLanguage, String targetLanguage)` |Translate from a known source language. Throws `TikaException` and `IOException`.
|`String translate(String text, String targetLanguage)` |Translate, letting the service detect the source language.
|`boolean isAvailable()` |Whether the translator is configured and usable (credentials present, tool installed, service reachable).
|`DefaultTranslator` |The service-loader aggregate: it asks the available implementations in discovery order and uses the first one whose `isAvailable()` is true. Also `getTranslators()` and `getTranslator()`.
|`EmptyTranslator` |The do-nothing implementation.
|`Tika.translate(String, String)` and related |The facade methods that call the facade's translator (by default a `DefaultTranslator`).
|===

The interface extends `SelfConfiguring`, the 4.x marker that lets a component be configured from the JSON
configuration, and `TikaLoader.loadTranslator()` returns the configured translator (in 4.1.0 with no configuration it
returned a `DefaultTranslator`). What a particular implementation reads (a properties file on the classpath and its keys,
or setters) is listed in the next section.

=== Writing your own Translator

Because the contract is three methods, the practical way to use translation in 4.x is often to implement `Translator`
for a service you already use, rather than to rely on the bundled ones. The toy implementation below replaces whole words
from a glossary. It is not a translation engine: it exists to show the contract and the facade, and it runs offline.

[source,java]
----
import java.util.Map;
import org.apache.tika.Tika;
import org.apache.tika.detect.DefaultDetector;
import org.apache.tika.language.translate.Translator;
import org.apache.tika.parser.AutoDetectParser;

/** Toy translator: replaces whole words from a glossary. Shows the Translator contract only. */
public class GlossaryTranslator implements Translator {

    private final Map<String, String> glossary;

    public GlossaryTranslator(Map<String, String> glossary) {
        this.glossary = glossary;
    }

    @Override
    public String translate(String text, String sourceLanguage, String targetLanguage) {
        StringBuilder out = new StringBuilder();
        for (String word : text.split("(?<=\\s)|(?=\\s)")) {
            out.append(glossary.getOrDefault(word.toLowerCase(), word));
        }
        return out.toString();
    }

    @Override
    public String translate(String text, String targetLanguage) {
        return translate(text, "und", targetLanguage);
    }

    @Override
    public boolean isAvailable() {
        return true;
    }

    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        Translator t = new GlossaryTranslator(
                Map.of("le", "the", "gardien", "keeper", "du", "of the", "phare", "lighthouse"));
        System.out.println(t.translate("Le gardien du phare", "fr", "en"));
        Tika tika = new Tika(new DefaultDetector(), new AutoDetectParser(), t);
        System.out.println(tika.translate("le phare", "en"));
    }
}
----

Output (Tika 4.1.0):

[source,text]
----
the keeper of the lighthouse
the lighthouse
----

The second line shows that the `Tika` facade's constructor accepts a translator and delegates to it. In a real
implementation `translate` would call your translation service's SDK, add authentication from the environment (never
hard-code keys), set timeouts, and translate in batches that fit the service's size limit. Register it as a service
(`META-INF/services/org.apache.tika.language.translate.Translator`) only if you want `DefaultTranslator` to find it;
otherwise pass it explicitly as above. The service-loading and `@TikaComponent` mechanics are covered in
xref:backend/apache-tika/configuration.adoc[Configuration] and xref:backend/apache-tika/extending-tika.adoc[Extending Tika].

Source: https://github.com/apache/tika/blob/main/tika-example/src/main/java/org/apache/tika/example/TranslatorExample.java[`TranslatorExample` in `tika-example`]
and https://tika.apache.org/4.1.0/api/org/apache/tika/language/translate/Translator.html[`Translator`, Javadoc 4.1.0].

[#translation-status]
== The bundled translators and their maintenance status

This is the part of the page that has to be blunt. The `tika-translate` module is published at 4.1.0 (Maven Central
lists it, and it is in the `tika` repository), so it is not removed. But "published" is not "maintained", and the
evidence points in the direction of "kept, not developed". The facts, each with how it was established:

[cols="4,5",options="header"]
|===
|Fact |How it was established

|`tika-translate` 4.1.0 exists and `tika-core` 4.1.0 still contains `Translator`, `DefaultTranslator` and `EmptyTranslator` |Maven Central metadata; the contents of the 4.1.0 jars (observed).
|There is no translation page in the 4.1.x documentation |The 4.1.x navigation (Using Tika, Pipes, Configuration, Migration, Advanced, Developers, FAQ) has no translation entry. The only 4.x statement about translation is in the server migration page (next row).
|tika-server's `+/translate/*+` endpoints were removed in 4.x, together with the server's dependency on `tika-translate` |The 4.1.x page "Tika Server Migration". Its stated reason: the endpoints "were never usable as shipped": a request had to name a translator implementation class in the URL, each bundled implementation wraps a commercial API that needs credentials, and the server had no way to supply them. The page's advice: call the translation service directly, or invoke `tika-translate` from your own code, and that "Tika's translation support is unaffected".
|The older documentation that mentions translation is stale |The 3.3.2 `examples.html` page still shows a Microsoft Translation API example linked to an old MSDN address, and in the same page a `LanguageIdentifier` snippet whose class was removed in 2.0.
|A default setup has no usable translator |Observed in 4.1.0: `DefaultTranslator.isAvailable()` returned `false`, `getTranslator()` returned `null`, every candidate reported `available=false`, and `+translate(...)+` threw `TikaException: No translators currently available`.
|The module logs errors for unconfigured translators |Observed: `JoshuaNetworkTranslator.isAvailable()` logged an `ERROR` with a `MalformedURLException` stack trace (property `joshua.server.url` not set) each time availability was checked. Harmless, but noisy.
|The bundled implementations depend on third-party services or old client libraries |See the table below. The parent POM pins `microsoft-translator-java-api` at 0.6.2.
|===

The implementations registered by the 4.1.0 module's service file, and what each one needs (read from the class
constants, not from documentation, because there is none in 4.x):

[cols="3,5,4",options="header"]
|===
|Class |What it calls |Configuration it reads

|`GoogleTranslator` |The HTTP endpoint `https://www.googleapis.com/language/translate/v2` |`translator.google.properties`, key read from `translator.client-secret` (a string constant in the class; no public setter was found); default placeholder `dummy-secret`.
|`MicrosoftTranslator` |Microsoft translator client library 0.6.2 |`translator.microsoft.properties` with `translator.client-id` and `translator.client-secret`; placeholders `dummy-id` and `dummy-secret`.
|`YandexTranslator` |The endpoint `https://translate.yandex.net/api/v1.5/tr.json/translate` |`translator.yandex.properties` with `translator.api-key`; `translator.text.format`.
|`Lingo24Translator` |The endpoint `https://api.lingo24.com/mt/v1/translate` |`translator.lingo24.properties` with `translator.user-key`.
|`MosesTranslator` |A local Moses statistical machine translation installation (external process) |`translator.moses.properties` with `translator.smt_path` and `translator.script_path`.
|`MarianTranslator` |A local Marian NMT installation (external process or server) |`translator.marian.properties` with `translator.marian.path` and several tuning keys.
|`JoshuaNetworkTranslator` |A Joshua translation server over HTTP |`translator.joshua.properties` with `joshua.server.url`.

What this evidence supports, and what it does not:

* *Supported:* the module compiles and is published with Tika 4.1.0; no translator is usable without extra
  configuration; the server no longer offers translation; the project's own migration page tells you to call your
  translation service directly; and there is no 4.x narrative documentation for the module.
* *Not established:* whether any of the four commercial endpoints above still accepts requests in the form this code
  sends. Some of them name API versions (Yandex "v1.5", Google "v2") and a vendor library from a period when
  these services used other authentication schemes, and at least some of those services have changed since. None of
  them was called for this page (that would need real accounts and credentials, which this page must not carry), so the
  page makes no claim that they work and no claim that they are broken. Moses, Marian, Joshua and RTG, which need
  their own installations, were not exercised either.
* *No statement from the project* in the 4.1.x documentation says the module is deprecated or scheduled for removal.
  Calling it "unmaintained" would be an inference; the neutral statement is that nothing in 4.x documents, tests (for this
  page) or recommends it.

The recommendation for the Bookshelf intake service follows from this. Do not make a translation step through
`tika-translate` a hard dependency of the pipeline. If you need translation:

. Keep language detection in Tika, which is current, and treat the translation step as a separate stage that takes
  `(text, detected language)`.
. Call your translation provider's own SDK or REST API directly, as the migration page says; if you want it behind
  Tika's interface, implement `Translator` as shown above.
. Put credentials in environment variables or a secrets manager, set a timeout and a size limit per request, translate
  chunks rather than whole files, and cache by content hash (the `CachedTranslator` idea) so that re-ingesting a file
  does not pay twice.
. Record that a text was machine translated, and keep the original: a translation is a derived value, not the document.
. Mind privacy: a hosted translation service receives the text. Reviews may contain personal data, and manuscripts are
  unpublished commercial material; check the provider's data-handling terms before sending them.

https://github.com/apache/tika/tree/main/tika-translate[`tika-translate` module on GitHub] and
https://tika.apache.org/3.3.2/examples.html[Apache Tika 3.3.2, Examples].

[#evaluating-on-your-own-documents]
== Evaluating on your own documents

The numbers in the 4.1.x documentation (204 languages, benchmark comparisons against other detectors) describe the
model on its test corpus. Your documents are different: shorter, noisier, with domain vocabulary, boilerplate and
extraction artefacts. Before you pick thresholds, label a sample of a few hundred of your own extracted texts with the
true language (a native speaker or a trusted source), run the detector, and look at:

* accuracy by length bucket (under 50 characters, 50 to 200, 200 to 1,000, over 1,000), since length is the main driver;
* the precision of `HIGH` and of `HIGH` plus `MEDIUM`, which tells you where to put the gate;
* the confusions (Spanish with Galician or Catalan, Portuguese with Spanish, Croatian with Serbian);
* the share of `NONE` on non-trivial text, which is a symptom of extraction problems.

The project's own tool for measuring extraction quality, including the language of the extracted text, is
described in the xref:backend/apache-tika/tika-eval-and-extraction-quality.adoc[tika-eval & Extraction Quality] page.
Detectors and thresholds are not something to set once: re-run the check when you
change the extraction (an OCR engine, a parser upgrade) because that changes the text the detector sees.

== Memory, speed and thread safety

* *Memory.* The CharSoup model is a 6.4 MB file kept as one INT8 matrix, so a detector costs roughly that plus a rolling
  buffer, much smaller than a per-language model set. OpenNLP loads its own model. Create detectors once per thread and
  reuse them; do not call `loadModels()` per document.
* *Speed.* In the comparison run, CharSoup took about 1 to 10 milliseconds per document in a cold JVM (the first call,
  including class loading, was the slowest) against 1 to 15 milliseconds for OpenNLP. These are single, unwarmed
  measurements on one laptop and only indicate order of magnitude. The 100,000-character truncation bounds the cost per
  document.
* *Thread safety.* A `LanguageDetector` instance holds the accumulated text and is not safe to share between threads
  without external locking. Use a detector per worker, or a pool.
* *Inside a Pipes fork.* The metadata filter runs in the process that applies filters; for the forked parsing of the
  server and `PipesForkParser`, the isolation and limits are covered in the
  xref:backend/apache-tika/robustness-limits-and-timeouts.adoc[Robustness, Limits & Timeouts] page. The `/language`
  endpoint is the exception because it is not forked.

== Pitfalls

* *Trusting `getRawScore()` on short text.* The `ok` run above had a raw score of 0.779 for Zazaki. Gate on the
  confidence level.
* *Treating `HIGH` as a guarantee.* It is a statistical statement about the text. A Spanish text full of French
  quotations can still be `HIGH` for Spanish and wrong for the quotation.
* *Comparing declared and detected codes as strings.* `es` is not `spa`. Normalise first.
* *Forgetting `reset()`.* Reusing a detector with `addText` and `detect()` without a reset mixes documents.
* *Running detection on boilerplate.* Detect on the body text; strip navigation and footers where you can.
* *Mixing thresholds between detectors.* CharSoup and OpenNLP scores are not comparable.
* *Depending on `setPriors`.* It threw `UnsupportedOperationException` for CharSoup in 4.1.0.
* *Exposing `/language` to untrusted callers in a shared server.* It is not isolated in a fork. Keep it off or behind an
  authenticated proxy, in line with the guidance in the xref:backend/apache-tika/security.adoc[Security] page.
* *Building on the translator module without checking it.* See the maintenance section above.
* *Assuming the 3.x documentation is current.* The 3.3.2 examples page still shows a removed class.

[#what-is-not-verified]
== What this page did not verify

* The behaviour of `setMixedLanguages`, `setShortText` and `loadModels(Set)` on CharSoup was not exercised.
* `setPriors` was exercised only on `CharSoupLanguageDetector` (it throws); `OpenNLPDetector.setPriors` was not.
* The Optimaize, Lingo24 and MIT-LL language-detection modules and every translator implementation's remote or local
  back end were not called. Their existence at 4.1.0 and the endpoints and property names were read from Maven Central
  metadata and the compiled classes.
* The 4.x JSON selection of a detector by friendly name (`charsoup-language-detector`) in the parse-context section was
  not exercised; only the metadata filter was configured and run.
* Timings are single unwarmed measurements.

== Checklist

* Add `tika-langdetect-charsoup` (and nothing else) to start; call `loadModels()` once per detector.
* Detect on body text of a reasonable length; split mixed documents and detect per chunk.
* Gate on `getConfidence()` or `isReasonablyCertain()`, never on the raw score, and keep an explicit allow-list of
  languages the service handles.
* Prefer the `charsoup-metadata-filter` (`+tk:detected-language*+`) over `LanguageHandler` when you also need the text.
* Store the declared and the detected language separately and normalise their codes before comparing.
* Reset detectors between documents, and do not share one between threads.
* Keep `/language` off public servers, or behind authentication.
* Treat `tika-translate` as undocumented and untested in 4.x, and do not depend on it: call your translation service directly,
  or wrap it in your own `Translator`, and keep credentials in the environment.
* Label a sample of your own documents and choose thresholds from it.
