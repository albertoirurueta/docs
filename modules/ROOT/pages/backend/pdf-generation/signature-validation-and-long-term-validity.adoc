Signing a PDF is only half of the story. A signature protects nobody until somebody checks it, and the check has to
answer more questions than "does the cryptography add up". This page explains what a validator really verifies, shows
how to run the check from Java with iText 9.8 and with the EU Digital Signature Service (DSS) 6.5, how to read the
answers, how Adobe Acrobat decides whom to trust, and what you must do so that a signature made today can still be
verified in 2046. Versions are stated as of 2026-10-09: iText Core 9.8.0, EU DSS 6.5, Apache PDFBox 3.0.8 (used by
DSS) and Bouncy Castle 1.86 on Java 21.

The examples validate the invoice of the *Bookshelf Billing* scenario that is created and signed on the page
xref:backend/pdf-generation/signing-with-itext.adoc[Signing with iText]. The signing code shown here is only a small
helper to obtain a test file; how signing works, which options exist and how to put the key in a hardware module are
explained there and in xref:backend/pdf-generation/remote-hsm-and-deferred-signing.adoc[Remote Signing, HSMs & Deferred Signing].
The vocabulary (digest, certificate chain, CRL, OCSP, timestamp) is introduced in
xref:backend/pdf-generation/digital-signatures-fundamentals.adoc[Digital Signatures Fundamentals], and the profile
names used below (B-B, B-T, B-LT, B-LTA) are defined in
xref:backend/pdf-generation/pades-baseline-levels.adoc[PAdES Baseline Levels]. Signing with PDFBox and DSS is in
xref:backend/pdf-generation/signing-with-pdfbox-and-eu-dss.adoc[Signing with PDFBox & EU DSS].

== What a validator checks

"Valid" is not one question but a stack of them, and a validator reports a different answer when a different layer
fails. Following the order used by ETSI EN 319 102-1 (the standard that both iText and DSS implement), the layers are:

. *Format.* The signature container is well formed: the `/ByteRange` is plausible, `/Contents` holds a parseable
  CMS (PKCS#7) structure, and mandatory signed attributes such as the content type and message digest are present.
. *Integrity.* The digest of the bytes named by `/ByteRange` equals the digest signed in the CMS, and the signature value
  verifies with the public key of the signing certificate. Any change inside the covered range ends here with a definite
  negative: the document was altered, or the signature was not produced over this file.
. *Identification of the signer.* The signing certificate is found, and where the CMS carries a reference to it (the
  signing-certificate-v2 attribute, required for PAdES) it matches.
. *Certificate path.* A chain is built from the signer to a *trust anchor* that the validator has been told to trust.
  Each certificate must be inside its validity period at the relevant time, carry the extensions that its role needs (CA
  basic constraints, key usage) and be signed with an algorithm that is still acceptable.
. *Revocation.* For each certificate of the chain the validator wants evidence, from a CRL or an OCSP response, that it
  had not been revoked, and that the evidence is fresh enough.
. *Time.* The validator needs to know when the signature existed. Without a trusted timestamp the only time is the
  claimed signing time written by the signer, which proves nothing. With a signature timestamp (RFC 3161) or a document
  timestamp, there is a proof of existence, and the certificate path is judged at that moment instead of today.
. *Modifications after signing.* The signature covers the bytes up to its own revision. A later incremental update, the
  mechanism described in xref:backend/pdf-generation/pdf-file-structure.adoc[How a PDF File Works], leaves those bytes
  intact, so the signature stays mathematically correct, but the validator still has to decide whether the added
  revision is an allowed change (for example, a second signature, or filling a form field when the author's rules
  permit it) or a forbidden one (new page content, changed text). This is the layer that most often surprises developers.
. *Policy.* Finally the answers are combined according to a validation policy: which algorithms, key sizes and
  revocation freshness are acceptable, and whether a missing piece makes the result a failure, a warning or nothing.

Three outcomes come out of this, and the terms are the same in both libraries. *Valid* (iText `VALID`, DSS
`TOTAL_PASSED`) means every check that the policy requires passed. *Invalid* (iText `INVALID`, DSS `TOTAL_FAILED`)
means that a definite negative was found, typically a broken digest. *Indeterminate* (iText `INDETERMINATE`, DSS
`INDETERMINATE` with a sub-indication) means that no negative was found but something needed to conclude is missing:
a trust anchor, a revocation answer, a timestamp. Indeterminate is the answer you will get most often in practice, it
is not a polite way of saying valid, and treating it as valid (or as invalid) in application code is a design decision to
make consciously.

A last conceptual point: a validator does not decide whether the signer is *who they claim to be* in a legal sense. It
decides whether the certificate chains to something that the validating party has chosen to trust. Trust is
configuration, never a property of the PDF.

The diagram summarises the decision flow that both libraries walk through.

[mermaid]
....
flowchart TD
    A[Signed PDF] --> B{Signature container well formed?}
    B -- no --> X1[Invalid: format failure]
    B -- yes --> C{Digest over ByteRange and signature value match?}
    C -- no --> X2[Invalid: document altered or wrong key]
    C -- yes --> D{Certificate chain reaches a trust anchor?}
    D -- no --> I1[Indeterminate: untrusted chain]
    D -- yes --> E{Revocation evidence available and fresh?}
    E -- no --> I2[Indeterminate: revocation unknown]
    E -- revoked --> X3[Invalid: revoked before signing time]
    E -- yes --> F{Trusted timestamp present?}
    F -- no --> G[Judge the chain today, with the claimed time]
    F -- yes --> H[Judge the chain at the timestamp time]
    G --> J{Changes after the signature allowed?}
    H --> J
    J -- no --> X4[Invalid: forbidden modification]
    J -- yes --> K{Algorithms acceptable at that time?}
    K -- no --> I3[Indeterminate or invalid by policy]
    K -- yes --> V[Valid]
....

Revocation and chain conditions are shown before the time check for readability; in the libraries the time is chosen
first and the other checks are then evaluated against it.

== A file to validate

To keep this page self-contained, the examples need a signed file, a trust anchor and, for the long-term part, a
timestamp authority. The test PKI follows the convention used throughout this section: a self-signed test root
`billing-test-ca.p12` (alias `billing-test-ca`) that issues the signer certificate stored with its chain in
`billing-signer.p12` (alias `billing-signer`), the password read from the environment variable
`BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD` (any throwaway value, such as `changeit-placeholder`, for this experiment), and the CA
certificate exported to `billing-test-ca.pem`. The complete script is explained in
xref:backend/pdf-generation/digital-signatures-fundamentals.adoc[Digital Signatures Fundamentals]; this is the condensed
version used for this page. It creates the keys at run time, and nothing generated is ever committed.

[source,bash]
----
export BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD=changeit-placeholder
mkdir -p pki && cd pki

keytool -genkeypair -alias billing-test-ca -keyalg RSA -keysize 3072 -sigalg SHA256withRSA \
  -dname "CN=Bookshelf Billing Test CA, O=Bookshelf Test, C=ES" -validity 3650 \
  -ext bc:c -ext ku:c=keyCertSign,cRLSign -keystore billing-test-ca.p12 -storetype PKCS12 \
  -storepass:env BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD -keypass:env BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD

keytool -genkeypair -alias billing-signer -keyalg RSA -keysize 3072 -sigalg SHA256withRSA \
  -dname "CN=Bookshelf Billing Signer, O=Bookshelf Test, C=ES" -validity 730 \
  -keystore billing-signer.p12 -storetype PKCS12 \
  -storepass:env BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD -keypass:env BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD
keytool -certreq -alias billing-signer -keystore billing-signer.p12 \
  -storepass:env BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD -file billing-signer.csr
keytool -gencert -alias billing-test-ca -keystore billing-test-ca.p12 \
  -storepass:env BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD -sigalg SHA256withRSA -validity 730 -rfc \
  -ext ku:c=digitalSignature,nonRepudiation -ext eku=emailProtection \
  -infile billing-signer.csr -outfile billing-signer.pem

keytool -exportcert -rfc -alias billing-test-ca -keystore billing-test-ca.p12 \
  -storepass:env BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD -file billing-test-ca.pem
keytool -importcert -noprompt -alias billing-test-ca -file billing-test-ca.pem \
  -keystore billing-signer.p12 -storepass:env BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD
keytool -importcert -alias billing-signer -file billing-signer.pem \
  -keystore billing-signer.p12 -storepass:env BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD
rm -f billing-signer.csr billing-signer.pem
cd ..
----

The helper below builds a two-line invoice and signs it with a detached CAdES signature in append mode. It is
deliberately minimal: no timestamp, no revocation data, so the result is a plain B-B signature. The reasons for each
line are in the signing page.

[source,java]
----
package example;

import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfDocument;
import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfReader;
import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfWriter;
import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.StampingProperties;
import com.itextpdf.layout.Document;
import com.itextpdf.layout.element.Paragraph;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.BouncyCastleDigest;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.IExternalSignature;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.PdfSigner;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.PrivateKeySignature;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.SignerProperties;

import java.io.OutputStream;
import java.nio.file.Files;
import java.nio.file.Path;
import java.security.KeyStore;
import java.security.PrivateKey;
import java.security.Security;
import java.security.cert.Certificate;

import org.bouncycastle.jce.provider.BouncyCastleProvider;

public final class SampleSigner {

    public static void createInvoice(Path target) throws Exception {
        try (PdfDocument pdf = new PdfDocument(new PdfWriter(target.toString()));
             Document document = new Document(pdf)) {
            document.add(new Paragraph("Bookshelf Billing - invoice 2026-0001"));
            document.add(new Paragraph("Total: 42.00 EUR"));
        }
    }

    public static void sign(Path unsigned, Path signed, String fieldName) throws Exception {
        Security.addProvider(new BouncyCastleProvider());
        char[] storePassword = System.getenv("BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD").toCharArray();
        KeyStore store = KeyStore.getInstance("PKCS12");
        try (var in = Files.newInputStream(Path.of("pki/billing-signer.p12"))) {
            store.load(in, storePassword);
        }
        PrivateKey privateKey = (PrivateKey) store.getKey("billing-signer", storePassword);
        Certificate[] chain = store.getCertificateChain("billing-signer");

        IExternalSignature signature =
                new PrivateKeySignature(privateKey, "SHA-256", BouncyCastleProvider.PROVIDER_NAME);
        try (PdfReader reader = new PdfReader(unsigned.toString());
             OutputStream out = Files.newOutputStream(signed)) {
            PdfSigner signer = new PdfSigner(reader, out, new StampingProperties().useAppendMode());
            signer.setSignerProperties(new SignerProperties().setFieldName(fieldName));
            signer.signDetached(new BouncyCastleDigest(), signature, chain, null, null, null, 0,
                    PdfSigner.CryptoStandard.CADES);
        }
    }

    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        Files.createDirectories(Path.of("out"));
        createInvoice(Path.of("out/invoice.pdf"));
        sign(Path.of("out/invoice.pdf"), Path.of("out/invoice-signed.pdf"), "billing-signature");
    }
}

== Validating with iText 9

Until iText Core 8.0.5 there was no validator in the library; developers combined `SignatureUtil`, `PdfPKCS7` and
their own chain code. Since 8.0.5 the `sign` module contains a validation API, and iText Core 9.0 finalised it in the
package `com.itextpdf.signatures.validation` of that same module, with a policy that follows RFC 5280, RFC 3161 and
ETSI EN 319 102-1, according to the iText Knowledge Base. The pieces are:

* `ValidatorChainBuilder` assembles the validators and their configuration: trusted certificates, extra known
  certificates, revocation clients, resource retrievers, the EU list of trusted lists and the report generators.
* `SignatureValidator`, obtained from `buildSignatureValidator(PdfDocument)`, validates all signatures
  (`validateSignatures()`) or one by name (`validateSignature(String)`), newest first, because the timestamp of a later
  signature fixes what an earlier one was covering.
* `ValidationReport` is the result. `getValidationResult()` returns `VALID`, `INVALID` or `INDETERMINATE`;
  `getFailures()` and `getCertificateFailures()` list what went wrong; `getLogs()` and `getCertificateLogs()` list
  everything that was examined.
* `SignatureValidationProperties` is the validation policy, described below.

Licence: the validation API is part of the `sign` module of iText Core, under the same AGPLv3 or commercial licence as
the rest of iText Core; it is not a separate add-on.

=== The first run: three honest answers

The class below validates the same file three times and prints the report. The first run has no trust anchor, the
second trusts the test CA, the third also supplies a CRL issued by that CA. A CRL is the simplest revocation evidence to
fabricate for a test PKI: the helper `TestCrl` signs an empty list with the CA key at run time and hands it to the
validator through `ICrlClient`, so that the experiment needs no network and no certificate carries a distribution
point.

[source,java]
----
package example;

import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfDocument;
import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfReader;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.ValidatorChainBuilder;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.report.CertificateReportItem;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.report.ReportItem;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.report.ValidationReport;

import java.io.InputStream;
import java.nio.file.Files;
import java.nio.file.Path;
import java.security.cert.CertificateFactory;
import java.security.cert.X509Certificate;
import java.util.List;

public final class ItextValidate {

    static X509Certificate loadCertificate(Path pem) throws Exception {
        try (InputStream in = Files.newInputStream(pem)) {
            return (X509Certificate) CertificateFactory.getInstance("X.509").generateCertificate(in);
        }
    }

    static ValidationReport validate(Path pdf, List<X509Certificate> trustAnchors, boolean withCrl)
            throws Exception {
        try (PdfDocument document = new PdfDocument(new PdfReader(pdf.toString()))) {
            ValidatorChainBuilder builder = new ValidatorChainBuilder()
                    .withTrustedCertificates(List.copyOf(trustAnchors));
            if (withCrl) {
                builder.getProperties().addCrlClient(new TestCrl());
            }
            return builder.buildSignatureValidator(document).validateSignatures();
        }
    }

    static void print(String title, ValidationReport report) {
        System.out.println("== " + title + ": " + report.getValidationResult());
        for (ReportItem failure : report.getFailures()) {
            System.out.println("  failure [" + failure.getCheckName() + "] " + failure.getMessage());
        }
        for (CertificateReportItem failure : report.getCertificateFailures()) {
            System.out.println("  certificate failure: " + failure.getMessage());
        }
    }

    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        Path signed = Path.of(args[0]);
        X509Certificate ca = loadCertificate(Path.of("pki/billing-test-ca.pem"));
        print("without trust anchor", validate(signed, List.of(), false));
        print("with test CA as trust anchor", validate(signed, List.of(ca), false));
        print("with trust anchor and a CRL", validate(signed, List.of(ca), true));
    }
}

`TestCrl` needs Bouncy Castle's certificate API, which `bouncy-castle-adapter` already brings in:

[source,java]
----
package example;

import com.itextpdf.signatures.ICrlClient;

import java.io.InputStream;
import java.nio.file.Files;
import java.nio.file.Path;
import java.security.KeyStore;
import java.security.PrivateKey;
import java.security.cert.X509Certificate;
import java.util.Collection;
import java.util.Date;
import java.util.List;

import org.bouncycastle.cert.X509CRLHolder;
import org.bouncycastle.cert.X509v2CRLBuilder;
import org.bouncycastle.cert.jcajce.JcaX509CertificateHolder;
import org.bouncycastle.operator.jcajce.JcaContentSignerBuilder;

/** Issues an empty CRL (nothing revoked) from the test CA and serves it to the validator. */
public final class TestCrl implements ICrlClient {

    private final byte[] encodedCrl;

    public TestCrl() throws Exception {
        char[] storePassword = System.getenv("BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD").toCharArray();
        KeyStore store = KeyStore.getInstance("PKCS12");
        try (InputStream in = Files.newInputStream(Path.of("pki/billing-test-ca.p12"))) {
            store.load(in, storePassword);
        }
        PrivateKey caKey = (PrivateKey) store.getKey("billing-test-ca", storePassword);
        X509Certificate ca = (X509Certificate) store.getCertificate("billing-test-ca");

        long now = System.currentTimeMillis();
        X509v2CRLBuilder builder = new X509v2CRLBuilder(
                new JcaX509CertificateHolder(ca).getSubject(), new Date(now - 60_000));
        builder.setNextUpdate(new Date(now + 7L * 24 * 3600 * 1000));
        X509CRLHolder crl = builder.build(new JcaContentSignerBuilder("SHA256withRSA").build(caKey));
        this.encodedCrl = crl.getEncoded();
    }

    @Override
    public Collection<byte[]> getEncoded(X509Certificate checkCert, String url) {
        return List.of(encodedCrl);
    }
}

[source,text]

== without trust anchor: INDETERMINATE
  failure [Revocation data check.] Certificate revocation status cannot be checked: no revocation data available or the status cannot be determined.
  failure [Certificate check.] Certificate CN=Bookshelf Billing Signer, O=Bookshelf Test, C=ES isn't trusted and issuer certificate isn't provided.
  certificate failure: (the same two messages again, as certificate-level items)
== with test CA as trust anchor: INDETERMINATE
  failure [Revocation data check.] Certificate revocation status cannot be checked: no revocation data available or the status cannot be determined.
  certificate failure: (the same revocation message)
== with trust anchor and a CRL: VALID

This is the honest answer for a self-signed test CA, and it is worth reading slowly. The signature is cryptographically
perfect in all three runs. The first run is `INDETERMINATE` because the validator has never heard of the issuer: the
chain ends in a certificate that nobody told it to trust, which is exactly what happens when an unknown Acrobat user
opens a document signed with a private CA. The second run trusts the CA but still cannot conclude, because the file
contains no revocation evidence and the test certificate has no CRL distribution point or OCSP address that iText could
fetch. Only when evidence is supplied does the result become `VALID`. Nothing was "fixed" by the last run: the three
results differ only in the information given to the validator. In production the second and third runs are
replaced by a real CA that publishes revocation endpoints (or by embedding revocation data in the file, see the
long-term section), and the first by a trust list that you control or by the EU trusted lists described below.

=== Failures that are not indeterminate

The next example makes two different alterations of the signed invoice and validates both with the trust anchor and the
CRL from before. The first replaces one digit of the page width inside the signed bytes (the same number of
characters, so the `/ByteRange` offsets stay valid); the second appends a paragraph to the document in append mode, a
new revision that leaves every signed byte untouched.

[source,java]
----
package example;

import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfDocument;
import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfReader;
import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfWriter;
import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.StampingProperties;
import com.itextpdf.layout.Document;
import com.itextpdf.layout.element.Paragraph;

import java.nio.charset.StandardCharsets;
import java.nio.file.Files;
import java.nio.file.Path;
import java.security.cert.X509Certificate;
import java.util.List;

public final class TamperDemo {

    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        Path signed = Path.of("out/invoice-signed.pdf");
        X509Certificate ca = ItextValidate.loadCertificate(Path.of("pki/billing-test-ca.pem"));

        // 1. Change one byte inside the signed range: page width 595 becomes 596.
        String text = new String(Files.readAllBytes(signed), StandardCharsets.ISO_8859_1);
        Files.write(Path.of("out/invoice-edited-bytes.pdf"),
                text.replaceFirst("MediaBox\\[0 0 595 842]", "MediaBox[0 0 596 842]")
                        .getBytes(StandardCharsets.ISO_8859_1));

        // 2. Append a new revision after the signature.
        try (PdfDocument pdf = new PdfDocument(new PdfReader(signed.toString()),
                new PdfWriter("out/invoice-appended.pdf"), new StampingProperties().useAppendMode());
             Document document = new Document(pdf)) {
            document.add(new Paragraph("Added after signing"));
        }

        for (String name : List.of("invoice-edited-bytes.pdf", "invoice-appended.pdf")) {
            ItextValidate.print(name, ItextValidate.validate(Path.of("out/" + name), List.of(ca), true));
        }
    }
}

[source,text]
----
== invoice-edited-bytes.pdf: INVALID
  failure [Signature verification check.] Signature billing-signature cannot be mathematically verified.
== invoice-appended.pdf: INVALID
  failure [DocMDP check.] Page was unexpectedly modified.
----

The first result is the textbook integrity failure. The second is more instructive: the original signature is still
mathematically correct, since the bytes it covers did not change, but the document revisions validator
(`DocumentRevisionsValidator`, which `SignatureValidator` calls internally) compared the later revision with the signed
one and found a change to a page that the document's modification rules (DocMDP, "document modification detection and
prevention") do not allow. An uncertified signature allows little beyond adding signatures and filling form fields, and
a certification signature can tighten or relax this. A consequence for the *Bookshelf Billing* workflow: if a stamp, a
page number or an archiving annotation has to be added to an invoice, it must be added before the signature, or the
document must be signed in a way that explicitly permits it.

=== The validation policy

`SignatureValidationProperties` holds the policy, and it is set per combination of three dimensions, each with a small
enum of constants in `com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.context`: the validator that is running (for example
`CRL_VALIDATOR`, `OCSP_VALIDATOR`, `REVOCATION_DATA_VALIDATOR`, `CERTIFICATE_CHAIN_VALIDATOR`, `SIGNATURE_VALIDATOR`,
`DOCUMENT_REVISIONS_VALIDATOR`), the role of the certificate being examined (`SIGNER_CERT`, `CERT_ISSUER`, `TRUSTED`,
`TIMESTAMP`, `OCSP_ISSUER`, `CRL_ISSUER`, `LOTL_CERT`), and whether the evidence is `HISTORICAL` (covered by a
timestamp) or `PRESENT` (judged now). The factory classes `ValidatorContexts`, `CertificateSources` and
`TimeBasedContexts` build the sets (`+of(...)+`, `all()`, `+complementOf(...)+`).

Three settings matter most:

* *Freshness.* The maximum accepted age of a CRL or OCSP response when the validation happens. The defaults, from the
  iText Knowledge Base article on the policy, are one minute for historical revocation data (data stored in the
  document and covered by a timestamp) and 30 days for present data (data not covered by any timestamp).
* *Online fetching.* `ALWAYS_FETCH`, `FETCH_IF_NO_OTHER_DATA_AVAILABLE` or `NEVER_FETCH` decide whether the library
  may open network connections to get CRLs, OCSP responses and issuer certificates. In a locked-down service the right
  choice is often `NEVER_FETCH` plus clients that read from your own store.
* *Continue after failure.* Whether the validator stops at the first failing check or keeps collecting failures, which
  is what you want for a diagnostic report and not what you want for a fast reject.

The policy also states which certificate extensions are required. Per the same article, a signing certificate needs no
key usage extension, which technically contradicts ETSI but matches Adobe Acrobat and most other tools; a CA
certificate must have the basic constraints extension with the CA flag, and `keyCertSign` if key usage is present;
certificates that sign OCSP responses need the `ocsp_signing` extended key usage, and those that sign timestamps need
`time_stamping`. For certificate validity, iText uses only the "shell" model: the whole chain must have been valid at
the time of signing, so an intermediate CA's validity must contain that of every certificate it issued. The "chain"
model, which only asks that each certificate was valid when it signed the next one, is not implemented in iText
(DSS's bundled policy also declares the shell model, shown below).

=== Trusting the EU list of trusted lists

Maintaining your own set of trust anchors is fine for a closed ecosystem, but a Spanish invoice that was signed with a
qualified certificate must be checked against the trusted lists that every EU member state publishes under eIDAS.
The *List of Trusted Lists* (LOTL) is a signed XML file at https://ec.europa.eu/tools/lotl/eu-lotl.xml that points to
those national lists; each national list names the trust service providers and the certificates of their services,
with a service type that tells in which role a certificate can be trusted (issuing qualified certificates, timestamps,
OCSP). A careful validator must not just download the certificates, it must verify the signatures of the LOTL and of
every national list. iText does this, per the Knowledge Base article on the subject.

Switching it on is one call on the builder, `trustEuropeanLotl(true)`, plus one rule: the data is large and slow to
fetch, so it lives in a static cache that you must initialise once, at application start, before the first
validation, with `LotlService.initializeGlobalCache(LotlFetchingProperties)`. Initialising it twice is refused on
purpose. The cache is refreshed in the background by a timer; by default the data is refreshed about every 5.5 hours
(23% of the staleness) and is usable for 24 hours if refresh fails. `LotlFetchingProperties` takes a mandatory strategy
for what to do when one country's list cannot be retrieved (`RemoveOnFailingCountryData` ignores that country,
`ThrowExceptionOnFailingCountryData` fails), and optional filters for the countries and for the service types. Because
the LOTL's own signature must be verified with certificates that are not in the LOTL, iText needs the certificates
published in the Official Journal of the EU; they ship in the separate artifact
`com.itextpdf:eu-trusted-lists-resources` (version 1.1.0 on Maven Central at the time of writing), which you add as a
dependency and update when a new version appears. The article states that a transition period of about 15 days applies
when the journal certificates change, during which iText logs a warning, and afterwards LOTL validation fails if the
resources are outdated.

[source,java]
----
package example;

import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfDocument;
import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfReader;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.ValidatorChainBuilder;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.lotl.LotlCountryCodeConstants;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.lotl.LotlFetchingProperties;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.lotl.LotlService;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.lotl.RemoveOnFailingCountryData;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.validation.report.ValidationReport;

public final class ItextLotl {

    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        // Once per JVM, at start-up: downloads and validates the EU LOTL and the selected national lists.
        LotlService.initializeGlobalCache(
                new LotlFetchingProperties(new RemoveOnFailingCountryData())
                        .setCountryNames(LotlCountryCodeConstants.SPAIN));

        try (PdfDocument document = new PdfDocument(new PdfReader(args[0]))) {
            ValidatorChainBuilder builder = new ValidatorChainBuilder().trustEuropeanLotl(true);
            ValidationReport report = builder.buildSignatureValidator(document).validateSignatures();
            System.out.println("result: " + report.getValidationResult());
            report.getFailures().forEach(f -> System.out.println("  " + f.getMessage()));
        }
    }
}

== Validating with EU DSS

DSS is the European Commission's open-source reference implementation of the AdES standards (PAdES for PDF, and also
CAdES, XAdES, JAdES and ASiC). It is licensed under LGPL-2.1 and is the library behind the Commission's own validation
service. For PDF it parses documents with PDFBox (Apache-2.0), so it does not depend on iText. Where iText returns one
report with a handful of messages, DSS produces four XML documents of increasing detail, which makes it the better
tool when the answer must be explained to an auditor.

The entry point is `SignedDocumentValidator.fromDocument(DSSDocument)`, which picks the right validator for the
document type through the Java service loader, then a `CertificateVerifier` that carries the trust anchors and the
sources of revocation data, and finally `validateDocument()`, which returns a `Reports` object.

DSS 6 split optional pieces into separate modules that must be on the classpath, and the first run tells you which in a
message (three messages were hit while building the example, with the artifact list in the Maven section
above as the cure): `No ValidationPolicyFactory has been found` means add `dss-policy-jaxb`; `No implementation found
for ICMSUtils` means choose one of `dss-cms-object` or `dss-cms-stream`; `No implementation found for ICRLUtils` means
choose `dss-crl-parser-x509crl` or `dss-crl-parser-stream`; and the class `SignedDocumentValidator` itself lives in
`dss-validation`, which is not pulled in transitively by `dss-pades-pdfbox`.

[source,java]
----
package example;

import eu.europa.esig.dss.enumerations.Indication;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.model.DSSDocument;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.model.FileDocument;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.simplereport.SimpleReport;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.spi.DSSUtils;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.spi.validation.CommonCertificateVerifier;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.spi.x509.CommonTrustedCertificateSource;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.validation.SignedDocumentValidator;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.validation.reports.Reports;

import java.io.File;
import java.nio.file.Files;
import java.nio.file.Path;

public final class DssValidate {

    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        DSSDocument document = new FileDocument(new File(args[0]));

        CommonTrustedCertificateSource trusted = new CommonTrustedCertificateSource();
        for (int i = 1; i < args.length; i++) {
            trusted.addCertificate(DSSUtils.loadCertificate(new File(args[i])));
        }
        CommonCertificateVerifier verifier = new CommonCertificateVerifier();
        verifier.setTrustedCertSources(trusted);

        SignedDocumentValidator validator = SignedDocumentValidator.fromDocument(document);
        validator.setCertificateVerifier(verifier);

        String policy = System.getProperty("policy");
        Reports reports = policy == null
                ? validator.validateDocument()
                : validator.validateDocument(new File(policy));

        SimpleReport simple = reports.getSimpleReport();
        for (String id : simple.getSignatureIdList()) {
            Indication indication = simple.getIndication(id);
            System.out.println("signature: " + indication + " / " + simple.getSubIndication(id)
                    + " / format " + simple.getSignatureFormat(id));
            simple.getAdESValidationErrors(id).forEach(m -> System.out.println("  error: " + m.getValue()));
            simple.getAdESValidationWarnings(id).forEach(m -> System.out.println("  warning: " + m.getValue()));
        }

        Files.createDirectories(Path.of("out/reports"));
        Files.writeString(Path.of("out/reports/simple.xml"), reports.getXmlSimpleReport());
        Files.writeString(Path.of("out/reports/detailed.xml"), reports.getXmlDetailedReport());
        Files.writeString(Path.of("out/reports/diagnostic.xml"), reports.getXmlDiagnosticData());
        Files.writeString(Path.of("out/reports/etsi.xml"), reports.getXmlValidationReport());
    }
}

=== The four reports

* The *simple report* (`SimpleReport`) is the macroscopic view: for each signature an indication, a sub-indication, the
  detected format (here `PAdES-BASELINE-B`), the signer, the signing time, and the errors and warnings seen above. It is
  what a user interface shows. It also names the validation policy that was used; with the default one that is "QES
  AES/QC AES TL based", which expects the signer's chain to end in a certificate from a trusted list.
* The *detailed report* (`DetailedReport`) lists every constraint that the policy evaluated, in the building blocks of
  ETSI EN 319 102-1 (format checking, identification of the signing certificate, validation context initialisation,
  X.509 certificate validation, cryptographic verification, signature acceptance validation, and so on), each with its
  status and a message key. In the test above the detailed document for one signature contains about 66 constraint
  entries. It answers "which check, exactly, failed". The documentation chapter "Interpreting a detailed report" shows
  how to follow a failure back to its root.
* The *diagnostic data* (`DiagnosticData`) is the raw, policy-independent material: every certificate with its
  extensions, every digest comparison, every revocation datum found, every timestamp. The detailed and simple reports
  are computed from it plus the policy, which makes it the right artefact to keep for a re-evaluation: you can run the
  same diagnostic data against a new policy later without having the original file or the network.
* The *ETSI validation report* is the machine-readable report standardised in ETSI TS 119 102-2 (root element
  `ValidationReport`, here in the namespace `http://uri.etsi.org/19102/v1.4.1#`), meant to be exchanged between systems or
  archived. It carries the signature identifier with its digest, the validation status and the validation time.
  (The iText XML report above uses version 1.2.1 of the same schema.)

Sample documents for each report and a section on reading them are in the DSS documentation, chapter
https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/DSS/webapp-demo/doc/dss-documentation.html#_inspection_of_validation_reports[Inspection of validation reports].
Stylesheets that turn the simple and detailed reports into HTML or PDF come with the library.

=== Policy and validation level in DSS

DSS ships its default policy as an XML file inside `dss-policy-jaxb` (`policy/constraint.xml`). Each rule has a level:
`FAIL`, `WARN`, `INFORM` or `IGNORE`. That file is the policy you copy and edit to adapt the validator, and then pass
to `validateDocument(File)`, as the example above does when started with `+-Dpolicy=...+`. As an experiment, the file was
copied and the two lines for the signing certificate, `RevocationDataAvailable` and `AcceptableRevocationDataFound`,
were changed from `FAIL` to `WARN`. The same `invoice-signed.pdf` with the test CA then gave `TOTAL_PASSED` with an
extra warning "No revocation data found for the certificate!". The lesson is double-edged: a policy is how you adapt the
validator to an organisation that has no revocation infrastructure, and also how you can quietly turn a failure into a
pass. A policy used in production must be reviewed, versioned and archived with the validation results.

The bundled policy also contains the cryptographic suitability rules, which matter for the long-term section. It lists
the acceptable encryption and digest algorithms, minimum key sizes, and an `AlgoExpirationDate` table with the date
from which an algorithm (or an algorithm with a key size below a given number) is no longer accepted, citing ETSI
TS 119 312. In the DSS 6.5 file, for example, SHA-1 is dated 2012-08-01 and MD5 2004-08-01, SHA-224 2029-01-01, RSA keys
shorter than 1900 bits 2027-01-01 and RSA keys shorter than 3000 bits 2035-01-01, while SHA-256 and larger have no end
date. The signer key of the test PKI (RSA 3072, SHA-256) is therefore acceptable until the table is revised. The same
file declares `<Model Value="SHELL" />`, the same certificate validity model that iText uses. Treat those dates as the
state of the policy at the time of writing, not as law.

Two more knobs are worth knowing. The validator can be told how deep to go with `setValidationLevel`: `BASIC_SIGNATURES`,
`TIMESTAMPS`, `LONG_TERM_DATA` or `ARCHIVAL_DATA` (the default), which correspond to sections 5.3 to 5.6 of ETSI EN
319 102-1. And revocation data can come from sources set on the `CertificateVerifier` (`OnlineOCSPSource`,
`OnlineCRLSource`, offline sources), which are in `dss-service`; without them, only the data embedded in the file is used.

=== Trusted lists in DSS

DSS reads the same EU trusted lists with a `TLValidationJob` (module `dss-tsl-validation`). You describe the LOTL as a
`LOTLSource` with its URL and the certificates of the Official Journal in a `KeyStoreCertificateSource`, give the job a
cached data loader, run `onlineRefresh()` and pass the resulting `TrustedListsCertificateSource` to the
`CommonCertificateVerifier` as a trusted source. The sketch below was compiled against DSS 6.5 but not run, because it
needs a keystore with the Official Journal certificates that this page does not ship; its path and password come from
environment variables, and the maintained way to get that keystore, and the application code that schedules the
refresh, are in the DSS documentation chapter on `TLValidationJob`.

[source,java]
----
package example;

import eu.europa.esig.dss.service.http.commons.CommonsDataLoader;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.service.http.commons.FileCacheDataLoader;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.spi.tsl.TrustedListsCertificateSource;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.spi.validation.CommonCertificateVerifier;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.spi.x509.KeyStoreCertificateSource;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.tsl.job.TLValidationJob;
import eu.europa.esig.dss.tsl.source.LOTLSource;

import java.io.File;

public final class DssLotl {

    public static CommonCertificateVerifier verifierWithEuTrustedLists() throws Exception {
        KeyStoreCertificateSource officialJournalCerts = new KeyStoreCertificateSource(
                new File(System.getenv("OJ_KEYSTORE_PATH")), "PKCS12",
                System.getenv("OJ_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD").toCharArray());

        LOTLSource lotl = new LOTLSource();
        lotl.setUrl("https://ec.europa.eu/tools/lotl/eu-lotl.xml");
        lotl.setCertificateSource(officialJournalCerts);
        lotl.setPivotSupport(true);

        FileCacheDataLoader loader = new FileCacheDataLoader(new CommonsDataLoader());
        loader.setCacheExpirationTime(24L * 3600 * 1000);
        loader.setFileCacheDirectory(new File("dss-tl-cache"));

        TrustedListsCertificateSource trustedLists = new TrustedListsCertificateSource();
        TLValidationJob job = new TLValidationJob();
        job.setTrustedListCertificateSource(trustedLists);
        job.setOnlineDataLoader(loader);
        job.setListOfTrustedListSources(lotl);
        job.onlineRefresh();

        CommonCertificateVerifier verifier = new CommonCertificateVerifier();
        verifier.setTrustedCertSources(trustedLists);
        return verifier;
    }
}

== Adobe Acrobat and what the recipient sees

Whatever your library says, the customer will probably open the invoice in Adobe Acrobat or Acrobat Reader, and what
they see depends on settings of their installation, not on your code. Acrobat keeps its own store of trusted
certificates and uses three kinds of sources:

* *The Adobe Approved Trust List (AATL).* Certificates of authorities that Adobe has approved are trusted globally, and a
  signature whose credential chains to a member of the list gets a green check. Acrobat downloads the list from an Adobe
  server automatically, which can be disabled in Preferences, Trust Manager, or by an administrator.
  See https://helpx.adobe.com/acrobat/kb/approved-trust-list1.html[Adobe Approved Trust List Members].
* *The European Union Trusted Lists (EUTL).* Acrobat has built-in support for them and refreshes them on a schedule
  (Preferences, Trust Manager, Automatic European Union Trust Lists updates, Update Now). The validation panel
  can show a qualified-status result, and Adobe states on its page that Acrobat does not query the authoritative list
  at the moment of validation and that legal decisions should use a qualified validation service or the EU Trusted
  List Browser at https://eidas.ec.europa.eu/efda/tl-browser/. See Adobe's page
  https://helpx.adobe.com/document-cloud/kb/european-union-trust-lists.html[European Union Trusted Lists].
* *Certificates the user trusts manually.* A recipient can add the issuer certificate and choose what to trust it for
  (signatures, certified documents, dynamic content). Trusting a certificate as a root trusts everything issued under it,
  so this should be done with a certificate obtained through a channel other than the signed document. See
  https://helpx.adobe.com/acrobat/desktop/e-sign-documents/manage-digital-signatures/set-certificate-trust.html[Set certificate trust level in Acrobat].

For a closed ecosystem (your own customers, a private CA like the test CA), the practical procedure is to distribute
the CA certificate (not the signer's certificate) and tell recipients to add it as a trusted root for certified
documents and signatures, or to push it by policy in a managed environment. Without that step the signature appears
with a yellow warning or a question mark: the signature itself is intact, and the chain is not trusted, which is the
`INDETERMINATE` of the first iText run seen from the user's side.

The preferences under Preferences, Signatures (verifying signatures when a document is opened, using the time of an
expired timestamp, requiring revocation checking) change how strictly Acrobat judges. Adobe's help page
https://helpx.adobe.com/acrobat/desktop/e-sign-documents/manage-digital-signatures/set-preferences.html[Set digital signature validation preferences in Acrobat]
lists them. Two practical consequences: the same file can look different on two machines, so a support answer
cannot be given without knowing the recipient's settings; and "Acrobat says valid" is no substitute for server-side
validation when you need an audit trail. Adobe's internal definition of "LTV enabled", as recalled in the iText
Knowledge Base article on timestamps and LTV, is that everything needed to validate the file except root certificates is
contained in it, and that no timestamp is strictly required for that label.

The pages on Adobe Help block automated requests (a command line client received HTTP 403 for every address), so the
links were checked through a web search that returned them, not by fetching them; open them in a browser.

== Keeping signatures valid for decades

An invoice has to be kept for several years and some documents for much longer. The promise of a signature (this
content existed at this time and was approved by this key holder) must outlast the certificates, the revocation
services and the algorithms that supported it. Three things expire.

* The *signer certificate*: typically two or three years. After that, the signature is still good if the validator can
  show it was made while the certificate was valid, which needs a trusted timestamp.
* The *revocation services*: CRLs and OCSP responders are not required to keep answers for old certificates, so
  a validation in 2040 may not be able to find out what the status was in 2026 unless the answer was saved in the file.
* The *cryptography*: a hash function or a key size that is safe today is broken or deprecated within the retention
  period. SHA-1 is the history lesson (see the policy table above, and finding O11 of this section's source analysis:
  tutorials from the 2000s that sign with SHA-1 produce signatures that modern policies refuse for new signatures).

=== B-LT and B-LTA in practice

The PAdES baseline levels of xref:backend/pdf-generation/pades-baseline-levels.adoc[PAdES Baseline Levels] map to
these three problems. B-T adds a signature timestamp, which fixes the time of signing. B-LT embeds the validation
material (the certificates, and the CRL or OCSP answers for each) in a document security store, the `/DSS`
dictionary, so that the revocation question can be answered offline. B-LTA adds a *document timestamp* over the whole
file, including the DSS, so that the evidence itself has a proof of existence. Before the timestamp's own certificate
expires, or before its algorithm weakens, another document timestamp is added over everything, with fresh
validation data for the previous timestamp. This is the renewal, and because each timestamp is an incremental update
it does not disturb the earlier ones. A chain of document timestamps is what lets a validator in 2046 accept a signature
from 2026: it sees that the file existed in 2026 (first timestamp), still existed in 2031 when the algorithm was still
strong (second), and so on.

The Knowledge Base article on the relation of LTV and document timestamps, quoting a StackOverflow answer by mkl,
adds a historical note worth knowing: the PAdES part that defined timestamps and the part that defined LTV information
were written together, so some developers believe that adding a DSS requires a timestamp. Adobe's own statement, quoted
there, is that no timestamp is required for "LTV enabled", but that whenever a timestamp is added, validation
information for the timestamp must be added too. In an archive you want both.

iText offers `PdfPadesSigner` for the whole sequence, `+signWithBaselineLTAProfile(...)+`, and `prolongSignatures(ITSAClient)`
for the renewal of an existing B-LTA file; the Java API page is
https://api.itextpdf.com/iText/java/latest/com/itextpdf/signatures/PdfPadesSigner.html[PdfPadesSigner]. The example below
makes an LTA file with the test PKI and a public timestamp authority, then renews it. The timestamp server is
`https://freetsa.org/tsr`, a free service that needs no credentials and is suitable for tests only.

[source,java]
----
package example;

import com.itextpdf.kernel.pdf.PdfReader;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.ITSAClient;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.PdfPadesSigner;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.SignerProperties;
import com.itextpdf.signatures.TSAClientBouncyCastle;

import java.io.OutputStream;
import java.nio.file.Files;
import java.nio.file.Path;
import java.security.KeyStore;
import java.security.PrivateKey;
import java.security.Security;
import java.security.cert.Certificate;
import java.security.cert.X509Certificate;
import java.util.List;

import org.bouncycastle.jce.provider.BouncyCastleProvider;

public final class LtaRenewal {

    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        Security.addProvider(new BouncyCastleProvider());
        char[] storePassword = System.getenv("BILLING_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD").toCharArray();
        KeyStore store = KeyStore.getInstance("PKCS12");
        try (var in = Files.newInputStream(Path.of("pki/billing-signer.p12"))) {
            store.load(in, storePassword);
        }
        PrivateKey privateKey = (PrivateKey) store.getKey("billing-signer", storePassword);
        Certificate[] chain = store.getCertificateChain("billing-signer");
        ITSAClient tsa = new TSAClientBouncyCastle("https://freetsa.org/tsr");
        X509Certificate ca = ItextValidate.loadCertificate(Path.of("pki/billing-test-ca.pem"));

        try (PdfReader reader = new PdfReader("out/invoice.pdf");
             OutputStream out = Files.newOutputStream(Path.of("out/invoice-lta.pdf"))) {
            PdfPadesSigner signer = new PdfPadesSigner(reader, out)
                    .setCrlClient(new TestCrl())
                    .setTrustedCertificates(List.of(ca));
            signer.signWithBaselineLTAProfile(new SignerProperties().setFieldName("billing-signature"),
                    chain, privateKey, tsa);
        }

        // Later - for instance from a yearly job: add a fresh document timestamp.
        try (PdfReader reader = new PdfReader("out/invoice-lta.pdf");
             OutputStream out = Files.newOutputStream(Path.of("out/invoice-lta-renewed.pdf"))) {
            new PdfPadesSigner(reader, out).prolongSignatures(tsa);
        }
    }
}
----

The TSA call succeeded from the test machine and the files were produced. Validating the results with the public CA
certificate of the timestamp service added as a trust anchor (downloaded at run time from the service, not reproduced
here) gave different answers in the two libraries, and the difference is worth understanding rather than hiding:

[cols="2,2,3",options="header"]
|===
|File |iText level report |DSS simple report

|`invoice-lta.pdf` (after `signWithBaselineLTAProfile`)
|`B_T`; B-LT not reached: issuers and revocation data missing
|`TOTAL_PASSED`, format `PAdES-BASELINE-T`

|`invoice-lta-renewed.pdf` (after `prolongSignatures`)
|`B_T`; same reasons
|`TOTAL_PASSED`, format `PAdES-BASELINE-LTA`
|===

On this test PKI, `signWithBaselineLTAProfile` produced a file that DSS classifies as B-T, not B-LTA, because there was
nothing to embed for B-LT: neither the test CA nor the free timestamp service publishes a revocation endpoint that the
library can query, so the document security store could not be completed. After `prolongSignatures`, DSS recognises the
structure as B-LTA and accepts it, while iText still reports B-T and names the missing issuer certificates and revocation
data of the timestamp chain. Both reports are internally consistent; they apply different rules to a PKI that
does not support the long-term profile. In production, with a CA and a TSA that publish CRLs or OCSP, the same
two calls produce a file that both libraries should rate B-LTA, and the level report is how you check it in an
automated test: assert on `getDocumentLevel()` rather than assuming. This was not verified against a real CA for this
page. DSS can perform the same renewal with `PAdESService.extendDocument` and `PAdES_BASELINE_LTA` parameters plus an
`OnlineTSPSource` (an extra document timestamp was added to the test file this way, the count of timestamps in the
diagnostic data going from two to three); the details are in
xref:backend/pdf-generation/signing-with-pdfbox-and-eu-dss.adoc[Signing with PDFBox & EU DSS].

=== A renewal schedule

When to add the next document timestamp is a policy decision, and the inputs are all measurable:

* the notAfter date of the current timestamp authority's certificate (renew comfortably before it);
* the algorithm expiry dates in the policy you validate with (for example the digest of the last timestamp, and the key
  size of the timestamp authority);
* the revocation data retention you can rely on.

A pragmatic scheme for the *Bookshelf Billing* archive is a yearly batch job (the structure is described in
xref:backend/pdf-generation/generating-reports-from-data.adoc[Generating Reports from Databases & Services] and the Spring
Batch and scheduling pages it links to) that selects the archived invoices whose last document timestamp is older
than a year, or whose timestamp authority certificate expires within the next year, calls `prolongSignatures` or the DSS
equivalent, validates the result, and stores the validation report next to the file. Because each renewal adds
tens of kilobytes (the test file grew from about 63 KB to 96 KB), the storage cost of keeping thousands of invoices for
a decade should be estimated before choosing the interval. A separate approach is an evidence record (RFC 4998, an
Evidence Record Syntax) that renews many documents with one hash tree; DSS can validate those, but PDF readers do not
understand them, so they are for the archive, not for the customer's copy.

=== Algorithm expiry and migration

Algorithm expiry works through the same table that the policy uses. When a hash function reaches its end date, a
signature made with it remains acceptable only if a timestamp, made with a still-acceptable algorithm before that end
date, covers it. That is exactly why the document timestamp chain exists: each one lifts the protection of the previous
ones to the current algorithms. A file signed with SHA-1 in 2010 and never timestamped cannot be saved afterwards,
because there is no proof it existed before the algorithm was weak; this is the situation of every B-B signature without a
timestamp. The default digest of the helper above is SHA-256, which the bundled DSS policy has no end date for.

=== Post-quantum signatures

The larger risk is that RSA and elliptic-curve signatures, which every PDF signature in the world uses today, would be
breakable by a sufficiently powerful quantum computer, and that a signature that must stay credible for thirty years
is exposed to it. The countermeasures are signature algorithms standardised by NIST: ML-DSA (FIPS 204, from the
CRYSTALS-Dilithium family) and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205, based on SPHINCS+), with FN-DSA (Falcon) expected next. The iText
Knowledge Base article on PQC support states the status plainly, and it is worth repeating without embellishment:

* from iText Core 9.5.0, using Bouncy Castle, iText can generate and validate quantum-resistant signatures in a PDF,
  as a proof of concept;
* this is a pre-standard implementation: ISO 32000 does not yet define how to embed PQC signatures, so a PDF signed this
  way is not a standards-compliant PDF for that feature, and other processors such as Adobe Reader will not validate
  it; it is useful for iText-to-iText workflows and experiments only;
* it is not yet supported in the FIPS mode of Bouncy Castle;
* ML-DSA is the recommended starting point (moderate size, about 2 to 4 KB per the article), SLH-DSA signatures are very
  large (8 to 50 KB), and the article notes that Rainbow, which was an NIST candidate, was broken and must not be used.

What this means for planning, from the article and from the structure of the problem: keep signing with RSA 3072 or
ECDSA P-256 and SHA-256 for production today; make sure that every long-lived invoice has document timestamps so that a
later algorithm change can be bridged by a new timestamp; do not use pre-standard PQC signatures for documents that
customers must open in other tools; prepare the surrounding infrastructure (key storage, certificate authorities that can issue
PQC certificates, larger `/Contents` placeholders because PQC signatures are bigger, validators that understand the new
algorithms); and follow the PDF Association and ISO work to know when the format is final. This page does not show
a PQC signing example because the pre-standard result cannot be validated by anything but iText itself, and it would give
a false impression of readiness. The article links iText's sample code on GitHub for those who want to experiment.

Derived from https://kb.itextpdf.com/itext/post-quantum-cryptography-pqc-support-in-itext[Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) support in iText].
The NIST standards are https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/204/final[FIPS 204] and https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/205/final[FIPS 205].

== Practical advice

* *Decide what each result means in your application.* Write down what happens on `VALID`, `INDETERMINATE` and
  `INVALID`. Accepting `INDETERMINATE` silently and rejecting it silently are both bugs.
* *Validate on the server, with a policy you control.* The recipient's Acrobat is an opinion; your archive needs a
  repeatable verdict. Store the validation report (the DSS diagnostic data and the ETSI report, or the iText report)
  with the document and the validation time.
* *Keep trust anchors in configuration, not in the PDF.* A certificate embedded in the file is evidence to be
  checked, never a reason to trust.
* *Provide revocation data when you sign.* A file with no revocation data and a CA that will disappear is a file that
  will be `INDETERMINATE` forever. Use B-LT at least for documents with a retention period.
* *Add a document timestamp as the last step of anything that must be kept*, and plan the renewal as a job, not as a
  hope.
* *Test the failure modes.* The two tamper tests above are a few lines of code and should be in the test suite of any
  service that accepts or produces signed PDFs; see xref:backend/pdf-generation/itext-testing-and-performance.adoc[Testing & Performance with iText].
* *Mind the licences.* iText Core is AGPLv3 or commercial. DSS (LGPL-2.1) and PDFBox (Apache-2.0) are
  alternatives when the AGPL does not fit.
* *Do not send confidential files to online demonstrations.*

== Outdated points to avoid

. Validating with `PdfPKCS7.verify()` alone: it checks the integrity layer and says nothing about the chain,
  revocation, timestamps or later modifications. Use `SignatureValidator` or DSS.
. `PdfStamper.createSignature` and `adbe.pkcs7.sha1` signatures from the iText 1 and 2 era, and SHA-1 digests:
  policies reject them for new signatures (finding O11).
. Treating "the signature verifies" as "the document is valid": a verified signature with an untrusted chain, no
  revocation data or a forbidden later revision is not a valid document.
. Believing that an expired certificate invalidates earlier signatures. With a trusted timestamp (and a validator
  that uses it), signatures made before expiry stay valid, as the iText Knowledge Base explains.
. Assuming that "LTV enabled" requires a timestamp, or that a timestamp makes a file LTV enabled: they are different
  mechanisms that are best used together.
. Assuming Acrobat's green check is a legal conclusion. Adobe's own page on the EU trusted lists warns against it.
. Planning PQC around pre-standard signatures. They only interoperate with iText.
