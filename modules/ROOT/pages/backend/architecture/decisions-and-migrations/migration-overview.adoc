== The migration balancing act

Every migration decision sits at the intersection of two different kinds of pressure (Golding, ch. 13, "The
Migration Balancing Act"):

* **Business motivators** -- competitive pressure, the operational cost of running the current system, the need
  to enter a new market, or customers directly asking for capabilities the current architecture cannot deliver.
* **Technical desires** -- erasing accumulated technical debt, adopting a newer language or framework, or simply
  the engineering team's appetite for a cleaner design.

A migration chosen purely for the second reason, in a technical vacuum, tends to lose executive support the moment
it stops looking urgent to the business. The strategy -- what to migrate, in what order, and how fast -- has to be
driven by the business motivators; the technical desires ride along on whatever budget and priority the business
case earns, not the other way around.

== Timing: modernize first, or deliver and modernize incrementally

Two timing strategies compete for every migration (Golding, "Timing Considerations"):

* **Modernize first.** Pause new feature delivery, rebuild the target architecture, then resume delivering
  features on the new foundation.
* **Deliver now, modernize incrementally.** Keep shipping features on the legacy system while migrating it
  underneath, a slice at a time, using the techniques the path-specific pages describe.

In practice, "modernize first" rarely survives contact with the business: customers and the market do not pause
while an architecture is rebuilt, and early feedback from whatever ships first usually reveals that the target
design needs to change anyway. That feedback loop is what tips the balance toward incremental modernization for
almost every real migration -- not because it is architecturally purer, but because it is the only strategy that
keeps learning from real usage throughout the migration instead of only at the end.

=== The fish model

The **fish model** Golding borrows from Lah and Wood's *Technology-as-a-Service Playbook* ("What Kind of Fish Are
You?") names the cost-and-revenue shape a migration produces over time: costs rise as the team invests in the new
architecture while still operating the old one, then fall once the
legacy system is retired; revenue often dips first -- feature delivery slows while effort goes into the migration
-- before spiking once the new architecture unlocks capabilities the old one could not deliver.

spiking once the new architecture starts paying off. Naming which shape a given migration is likely to produce is
part of building the business case for it.

== No big bang rewrite

However the timing question is answered, one choice is off the table: rewriting the whole system from scratch and
cutting over in one release. As Richardson attributes to Martin Fowler, "the only thing a Big Bang rewrite
guarantees is a Big Bang" (Richardson, ch. 13.1.2) -- a single, high-risk cutover with months or years of
accumulated divergence between the old and new systems, tested only at the very end, with no way to walk any part
of it back independently.

The alternative every migration path in this section follows instead is the **strangler fig**, Martin Fowler's
`StranglerFigApplication`: named for the strangler fig tree, which grows around a host tree, gradually replacing
it, until the host can eventually be removed while the strangler continues standing on its own. Applied to
software, new functionality is built around the edges of the legacy system, one capability at a time, routed to
through the same seam -- typically the gateway --, until the legacy system has been reduced to nothing worth
keeping.

== Principles that hold across every path

A handful of principles recur, independent of which specific migration path applies:

* **Demonstrate value early and often.** Ship the first slice of the migration -- one extracted capability, one
  modernized module -- as soon as it is usable, rather than waiting for the whole migration to finish before
  anyone outside the team sees a result. Early wins are what keep the business case, and the team's own morale,
  intact through a migration that may run for months.
* **Minimize changes to the legacy system.** Every line changed in the system being migrated away from is a line
  that has to be tested, deployed, and risked on infrastructure that is on its way out. Prefer routing around the
  legacy system, or wrapping it, over modifying its internals -- each path-specific page names the least-invasive
  technique available for its situation.
* **Don't build the deployment infrastructure up front.** It is tempting to spend the first weeks of a migration
  building out the full target platform -- Kubernetes clusters, service meshes, the works -- before migrating
  anything. Richardson's guidance is narrower: the one piece of infrastructure a migration genuinely cannot live
  without is **an automated deployment pipeline with automated tests** (Richardson, ch. 13.1.2); everything else
  can be added incrementally, when a specific migrated slice actually needs it.
* **Define a time-boxed target architecture.** Spend on the order of a couple of weeks sketching where the
  migration is heading -- which capabilities become which services or modules, which technology stack replaces
  which -- and treat that sketch as a destination to aim for and revise as the migration teaches the team more,
  not as a spec to freeze and follow blindly.

== Five pitfalls every path warns about

Each path-specific page below calls out how its situation avoids these five pitfalls concretely; named once here,
they are the same five failure modes underlying every one of them:

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
| Pitfall | Why it derails a migration

| Big bang
| Replaces incremental, revertible progress with one high-risk cutover, tested only at the very end.

| Distributed monolith
| Splitting deployables without splitting their data or their release coupling produces all the operational cost
  of microservices with none of the independent-deployability benefit.

| Shared database
| Two services sharing one schema recreate the tight coupling a migration is meant to remove, just across a
  network call instead of a method call.

| Premature infrastructure investment
| Building the full target platform before any capability actually needs it burns budget and time the business
  case was not sized for, and often gets the platform wrong before real usage informs it.

| Forgetting the organization and process side
| A migration changes what teams own and how they release; treating it as a purely technical exercise leaves that
  organizational change unaddressed. This section covers the technical side of migration only -- team topology and process transformation are out of scope here.
|===
