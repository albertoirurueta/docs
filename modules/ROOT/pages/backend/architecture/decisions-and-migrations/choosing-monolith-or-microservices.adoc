= Choosing: Monolith or Microservices?
:description: A plain-language decision guide for when a monolith (modular from day one) is the right choice, when microservices earn their keep, and the signals that it is time to split -- or to merge back.
:keywords: monolith vs microservices, MonolithFirst, MicroservicePrerequisites, monolithic hell, architecture decision, Prime Video monolith, modular monolith

include::partial$decisions-and-migrations-disclaimer.adoc[]

xref:backend/architecture/decisions-and-migrations/architectural-styles.adoc[Architectural Styles] defined what a monolith, a modular
monolith, and microservices actually are, and
xref:backend/architecture/decisions-and-migrations/benefits-and-drawbacks.adoc[Benefits and Drawbacks of Microservices] named what each
style buys and what it costs. This page turns those trade-offs into a decision, using **Bookshop** -- the same
Spring Boot 4.1 monolith fronting Catalogue, Orders, Payments, Shipping, and Customers -- to ground both sides of
the call.

== Choose a monolith when

A monolith -- built as a **modular monolith** from day one, per
xref:backend/architecture/decisions-and-migrations/architectural-styles.adoc[Architectural Styles]'s enforced module boundaries, not as an
undifferentiated ball of packages -- is the right default in most of these situations:

* **The project is small.** A handful of screens and a handful of business rules do not need five services and
  five deployment pipelines to be maintainable.
* **The team is small.** If everyone building Bookshop still fits around one table, there is no team-autonomy
  problem for microservices to solve; the coordination cost microservices exist to remove is not there yet to
  remove.
* **The delivery is one-shot.** A prototype, a proof of concept, or a fixed-scope project that will be handed
  off and not iterated on for years does not benefit from independent deployability it will never exercise.
* **The application will not need to evolve much.** Stable requirements and a stable domain do not need the
  independent release cadence microservices are built around.
* **The business model itself is still being discovered.** Richardson is explicit about this: "a startup should
  almost certainly begin with a monolithic application" (*Microservices Patterns*, section 1.5.2) -- when the
  product itself might pivot next quarter, paying up front to decompose a domain model that might not survive
  contact with real customers is money spent on the wrong problem. Martin Fowler's
  https://martinfowler.com/bliki/MonolithFirst.html[MonolithFirst] makes the same argument: start monolithic,
  extract services once the boundaries have proven themselves against real usage, not before.
* **The domain is strictly transactional.** If most of Bookshop's core workflows genuinely need one ACID
  transaction across Order, Payment, and Shipment rows -- rather than being able to tolerate the eventual
  consistency xref:backend/architecture/architectural-patterns/sagas.adoc[Sagas] requires -- a single shared database inside
  one monolith keeps that guarantee for free.
* **The operational prerequisites for microservices are missing.** Fowler's
  https://martinfowler.com/bliki/MicroservicePrerequisites.html[MicroservicePrerequisites] names three: rapid
  provisioning of new infrastructure, basic monitoring, and a fully automated deployment pipeline. Running five
  services by hand, deployed by someone remoting into a server, is strictly worse than running one monolith the
  same way -- microservices only pay off once those prerequisites already exist.

== Choose microservices when

Microservices earn the cost xref:backend/architecture/decisions-and-migrations/benefits-and-drawbacks.adoc[Benefits and Drawbacks of
Microservices] lists when at least one of these is true:

* **The system is large, complex, and long-lived, built by several autonomous teams.** Once Bookshop has grown
  past one team's ability to hold the whole domain in their heads, splitting the codebase along the same lines
  the organization is already split along removes the coordination bottleneck a shared `bookshop.jar` and a
  shared release train impose.
* **Performance and scale reasons, when components have very different resource and scaling profiles.**
  Bookshop's Catalogue (memory-hungry full-text search, mostly read traffic) and Payments (CPU-light but
  latency-sensitive and must never be starved) want to scale on different axes of the
  xref:backend/architecture/decisions-and-migrations/architectural-styles.adoc[scale cube], to different instance types, on different
  schedules -- a single shared process cannot give either one what it needs without over-provisioning the other.
* **Fault isolation or independent release cadence is a business requirement**, not just a nice-to-have -- for
  example, Orders must keep taking new orders even if Shipping's carrier-integration code is misbehaving, or
  Orders needs to deploy ten times a day while Shipping deploys weekly without either one blocking the other.
* **Parts of the system genuinely need a different technology stack.** Bookshop's Recommendations service
  benefits from a different language and a different data store than the relational, Java-based core; forcing
  it to live inside `bookshop.jar` would mean forcing the whole monolith onto whatever compromise stack serves
  both jobs adequately and neither one well.
* **The product is consumer-facing or SaaS at scale.** High, spiky, unpredictable traffic against a
  customer-facing storefront is exactly the profile where independent, per-capability scalability stops being
  optional -- see xref:backend/architecture/decisions-and-migrations/migration-path-to-multi-tenant-saas.adoc[Migration Path: From
  Single-Tenant to Multi-Tenant SaaS] for how this plays out once Bookshop itself becomes a SaaS product.

== Is it really the architecture?

Before blaming the architecture, check whether the actual complaint is a **process** problem wearing an
architecture costume. Richardson makes this point directly (*Microservices Patterns*, section 13.1.1): slow
delivery and buggy releases are very often caused by manual testing, the absence of continuous integration, and
no automated deployment pipeline -- none of which microservices fix by themselves. Splitting a monolith with no
CI/CD into five services with no CI/CD does not produce five services that deploy faster and more safely; it
produces five services that are *each* slow and risky to deploy, with network calls between them added on top.
Fix the pipeline first. If delivery is still slow and releases are still risky once tests run on every commit and
deployment is one click (or fully automatic), *then* the architecture itself is a credible suspect.

== Signals it is time to split -- or to merge back

=== Signals it is time to split

If Bookshop's monolith is showing the **monolithic-hell** symptoms
xref:backend/architecture/decisions-and-migrations/benefits-and-drawbacks.adoc[Benefits and Drawbacks of Microservices] describes, that is
the signal to start extracting services rather than a theoretical concern:

* [ ] The edit-build-run-test loop has grown from seconds to minutes, and developers have stopped running the
  full test suite locally before committing.
* [ ] What used to be same-day deploys has become a monthly release train, because no one can be confident a
  change to one capability did not break another.
* [ ] Catalogue and Payments (or any two capabilities with genuinely different resource profiles) cannot be
  scaled independently, so one is chronically over-provisioned to keep the other healthy.
* [ ] A failure in one capability -- a memory leak in Catalogue's indexing, say -- takes the whole process down,
  including capabilities that were never broken.
* [ ] Upgrading a library, framework, or JVM version means testing and migrating the entire application at once,
  so upgrades keep getting deferred and the stack keeps falling further behind.
* [ ] New engineers need weeks to become productive because there is no capability small enough to understand
  end to end in a day.

=== Signals it is time to merge back

Splitting is not a one-way ratchet. Amazon's Prime Video team ran into the opposite problem with their
audio/video quality-monitoring service: a microservices-and-serverless pipeline that passed video frames between
several independently deployed components turned out to be dominated by the *cost of calling between components*
-- chatty inter-service calls, expensive serverless orchestration, and S3 round-trips for every frame -- rather
than by the monitoring logic itself. Merging the pipeline's components back into a single monolithic process, and
keeping data in memory instead of round-tripping it between services, cut their infrastructure cost by
approximately 90% at the same scale
(https://www.primevideotech.com/video-streaming/scaling-up-the-prime-video-audio-video-monitoring-service-and-reducing-costs-by-90[Prime
Video Tech]). The signal to watch for is the mirror image of the split checklist above: services that talk to
each other constantly and synchronously, where most of the per-request cost and latency is IPC and per-call
infrastructure overhead rather than actual work, and where the components were split along a line that does not
match any real team or scaling boundary. When that is the profile, folding the chattiest services back into one
deployable -- not "microservices were a mistake" as a blanket rule, but "these *particular* components were split
too fine for *this* workload" -- is a legitimate, and sometimes the correct, architectural move
(https://thenewstack.io/return-of-the-monolith-amazon-dumps-microservices-for-video-monitoring/[The New Stack --
Return of the Monolith]).

Bookshop's own path through this flowchart: a single small team, an unproven business model, and no automated
pipeline yet all point to a modular monolith -- exactly the starting point
xref:backend/architecture/decisions-and-migrations/architectural-styles.adoc[Architectural Styles] and
xref:backend/architecture/decisions-and-migrations/migration-path-monolith-to-modular-monolith.adoc[Migration Path: From a Monolith to a
Modular Monolith] describe, and exactly what later lets
xref:backend/architecture/decisions-and-migrations/migration-path-monolith-to-microservices.adoc[Migration Path: From a Monolith to
Microservices] extract Orders, Customers, Catalogue, and Payments once the team, traffic, and operational
maturity justify it.

| Prototype / one-shot delivery

| Small team, small product, stable domain
| Monolith (modular from day one)
| No team-autonomy problem to solve yet; module boundaries keep the door open

| Startup validating its business model
| Monolith (modular from day one)
| The domain boundaries are not proven yet; MonolithFirst

| Strictly transactional core domain
| Monolith
| Keeps single-database ACID transactions instead of sagas

| No automated deployment pipeline / monitoring yet

| Large, complex, long-lived, several autonomous teams
| Microservices

| Components with very different scaling/resource profiles

| Fault isolation or independent release cadence required
| Microservices
