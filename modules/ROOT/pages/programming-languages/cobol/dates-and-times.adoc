This page is written against ISO/IEC 1989:2023 (edition 3, January 2023; an edition 4 is upcoming, in DIS ballot), GnuCOBOL 3.2
(28 July 2023, the latest release) and IBM Enterprise COBOL for z/OS 6.5 (PTFs through September 2026), and was checked on

single warning (except the two examples that are deliberately wrong and say so) and run; the output and the diagnostics shown are real. The ISO standard is paywalled and was not read: the rules
come from the Enterprise COBOL manuals and the GnuCOBOL manuals, and where the two differ the page says so.

A date in a COBOL program is not a type: it is a number or a string that the program interprets. The language gives three
families of tools for working with that number, and the page follows them in turn:

* the `+ACCEPT ... FROM DATE|DAY|DAY-OF-WEEK|TIME+` statement, which is among the oldest features of the language and reads the system clock into a data item;
* `FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE` and the other intrinsic functions, which return four-digit years and the offset from UTC and can convert a calendar date into a number of days, add to it and convert it back;
* the ISO 8601 functions (`FORMATTED-DATE`, `FORMATTED-DATETIME`, `INTEGER-OF-FORMATTED-DATE` and their relatives), which read and write the text forms that web services and JSON use.

The examples use the layouts of the *Circulation* library back office, in particular `LOAN-DATE` in `LOANREC`, and the page ends with the
rule that computes when a book is due and how many days late it is. The general syntax of intrinsic functions, and the table of every
function, are in xref:programming-languages/cobol/intrinsic-functions.adoc[Intrinsic Functions]; the numeric items that hold the dates are
described in xref:programming-languages/cobol/picture-clause.adoc[PICTURE Clause] and
xref:programming-languages/cobol/usage-and-numeric-representation.adoc[USAGE and Numeric Representation].

A word about reproducibility. Anything that reads the clock produces a different result every time it is run, so the examples below are of
two kinds. Those that use fixed dates (a literal, a `VALUE` clause or a record from a file) print the same output on any day and any
machine. The two that read the clock (`accept-demo` and `live-clock`, and the last lines of `current-date`) are labelled "varies": the output
shown is what the run on 2026-10-20 printed, with a container whose time zone is UTC.

== ACCEPT FROM DATE, DAY, DAY-OF-WEEK and TIME

Format 2 of the `ACCEPT` statement (Enterprise COBOL Language Reference, _ACCEPT statement_; GnuCOBOL Programmer's Guide, _ACCEPT FROM DATE/TIME_)
moves the date or the time kept by the system into a receiving item. Nothing is read from a device: the source is one of six
*conceptual data items* that cannot be declared in the program and that behave as `USAGE DISPLAY` numbers, moved to the receiver by the rules of
`MOVE` (without `CORRESPONDING`).

[cols="2,1,1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Phrase |Implicit `PICTURE` |Layout |Example for 27 April 2003
|`DATE` |`9(6)` |yymmdd |`030427`
|`DATE YYYYMMDD` |`9(8)` |yyyymmdd |`20030427`
|`DAY` |`9(5)` |yyddd (day of the year) |`03117`
|`DAY YYYYDDD` |`9(7)` |yyyyddd |`2003117`
|`DAY-OF-WEEK` |`9` |1 (Monday) to 7 (Sunday) |a Sunday is `7`
|`TIME` |`9(8)` |hhmmsscc (hundredths of a second) |2:41 PM is `14410000`
|===

The `YYYYMMDD` and `YYYYDDD` forms came with COBOL 2002; Enterprise COBOL accepts them and so does GnuCOBOL. The receiver does not have to be a numeric
item: an alphanumeric item receives the digits as text, and an alphanumeric group item is allowed as the receiver. The `TIME` and
`DATE` receivers are therefore often declared as groups whose subordinate items pick the fields apart, as the first example does by redefining the time.

[source,cobol]
----
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. ACCEPT-DEMO.
      * ACCEPT ... FROM the clock: the values change on every run.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  WS-DATE-6            PIC 9(6).
       01  WS-DATE-8            PIC 9(8).
       01  WS-DAY-5             PIC 9(5).
       01  WS-DAY-7             PIC 9(7).
       01  WS-WEEKDAY           PIC 9.
       01  WS-TIME              PIC 9(8).
       01  WS-TIME-PARTS REDEFINES WS-TIME.
           05  WS-HOUR          PIC 99.
           05  WS-MINUTE        PIC 99.
           05  WS-SECOND        PIC 99.
           05  WS-HUNDREDTH     PIC 99.
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           ACCEPT WS-DATE-6 FROM DATE
           ACCEPT WS-DATE-8 FROM DATE YYYYMMDD
           ACCEPT WS-DAY-5 FROM DAY
           ACCEPT WS-DAY-7 FROM DAY YYYYDDD
           ACCEPT WS-WEEKDAY FROM DAY-OF-WEEK
           ACCEPT WS-TIME FROM TIME
           DISPLAY "DATE          " WS-DATE-6
           DISPLAY "DATE YYYYMMDD " WS-DATE-8
           DISPLAY "DAY           " WS-DAY-5
           DISPLAY "DAY YYYYDDD   " WS-DAY-7
           DISPLAY "DAY-OF-WEEK   " WS-WEEKDAY
           DISPLAY "TIME          " WS-TIME
           DISPLAY "  hour " WS-HOUR " minute " WS-MINUTE
                   " second " WS-SECOND " hundredths " WS-HUNDREDTH
           STOP RUN.
----

The program prints the clock in every format it supports. This output *varies* with every run: it is the run of 2026-10-20 at 13:04:21 UTC,
a Tuesday (`DAY-OF-WEEK` 2) and the 293rd day of 2026.

[source,text]
----
DATE          261020
DATE YYYYMMDD 20261020
DAY           26293
DAY YYYYDDD   2026293
DAY-OF-WEEK   2
TIME          13095666
  hour 13 minute 09 second 56 hundredths 66
----

Source: https://gnucobol.sourceforge.io/HTML/gnucobpg.html#ACCEPT-FROM-DATE_002fTIME[GnuCOBOL Programmer's Guide, ACCEPT FROM DATE/TIME]

The same program also compiled and ran under `-std=ibm` and printed the same kinds of values. Three habits make the statement safer:

* *Take the date once.* `ACCEPT FROM DATE` followed by `ACCEPT FROM TIME` are two reads of the clock. A job that runs at 23:59:59 can get yesterday's date and tomorrow's
  time. Reading one `FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE` (next section) returns a consistent date and time.
* *Prefer the four-digit forms.* `ACCEPT FROM DATE` and `ACCEPT FROM DAY` return two-digit years, so `990101` and `000101` do not sort in time order. The
  `YYYYMMDD` and `YYYYDDD` phrases, or `CURRENT-DATE`, return the century.
* *Remember that this is local time.* `DATE` and `TIME` are the system's local time, which with GnuCOBOL follows the `TZ` environment variable of the process and on z/OS follows the system's time settings. A server in another time zone reports another day for the same instant.

== FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE

`FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE` takes no arguments and returns a 21-character alphanumeric value, so a single `MOVE` fills a group that names the pieces. The
layout is defined by the standard and is the same in Enterprise COBOL and GnuCOBOL:

[cols="1,1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Positions |Length |Contents
|1-4 |4 |year in the Gregorian calendar
|5-6 |2 |month, 01 to 12
|7-8 |2 |day of the month, 01 to 31
|9-10 |2 |hours past midnight, 00 to 23
|11-12 |2 |minutes, 00 to 59
|13-14 |2 |seconds, 00 to 59
|15-16 |2 |hundredths of a second (`00` where the system has no fractions)
|17 |1 |`-` if local time is behind Greenwich mean time, `pass:c[+]` if the same or ahead, `0` if the offset is not available
|18-19 |2 |hours of the offset (00 to 12 behind, 00 to 13 ahead)
|20-21 |2 |additional minutes of the offset (00 to 59)
|===

Enterprise COBOL's manual works the example of 15 February 1995, 05:14:27.81 in Eastern Standard Time, where the value is `1995021505142781-0500`. The
following program redefines the 21 characters in a group, parses that literal (so that its first part prints the same every day) and then reads the live clock. The last
lines vary.

[source,cobol]
----
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. CURRENT-DATE-DEMO.
      * Layout of FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE (21 characters).
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  WS-STAMP.
           05  WS-DATE.
               10  WS-YEAR      PIC 9(4).
               10  WS-MONTH     PIC 9(2).
               10  WS-DAY       PIC 9(2).
           05  WS-TIME.
               10  WS-HOUR      PIC 9(2).
               10  WS-MINUTE    PIC 9(2).
               10  WS-SECOND    PIC 9(2).
               10  WS-HUNDREDTH PIC 9(2).
           05  WS-OFFSET.
               10  WS-OFFSET-SIGN    PIC X.
               10  WS-OFFSET-HOURS   PIC 9(2).
               10  WS-OFFSET-MINUTES PIC 9(2).
       01  WS-RUN-DATE          PIC 9(8).
       01  WS-LENGTH            PIC 99.
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
      *    A fixed 21-character value: 15 Feb 1995, 05:14:27.81, UTC-5.
           MOVE "1995021505142781-0500" TO WS-STAMP
           DISPLAY "year " WS-YEAR " month " WS-MONTH " day " WS-DAY
           DISPLAY "time " WS-HOUR ":" WS-MINUTE ":" WS-SECOND "."
                   WS-HUNDREDTH
           DISPLAY "offset " WS-OFFSET-SIGN WS-OFFSET-HOURS ":"
                   WS-OFFSET-MINUTES
      *    The live clock: this part changes on every run.
           MOVE FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE TO WS-STAMP
           DISPLAY "now  " WS-STAMP
           MOVE FUNCTION LENGTH(FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE) TO WS-LENGTH
           DISPLAY "length " WS-LENGTH
           MOVE FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE(1:8) TO WS-RUN-DATE
           DISPLAY "run date " WS-RUN-DATE
           STOP RUN.
----

[source,text]
----
year 1995 month 02 day 15
time 05:14:27.81
offset -05:00
now  2026102013095712+0000
length 21
run date 20261020
----

Source: https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/SS6SG3_6.5/pdf/lrmvs.pdf[Enterprise COBOL for z/OS 6.5 Language Reference, CURRENT-DATE]

Two details are visible in the output. The live line ends in `pass:c[+0000]` because the container runs in UTC; running the same binary with `TZ=America/New_York` printed
`-0400` at that moment (daylight saving time), so the offset really follows the time zone of the process. And the reference-modified form
`FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE(1:8)` gives the `yyyymmdd` run date directly, which is how the nightly *Circulation* run obtains "today". The
offset in positions 17 to 21 is the difference between local time and Greenwich mean time, which is why a value stored with it can be
reduced to UTC later; the formatted functions below return the same information in ISO 8601 form.

== Integer dates: arithmetic on calendar dates

Subtracting two `yyyymmdd` numbers does not give a number of days, because the digits are a calendar and not a count. The standard solves this with
an *integer date*: the number of days since a fixed day zero, which can be added to and subtracted from, and converted back. Day 1 is Monday 1
January 1601 (in the Gregorian calendar, reckoned backwards), and the range is integer 1 (16010101) to 3,067,671 (99991231). The functions are:

[cols="2,2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Function |Argument |Result
|`INTEGER-OF-DATE` |standard date `yyyymmdd` |integer date
|`DATE-OF-INTEGER` |integer date |standard date `yyyymmdd`
|`INTEGER-OF-DAY` |Julian date `yyyyddd` |integer date
|`DAY-OF-INTEGER` |integer date |Julian date `yyyyddd`
|`TEST-DATE-YYYYMMDD` |an integer |0 if valid, 1 bad year, 2 bad month, 3 bad day
|`TEST-DAY-YYYYDDD` |an integer |0 if valid, 1 bad year, 2 bad day of the year
|===

The next program does the usual things: converts a date to an integer, adds 14 days (the loan period used below), converts back, gets the Julian form, finds the weekday
and counts days between two dates. It prints the same output on any day because every date is fixed.

[source,cobol]
----
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. INTEGER-DATES.
      * Integer dates: day 1 is Monday 1 January 1601.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  WS-DATE              PIC 9(8) VALUE 20261020.
       01  WS-JULIAN            PIC 9(7).
       01  WS-INT               PIC 9(7).
       01  WS-OUT               PIC 9(8).
       01  WS-OUT-JULIAN        PIC 9(7).
       01  WS-WEEKDAY           PIC 9.
       01  WS-DAYS              PIC S9(5).
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           COMPUTE WS-INT = FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(WS-DATE)
           DISPLAY "integer date of " WS-DATE " = " WS-INT
           COMPUTE WS-OUT = FUNCTION DATE-OF-INTEGER(WS-INT + 14)
           DISPLAY "14 days later          = " WS-OUT
           COMPUTE WS-OUT-JULIAN = FUNCTION DAY-OF-INTEGER(WS-INT)
           DISPLAY "Julian form            = " WS-OUT-JULIAN
           MOVE WS-OUT-JULIAN TO WS-JULIAN
           COMPUTE WS-INT = FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DAY(WS-JULIAN)
           DISPLAY "back from Julian       = " WS-INT
           COMPUTE WS-WEEKDAY = FUNCTION MOD(WS-INT - 1, 7) + 1
           DISPLAY "day of week (1=Mon)    = " WS-WEEKDAY
           COMPUTE WS-DAYS = FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(20261231)
                           - FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(20261020)
           DISPLAY "days to 31 Dec 2026    = " WS-DAYS
           DISPLAY "day 1                  = "
                   FUNCTION DATE-OF-INTEGER(1)
           DISPLAY "day 3067671            = "
                   FUNCTION DATE-OF-INTEGER(3067671)
      *    29 February: only leap years have it.
           DISPLAY "2028-02-28 + 1         = "
                   FUNCTION DATE-OF-INTEGER(
                       FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(20280228) + 1)
           DISPLAY "2100-02-28 + 1         = "
                   FUNCTION DATE-OF-INTEGER(
                       FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(21000228) + 1)
           STOP RUN.
----

[source,text]
----
integer date of 20261020 = 0155521
14 days later          = 20261103
Julian form            = 2026293
back from Julian       = 0155521
day of week (1=Mon)    = 2
days to 31 Dec 2026    = +00072
day 1                  = 16010101
day 3067671            = 99991231
2028-02-28 + 1         = 20280229
2100-02-28 + 1         = 21000301
----

Source: https://gnucobol.sourceforge.io/HTML/gnucobpg.html#Intrinsic-Functions[GnuCOBOL Programmer's Guide, Intrinsic Functions (INTEGER-OF-DATE and DATE-OF-INTEGER)]

The output shows the leap-year rule at work: 2028 has a 29 February and 2100, being a multiple of 100 and not of 400, does not. The
weekday expression `pass:c[MOD(integer - 1, 7) + 1]` gives 1 for Monday to 7 for Sunday, the same numbering as `ACCEPT FROM DAY-OF-WEEK`; it is valid
because day 1 is a Monday, and it needs care in Enterprise COBOL, where the `INTDATE` option can move the origin (see the table below).

=== Validate before converting

The conversion functions require a valid argument (the Enterprise COBOL manual states the valid forms and ranges, and `TEST-DATE-YYYYMMDD` exists to check them). The test function returns *which part* is wrong, in the order year, month, day, so a rejection message can be specific. Here are
six candidates, the Julian test, and what GnuCOBOL's `INTEGER-OF-DATE` does with an invalid date that nobody tested.

[source,cobol]
----
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. TEST-DATES.
      * Validating dates before using them.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  WS-CANDIDATES.
           05  FILLER PIC 9(8) VALUE 20261020.
           05  FILLER PIC 9(8) VALUE 20260230.
           05  FILLER PIC 9(8) VALUE 20261320.
           05  FILLER PIC 9(8) VALUE 15991231.
           05  FILLER PIC 9(8) VALUE 20280229.
           05  FILLER PIC 9(8) VALUE 21000229.
       01  WS-TABLE REDEFINES WS-CANDIDATES.
           05  WS-CANDIDATE     PIC 9(8) OCCURS 6 TIMES.
       01  WS-I                 PIC 9.
       01  WS-RESULT            PIC 9.
       01  WS-JULIAN-RESULT     PIC 9.
       01  WS-BAD               PIC 9(8) VALUE 20260230.
       01  WS-INT               PIC 9(7).
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           PERFORM VARYING WS-I FROM 1 BY 1 UNTIL WS-I > 6
               COMPUTE WS-RESULT =
                   FUNCTION TEST-DATE-YYYYMMDD(WS-CANDIDATE(WS-I))
               DISPLAY WS-CANDIDATE(WS-I) " -> " WS-RESULT
           END-PERFORM
           COMPUTE WS-JULIAN-RESULT = FUNCTION TEST-DAY-YYYYDDD(2027366)
           DISPLAY "Julian 2027366 -> " WS-JULIAN-RESULT
           COMPUTE WS-JULIAN-RESULT = FUNCTION TEST-DAY-YYYYDDD(2028366)
           DISPLAY "Julian 2028366 -> " WS-JULIAN-RESULT
      *    An unchecked invalid date reaches INTEGER-OF-DATE.
           COMPUTE WS-INT = FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(WS-BAD)
           DISPLAY "INTEGER-OF-DATE(20260230) = " WS-INT
           STOP RUN.
----

[source,text]
----
20261020 -> 0
20260230 -> 3
20261320 -> 2
15991231 -> 1
20280229 -> 0
21000229 -> 3
Julian 2027366 -> 2
Julian 2028366 -> 0
INTEGER-OF-DATE(20260230) = 0000000
----

Source: https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/SS6SG3_6.5/pdf/lrmvs.pdf[Enterprise COBOL for z/OS 6.5 Language Reference, TEST-DATE-YYYYMMDD and TEST-DAY-YYYYDDD]

The returned codes match the manual: 15991231 is year 1 (below 1601), 20261320 is month 2 and 20260230 is day 3. The last line is the trap: GnuCOBOL does not stop for
`20260230`; it returns `0`, which a program that computes with it treats as "no such day". The Enterprise COBOL function descriptions state the valid range and do not promise a result outside it, so the portable rule is the same in both: call
`TEST-DATE-YYYYMMDD` first and reject the record.

=== Where integer day 1 is: INTDATE

On z/OS the compiler option `INTDATE(ANSI)` (the default) makes day 1 January 1, 1601, as above. `INTDATE(LILIAN)` makes day 1 October 15, 1582, the
origin used by Language Environment's own date services. The Programming Guide tabulates which combinations agree:

[cols="2,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Option |Intrinsic function |Language Environment service
|`INTDATE(ANSI)` |`INTEGER-OF-DATE` |compatible with `CEECBLDY`; not with `CEEDAYS`
|`INTDATE(ANSI)` |`DATE-OF-INTEGER`, `DAY-OF-INTEGER` |not compatible with `CEEDATE`
|`INTDATE(LILIAN)` |`INTEGER-OF-DATE` |compatible with `CEEDAYS`; not with `CEECBLDY`
|`INTDATE(LILIAN)` |`DATE-OF-INTEGER`, `DAY-OF-INTEGER` |compatible with `CEEDATE`
|===

A program that exchanges integer dates with other programs or with those services must say which origin it uses, and the difference of two integer dates (a
count of days) is the same in both. GnuCOBOL has no `INTDATE` option: its integer dates always start at 1600-12-31, like `INTDATE(ANSI)`. These are z/OS facts and are
not run here.

== The ISO 8601 date and time functions

The standard of 2014 added a second family that reads and writes dates as text in ISO 8601 shapes. Enterprise COBOL 6.5 lists it among the 2002/2014
standard features it implements; the outline for this section dates it to the 6.3 service stream, a release this page could not re-check, so it names no release. GnuCOBOL 3.2
implements the family too. The functions are:

[cols="2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Function |Purpose
|`FORMATTED-DATE(format, integer-date)` |integer date to text in a date format
|`FORMATTED-TIME(format, seconds [, offset-minutes])` |seconds past midnight to text in a time format
|`FORMATTED-DATETIME(format, integer-date, seconds [, offset-minutes])` |both, to text in a combined format
|`FORMATTED-CURRENT-DATE(format)` |the current date and time in a combined format
|`INTEGER-OF-FORMATTED-DATE(format, text)` |text in a date (or combined) format to an integer date
|`SECONDS-FROM-FORMATTED-TIME(format, text)` |text in a time (or combined) format to seconds past midnight
|`TEST-FORMATTED-DATETIME(format, text)` |0 if the text fits the format, otherwise the position of the first bad character
|`COMBINED-DATETIME(integer-date, seconds)` |one number: the integer date, a decimal point and the seconds as five digits
|`SECONDS-PAST-MIDNIGHT` |the current local time of day in seconds
|===

The *format* is an alphanumeric (or national, or UTF-8) literal built from the strings in the Language Reference. Date formats: `YYYYMMDD` and
`YYYY-MM-DD` (calendar), `YYYYDDD` and `YYYY-DDD` (ordinal, the day of the year) and `YYYYWwwD` and `YYYY-Www-D` (ISO week date: year, week 01 to 52, day 1 to 7). Time formats:
`hhmmss` and `hh:mm:ss`, a fraction after the seconds written `.s` to `.sssssssss` (one to nine digits), the suffix `Z` for UTC, and the offset forms
`pass:c[hhmmss+hhmm]` and `pass:c[hh:mm:ss+hh:mm]`. A combined format joins a date format and a time format with an uppercase `T`, both
in the basic or both in the extended style: `pass:c[YYYY-MM-DDThh:mm:ss+hh:mm]` is allowed and `YYYYMMDDThh:mm:ss` is not.

The program below formats a fixed date and time (14:30:25 is 52,225 seconds past midnight), shows the same instant as UTC for a time zone two hours
ahead (offset 120 minutes), and parses text back. The `TEST-FORMATTED-DATETIME` results are positions: month 13 is wrong at character 7 of
`2026-13-03`, and 0 means valid.

[source,cobol]
----
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. FORMATTED-DEMO.
      * ISO 8601 style date and time functions (Enterprise COBOL 6.x,
      * GnuCOBOL 3.2). Every value here is fixed, so the output is too.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  WS-INT               PIC 9(7).
       01  WS-TEXT              PIC X(32).
       01  WS-SECONDS           PIC 9(5)V99.
       01  WS-CHECK             PIC 99.
       01  WS-COMBINED          PIC 9(7)V9(5).
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           COMPUTE WS-INT = FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(20261020)
           MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-DATE("YYYY-MM-DD", WS-INT)
               TO WS-TEXT
           DISPLAY "extended calendar : " WS-TEXT
           MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-DATE("YYYYDDD", WS-INT) TO WS-TEXT
           DISPLAY "basic ordinal     : " WS-TEXT
           MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-DATE("YYYY-Www-D", WS-INT)
               TO WS-TEXT
           DISPLAY "extended week     : " WS-TEXT
           MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-TIME("hh:mm:ss", 52225) TO WS-TEXT
           DISPLAY "time of day       : " WS-TEXT
           MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-DATETIME(
                    "YYYY-MM-DDThh:mm:ss", WS-INT, 52225)
               TO WS-TEXT
           DISPLAY "date and time     : " WS-TEXT
           MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-DATETIME(
                    "YYYY-MM-DDThh:mm:ssZ", WS-INT, 52225, 120)
               TO WS-TEXT
           DISPLAY "in UTC (+02:00)   : " WS-TEXT
           MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-DATETIME(
                    "YYYY-MM-DDThh:mm:ss+hh:mm", WS-INT, 52225, 120)
               TO WS-TEXT
           DISPLAY "with offset       : " WS-TEXT
      *    And back again.
           COMPUTE WS-INT = FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-FORMATTED-DATE(
                    "YYYY-MM-DD", "2026-11-03")
           DISPLAY "integer of text   : " WS-INT
           COMPUTE WS-SECONDS = FUNCTION SECONDS-FROM-FORMATTED-TIME(
                    "hh:mm:ss", "14:30:25")
           DISPLAY "seconds           : " WS-SECONDS
           COMPUTE WS-CHECK = FUNCTION TEST-FORMATTED-DATETIME(
                    "YYYY-MM-DD", "2026-13-03")
           DISPLAY "bad month at char : " WS-CHECK
           COMPUTE WS-CHECK = FUNCTION TEST-FORMATTED-DATETIME(
                    "YYYY-MM-DD", "2026-11-03")
           DISPLAY "good date         : " WS-CHECK
           COMPUTE WS-COMBINED = FUNCTION COMBINED-DATETIME(
                    155521, 52225)
           DISPLAY "combined          : " WS-COMBINED
           STOP RUN.
----

[source,text]
----
extended calendar : 2026-10-20                      
basic ordinal     : 2026293                         
extended week     : 2026-W43-2                      
time of day       : 14:30:25                        
date and time     : 2026-10-20T14:30:25             
in UTC (+02:00)   : 2026-10-20T12:30:25Z            
with offset       : 2026-10-20T14:30:25+02:00       
integer of text   : 0155535
seconds           : 52225.00
bad month at char : 07
good date         : 00
combined          : 0155521.52225
----

Source: https://gnucobol.sourceforge.io/HTML/gnucobpg.html#FORMATTED_002dDATETIME[GnuCOBOL Programmer's Guide, FORMATTED-DATETIME and the other formatted date functions]

Notes on the output. `2026-W43-2` is the ISO week date: week 43, Tuesday. With a `Z` format the result is the time *converted* to UTC (12:30:25 for 14:30:25 at +02:00); with an
offset format the time is shown as given and the offset is appended. `COMBINED-DATETIME(155521, 52225)` is the integer date, a decimal point and the seconds
padded to five digits. `INTEGER-OF-FORMATTED-DATE("YYYY-MM-DD", "2026-11-03")` returns 155535, fourteen days after 155521, the figure that the due date calculation below also reaches.

The functions that read the clock end the family. `FORMATTED-CURRENT-DATE` takes a combined format and returns the current date and time in it, and `SECONDS-PAST-MIDNIGHT` returns the
local time of day as a number that supports arithmetic. The next program uses both and also computes the seconds between 23:55 and 00:05, with the extra step that crossing midnight
requires. Its first three lines *vary* with every run.

[source,cobol]
----
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.

      * Clock-dependent functions: the output differs on every run.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  WS-STAMP             PIC X(32).

       01  WS-END               PIC 9(5)V99 VALUE 300.
       01  WS-ELAPSED           PIC 9(5)V99.
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-CURRENT-DATE(
                    "YYYY-MM-DDThh:mm:ss.ss+hh:mm") TO WS-STAMP
           DISPLAY "formatted now    : " WS-STAMP
           MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-CURRENT-DATE("YYYYMMDDThhmmssZ")
               TO WS-STAMP
           DISPLAY "formatted (UTC)  : " WS-STAMP
           COMPUTE WS-NOW = FUNCTION SECONDS-PAST-MIDNIGHT
           DISPLAY "seconds past midnight : " WS-NOW
      *    Elapsed time across midnight: 23:55:00 to 00:05:00.
           IF WS-END < WS-START
               COMPUTE WS-ELAPSED = 86400 - WS-START + WS-END
           ELSE
               COMPUTE WS-ELAPSED = WS-END - WS-START
           END-IF
           DISPLAY "elapsed seconds  : " WS-ELAPSED
           STOP RUN.
----

=== Format literals are checked at compile time

Because the format is described by a literal, the compiler can check it. A format that is not in the tables is an error, not a runtime surprise. This
deliberately wrong program uses a slash as the separator:

[source,cobol]
----
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. BAD-FORMAT.
      * Deliberately wrong: a format literal that is not in the tables.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  WS-TEXT              PIC X(12).
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-DATE("YYYY/MM/DD", 155521)
               TO WS-TEXT
           DISPLAY WS-TEXT
           STOP RUN.
----

GnuCOBOL refuses to compile it:

[source,text]
----

    7 |        PROCEDURE DIVISION.
    8 |        MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
    9 >            MOVE FUNCTION FORMATTED-DATE("YYYY/MM/DD", 155521)
   10 |                TO WS-TEXT
   11 |            DISPLAY WS-TEXT

Source: https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/SS6SG3_6.5/pdf/lrmvs.pdf[Enterprise COBOL for z/OS 6.5 Language Reference, Date and time formats]

== Computing a due date and days overdue in Circulation

The *Circulation* rule is simple: a book is due 14 days after the checkout, and every day after the due date costs a daily fine, with a maximum per loan. The
steps are the ones the page has built: validate the loan date, convert it to an integer date, add the loan period, convert back to print, subtract from the run date's integer
to count the days late (never below zero), and multiply. The run date is a fixed 20261105 so that the output is reproducible; a real run would replace its `VALUE` clause with
`MOVE FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE(1:8) TO WS-AS-OF`, or, better, would read it from a control card so that a rerun after a failure uses the original date.
The daily fine (0.25) and its cap (10.00) are values invented for the example.

A second sample file has five transactions chosen to hit the interesting cases: an ordinary loan, a loan from February whose due date is long past, a loan on the last day
of the year (whose due date falls in the next year), a loan with an impossible date and a return.

[source,text]
----
O00010000000120261020
O00010100000220260227
O00010000000320261231
O00010200000420260230
R00010000000120261029
----

The record layout is `LOANREC`, 30 bytes: type, member, book, `LOAN-DATE` and nine bytes of filler.

[source,cobol]
----
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. OVERDUE.
      * Circulation: due date and days overdue for checkouts.
       ENVIRONMENT DIVISION.
       INPUT-OUTPUT SECTION.
       FILE-CONTROL.
           SELECT LOANTRAN ASSIGN TO "LOANTRAN2"
               ORGANIZATION IS LINE SEQUENTIAL
               FILE STATUS IS WS-LOAN-STATUS.
       DATA DIVISION.
       FILE SECTION.
       FD  LOANTRAN.
           COPY LOANREC.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  WS-LOAN-STATUS       PIC XX.
           88  LOAN-OK          VALUE "00".
           88  LOAN-EOF         VALUE "10".
       01  WS-LOAN-DAYS         PIC 9(3) VALUE 14.
       01  WS-DAILY-FINE        PIC 9V99 VALUE 0.25.
       01  WS-MAX-FINE          PIC 99V99 VALUE 10.00.
      * Fixed "today", so that the output below is reproducible.
      * A real run would use FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE(1:8) instead.
       01  WS-AS-OF             PIC 9(8) VALUE 20261105.
       01  WS-AS-OF-INT         PIC 9(7).
       01  WS-LOAN-INT          PIC 9(7).
       01  WS-DUE-INT           PIC 9(7).
       01  WS-DUE-DATE          PIC 9(8).
       01  WS-OVERDUE           PIC 9(5).
       01  WS-FINE              PIC 99V99.
       01  WS-FINE-OUT          PIC Z9.99.
       01  WS-OVERDUE-OUT       PIC ZZ9.
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           COMPUTE WS-AS-OF-INT = FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(WS-AS-OF)
           DISPLAY "Circulation run date " WS-AS-OF
           OPEN INPUT LOANTRAN
           PERFORM READ-LOAN
           PERFORM UNTIL LOAN-EOF
               EVALUATE TRUE
                   WHEN NOT LOAN-CHECKOUT
                       DISPLAY "member " LOAN-MEMBER-ID " book "
                               LOAN-BOOK-ID " returned " LOAN-DATE
                   WHEN FUNCTION TEST-DATE-YYYYMMDD(LOAN-DATE) NOT = 0
                       DISPLAY "member " LOAN-MEMBER-ID " book "
                               LOAN-BOOK-ID " REJECTED, bad date "
                               LOAN-DATE
                   WHEN OTHER
                       PERFORM CHECK-OVERDUE
               END-EVALUATE
               PERFORM READ-LOAN
           END-PERFORM
           CLOSE LOANTRAN
           STOP RUN.
       READ-LOAN.
           READ LOANTRAN
               AT END SET LOAN-EOF TO TRUE
           END-READ.
       CHECK-OVERDUE.
           COMPUTE WS-LOAN-INT = FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(LOAN-DATE)
           COMPUTE WS-DUE-INT = WS-LOAN-INT + WS-LOAN-DAYS
           MOVE FUNCTION DATE-OF-INTEGER(WS-DUE-INT) TO WS-DUE-DATE
           IF WS-AS-OF-INT > WS-DUE-INT
               COMPUTE WS-OVERDUE = WS-AS-OF-INT - WS-DUE-INT
           ELSE
               MOVE ZERO TO WS-OVERDUE
           END-IF
           COMPUTE WS-FINE = FUNCTION MIN(WS-OVERDUE * WS-DAILY-FINE,
                                          WS-MAX-FINE)
           MOVE WS-OVERDUE TO WS-OVERDUE-OUT
           MOVE WS-FINE TO WS-FINE-OUT
           DISPLAY "member " LOAN-MEMBER-ID " book " LOAN-BOOK-ID
                   " out " LOAN-DATE " due " WS-DUE-DATE
                   " overdue " WS-OVERDUE-OUT " days, fine "
                   WS-FINE-OUT.
----

(see xref:programming-languages/cobol/accept-display-and-console-io.adoc[ACCEPT, DISPLAY and Console I/O]).

=== The mistake this replaces

The wrong way is to subtract the two `yyyymmdd` numbers. It looks plausible, and it is right only inside one month. This deliberately wrong program does it next to the right way:

[source,cobol]
----
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. DATE-SUBTRACTION.
      * Deliberately wrong: subtracting yyyymmdd numbers.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  WS-LOAN              PIC 9(8) VALUE 20261020.
       01  WS-AS-OF             PIC 9(8) VALUE 20261105.
       01  WS-WRONG             PIC S9(8).
       01  WS-RIGHT             PIC S9(8).
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           COMPUTE WS-WRONG = WS-AS-OF - WS-LOAN
           COMPUTE WS-RIGHT = FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(WS-AS-OF)
                            - FUNCTION INTEGER-OF-DATE(WS-LOAN)
           DISPLAY "yyyymmdd subtraction : " WS-WRONG
           DISPLAY "integer dates        : " WS-RIGHT
           STOP RUN.
----

[source,text]
----
yyyymmdd subtraction : +00000085
integer dates        : +00000016
----

Source: https://gnucobol.sourceforge.io/HTML/gnucobpg.html#Intrinsic-Functions[GnuCOBOL Programmer's Guide, Intrinsic Functions (INTEGER-OF-DATE)]

From 20 October to 5 November is 16 days, and the subtraction of the raw numbers says 85, because 20261105 - 20261020 is a difference between two
digit strings that skips the whole gap between 31 and 100 in the month position. The compiler reports nothing, and a fine computed from it is wrong
without anyone being told. Dates are converted to integer dates before they are subtracted or compared for distance (a plain `IF DATE-A > DATE-B` on two
`yyyymmdd` values is fine for ordering).

== Two-digit years and windowing (legacy)

Everything above uses four-digit years. Old programs, and records that still carry a `yymmdd` field, do not. Before the year 2000 the usual
way to save two digits per date was `+ACCEPT ... FROM DATE+`, and the Y2K work of the late 1990s was largely the job of finding such fields. The two
practical ways out were to *expand* the field to four digits (the permanent fix) or to *window* it: keep two digits and decide the century from a pivot. The standard
has intrinsic functions for the second approach, which are the only part of the old Y2K tooling that is still current:

[cols="2,2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Function |Argument |Returns
|`YEAR-TO-YYYY(yy [, window])` |two-digit year |four-digit year
|`DATE-TO-YYYYMMDD(yymmdd [, window])` |six-digit date |`yyyymmdd`
|`DAY-TO-YYYYDDD(yyddd [, window])` |five-digit Julian date |`yyyyddd`
|===

The *window* argument, default 50, is added to the year at execution time to give the last year of a 100-year interval into which the two-digit year must fall. The IBM manual's table makes the sliding
rule concrete: in 2002, `851003` with window 120 is 20851003 (interval 2023 to 2122), with window -20 it is 18851003 (1883 to 1982) and with window 10 it is 19851003 (1913 to 2012). Because the interval
moves with the clock, the same data gives different results in different years; the result is only stable when the data is regularly rewritten. GnuCOBOL's functions accept a third argument,
the "year of execution" (an extension, with no counterpart in the Enterprise COBOL syntax), which lets this program fix the year so that its output is repeatable. The first
four lines reproduce the IBM table, and the last two reproduce the `YEAR-TO-YYYY` table.

[source,cobol]
----
       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. WINDOWING.
      * Legacy: expanding two-digit years with a sliding window.
      * The third argument fixes the "year of execution" so the
      * output does not depend on the date the program is run.
       DATA DIVISION.
       WORKING-STORAGE SECTION.
       01  WS-YY                PIC 99.
       01  WS-YYYY              PIC 9(4).
       01  WS-YYMMDD            PIC 9(6).
       01  WS-YYYYMMDD          PIC 9(8).
       01  WS-YYDDD             PIC 9(5).
       01  WS-YYYYDDD           PIC 9(7).
       01  WS-PIVOT             PIC 99 VALUE 50.
       01  WS-YY-A              PIC 99 VALUE 99.
       01  WS-YY-B              PIC 99 VALUE 0.
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
       MAIN-PARAGRAPH.
           MOVE 851003 TO WS-YYMMDD
           COMPUTE WS-YYYYMMDD =
               FUNCTION DATE-TO-YYYYMMDD(WS-YYMMDD, 120, 2002)
           DISPLAY "851003, window +120, in 2002 : " WS-YYYYMMDD
           COMPUTE WS-YYYYMMDD =
               FUNCTION DATE-TO-YYYYMMDD(WS-YYMMDD, -20, 2002)
           DISPLAY "851003, window -20,  in 2002 : " WS-YYYYMMDD
           COMPUTE WS-YYYYMMDD =
               FUNCTION DATE-TO-YYYYMMDD(WS-YYMMDD, 10, 2002)
           DISPLAY "851003, window +10,  in 2002 : " WS-YYYYMMDD
           MOVE 10004 TO WS-YYDDD
           COMPUTE WS-YYYYDDD =
               FUNCTION DAY-TO-YYYYDDD(WS-YYDDD, -20, 2002)
           DISPLAY "10004 Julian, -20,   in 2002 : " WS-YYYYDDD
           MOVE 26 TO WS-YY
           COMPUTE WS-YYYY = FUNCTION YEAR-TO-YYYY(WS-YY, 50, 2026)
           DISPLAY "26 with the default window 50 in 2026 : " WS-YYYY
           MOVE 76 TO WS-YY
           COMPUTE WS-YYYY = FUNCTION YEAR-TO-YYYY(WS-YY, 50, 2026)
           DISPLAY "76 with the default window 50 in 2026 : " WS-YYYY
           MOVE 77 TO WS-YY
           COMPUTE WS-YYYY = FUNCTION YEAR-TO-YYYY(WS-YY, 50, 2026)
           DISPLAY "77 with the default window 50 in 2026 : " WS-YYYY
      *    The same idea written by hand: a fixed pivot year.
           IF WS-YY-A < WS-YY-B
               DISPLAY "99 sorts before 00 : true"
           ELSE
               DISPLAY "99 sorts before 00 : false (two digits only)"
           END-IF
           MOVE WS-YY-B TO WS-YY
           IF WS-YY < WS-PIVOT
               COMPUTE WS-YYYY = 2000 + WS-YY
           ELSE
               COMPUTE WS-YYYY = 1900 + WS-YY
           END-IF
           DISPLAY "00 with a hand-made pivot of 50 : " WS-YYYY
           COMPUTE WS-YYYY = FUNCTION YEAR-TO-YYYY(4, 23, 1995)
           DISPLAY "IBM table: 4, +23, in 1995       : " WS-YYYY
           COMPUTE WS-YYYY = FUNCTION YEAR-TO-YYYY(98, -15, 2008)
           DISPLAY "IBM table: 98, -15, in 2008      : " WS-YYYY
           STOP RUN.
----

[source,text]

851003, window -20,  in 2002 : 18851003
851003, window +10,  in 2002 : 19851003
10004 Julian, -20,   in 2002 : 1910004

The default window of 50 maps 26 to 2026, 76 to 2076 and 77 to 1977 when run in 2026 (the interval is 1977 to 2076); the same program run in 2027 would map 77 to 2077. The last block of the
program also shows the two things that go wrong with two digits: `99` compares as greater than `00`, so two-digit dates do not sort or compare in time order, and the hand-made `IF` with a fixed pivot of 50
works until the pivot is wrong for the data, which is why a fixed pivot is old code that needs a note in the program rather than a pattern to copy.

=== The Millennium Language Extensions: removed

Old code you may meet. Earlier Enterprise COBOL releases (up to V4) shipped the *Millennium Language Extensions*: the `DATE FORMAT` clause on a data item (for example
`DATE FORMAT YYXXXX` for a `yymmdd`-like field), the `DATEPROC` compiler option and `YEARWINDOW`, with which the compiler windowed such fields automatically during comparisons and moves. IBM removed them in Enterprise COBOL V5 and V6, and
searching the 6.5 Language Reference and Programming Guide for `DATEPROC`, `YEARWINDOW` and the `DATE FORMAT` clause finds nothing. A program that uses them does not compile on 6.5; the migration is one of:

* widen the field to `PIC 9(8)` or `PIC 9(7)` with a four-digit year and convert the stored data once (the preferred one);
* keep the stored `yymmdd` and call `DATE-TO-YYYYMMDD` or `YEAR-TO-YYYY` where the program needs a full date, as the program above does;
* leave a note beside the field naming the window and the planned year when it must be revisited.

The details of the V4 options are not documented for 6.5 and are not repeated here; the description above is from memory of the V4 documentation and is not re-verified.

== Where the book is outdated

The book consulted for this section (Taulli, _Modern Mainframe Development_, 2022) is a survey of mainframe work and is used here only for the topic list and the Y2K
narrative; nothing is copied from it. The points to carry into new code are:

* Two-digit years are not a design to follow. Teaching material that shows `+ACCEPT ... FROM DATE+` and then stores or compares the six digits is preserving a Y2K-era
  habit; new code uses `FUNCTION CURRENT-DATE(1:8)` or `+ACCEPT ... FROM DATE YYYYMMDD+` and a four-digit year in the record layout.
* The Millennium Language Extensions that the Y2K literature describes no longer exist in the current compiler (above); windowing survives only as the three intrinsic functions.
* The ISO 8601 functions are newer than most introductory books and than COBOL 2002; they are the way to produce and read the text dates of JSON and REST
  payloads, which the data-interchange pages of this section depend on.
* The book's Enterprise COBOL baseline (6.3) predates 6.4 and 6.5; the date functions above are in the 6.5 reference, which is the version this page follows.
