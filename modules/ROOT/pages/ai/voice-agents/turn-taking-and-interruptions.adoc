Turn-taking is the part of a voice agent that callers judge without knowing it exists. An agent that answers too early
cuts people off in the middle of a sentence; one that answers too late feels dead. One that keeps talking after the caller
says "no, wait" feels rude. This page covers the machinery behind those three impressions.

== Voice activity detection

A voice activity detector (VAD) classifies short audio frames as speech or non-speech. It is a small neural network that
runs on a CPU in well under a millisecond per frame, so it can run on every call. Two open-source choices are common:

* *Silero VAD* (MIT) is the default in Pipecat and LiveKit Agents. It takes 16 kHz (or 8 kHz) audio in small windows and
  returns a speech probability.
* *TEN VAD* (from the TEN Framework) is a low-latency detector aimed at the same job, with a lighter footprint.

A VAD alone gives two decisions, "speech started" and "speech stopped for N milliseconds". Both are parameterised:

[cols="2,4", options="header"]
|===
| Parameter | Effect

| Speech probability threshold
| Higher values ignore breathing and background noise but miss quiet speakers; lower values do the opposite.

| Minimum speech duration
| How long speech must last before "speech started" fires. Too short and coughs and door slams interrupt the agent.

| Silence (stop) duration
| How long silence must last before "speech stopped" fires. This is the main contributor to latexmath:[T_{eot}] in the
  xref:ai/voice-agents/voice-architectures.adoc[latency budget].

| Padding
| Audio kept before the detected start, so the first syllable is not clipped when the transcriber receives the segment.
|===

Telephone audio at 8 kHz with codec artefacts and line noise needs a higher threshold than studio audio, and noisy
environments (cars, call centres) benefit from a noise suppression step in front of the VAD; hosted APIs such as Azure
Voice Live and the OpenAI APIs offer it as a session option.

== Endpointing: when is the turn over?

Detecting that the caller stopped making sound is not the same as detecting that they finished their thought. "My order
number is..." followed by a pause to look something up is silence, not a finished turn. There are three levels of
sophistication.

. *Silence endpointing.* Declare the turn over after a fixed silence, typically a few hundred milliseconds up to about one
  second. It is simple, adds that wait to every turn, and still cuts off slow speakers.
. *Model-assisted endpointing.* The speech recogniser itself reports the end of the utterance. Deepgram Flux emits
  `EndOfTurn` and a speculative `EagerEndOfTurn` event on its `/v2/listen` endpoint, so the agent can start preparing the
  answer early and cancel if the caller keeps talking. AssemblyAI's streaming API marks turn boundaries with an
  `end_of_turn` field on its turn messages.
. *Semantic turn detection.* A small model reads the transcript (or the audio) and estimates whether the sentence is
  complete. The LiveKit turn detector (`livekit-plugins-turn-detector`) is a language model over the running transcript;
  Pipecat's Smart Turn is an audio model that looks at prosody and the last seconds of speech. Both run alongside the VAD
  and extend the wait when the utterance sounds unfinished.

[mermaid]
....
flowchart TD
  S[Speech frames] --> V{VAD: silence for N ms?}
  V -- no --> S
  V -- yes --> T{Turn model: sentence complete?}
  T -- "complete" --> E[End of turn: send transcript to the LLM]
  T -- "unfinished" --> W[Wait up to the maximum delay]
  W --> V2{Speech resumed?}
  V2 -- yes --> S
  V2 -- no --> E
....

The trade-off is the false-positive rate (interrupting the caller) against the added delay. A semantic detector typically
lets you use a short VAD silence (say 300 to 500 ms) for obviously finished sentences and a longer maximum for unfinished
ones, which lowers the average delay without raising the interruption rate. Test on recordings of hesitant speakers,
numbers read aloud, and non-native speech, where the models are weakest.

Speech-to-speech APIs make the same choice as a session option. OpenAI exposes server-side VAD with a
semantic mode alongside plain silence detection, Gemini Live has automatic activity detection with sensitivity
and silence settings, and Azure Voice Live offers semantic end-of-turn detection; see
xref:ai/voice-agents/realtime-apis.adoc[Realtime APIs] and check each vendor's current option names before copying
configuration.

[source,python]
----
from livekit.agents import AgentSession
from livekit.plugins import silero
from livekit.plugins.turn_detector.multilingual import MultilingualModel

session = AgentSession(
    vad=silero.VAD.load(),
    turn_detection=MultilingualModel(),
    allow_interruptions=True,
    min_endpointing_delay=0.5,
    max_endpointing_delay=3.0,
    # stt=..., llm=..., tts=... are configured as shown on the LiveKit Agents page
)
----

handling] and the xref:ai/voice-agents/livekit-agents.adoc[LiveKit Agents] page. For Pipecat, the VAD analyzer and the
Smart Turn analyzer are attached to the transport or the user aggregator; the current configuration is in the
https://github.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn[Smart Turn repository] and the xref:ai/voice-agents/pipecat.adoc[Pipecat] page.

== Barge-in

*Barge-in* is the caller speaking while the agent is speaking. Handling it well takes four actions, in this order and as
fast as possible:

. *Detect it.* The VAD fires "speech started" while the agent is in the speaking state. Require a minimum speech
  duration (200 to 300 ms) or a few recognised words, otherwise a cough or an echo stops the agent.
. *Stop the audio.* Cancel the TTS request and flush every audio buffer between the synthesiser and the caller. The
  buffers are the usual bug: audio already sent to the transport keeps playing unless the transport is told to clear it.
  Twilio Media Streams has a `clear` message for exactly this, ConversationRelay raises an `interrupt` message, and WebRTC
  playback is stopped by the client when it learns of the interruption.
. *Cancel the generation.* Cancel the running LLM stream, and any tool call that has not started side effects.
. *Truncate the history.* The conversation memory must contain what the caller actually heard, not the full answer the LLM
  had planned. Otherwise the next turn is confused: the caller says "what was the second step?" and the model believes it
  already spoke all five.

History truncation needs a record of how much audio reached the speaker. Speech-to-speech APIs provide this: the OpenAI
Realtime API accepts a `conversation.item.truncate` event with the audio end offset in milliseconds, and GPT-Live and
Gemini Live handle interruption inside the session. In a cascaded pipeline you estimate it from the playback position or,
for telephony, from the `mark` acknowledgements Twilio returns when queued audio reaches the caller.

.barge_in.py: framework-neutral interruption handler for a cascaded pipeline
[source,python]
----
import asyncio


class TurnState:
    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self.speaking_task: asyncio.Task | None = None
        self.spoken_text = ""  # text whose audio was confirmed played


async def on_user_speech_started(state: TurnState, transport, memory, thread_id: str) -> None:
    if state.speaking_task and not state.speaking_task.done():
        state.speaking_task.cancel()          # stops LLM stream and TTS requests
        await transport.clear_output_audio()   # flush queued audio (Twilio: send "clear")
        await memory.replace_last_assistant(thread_id, state.spoken_text + " [interrupted]")
----

This is a sketch, not a library API: `transport` and `memory` stand for whatever your transport and memory classes
provide. The Twilio `clear` and `mark` messages are specified in the
https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/media-streams/websocket-messages[Twilio Media Streams WebSocket messages] reference.

== Echo cancellation

If the caller's speaker output reaches their microphone, the agent hears itself and interrupts itself. Who removes the echo
depends on the transport.

[cols="2,4", options="header"]
|===
| Transport | Echo handling

| Browser, WebRTC or `getUserMedia`
| The browser applies acoustic echo cancellation when the constraint `echoCancellation` is on (it is by default), together
  with noise suppression and auto gain control. Keep these constraints enabled for headsets-free use. See
  xref:ai/voice-agents/browser-voice.adoc[Voice in the Browser].

| Phone network (PSTN)
| The carrier and the handset handle echo; the agent usually receives clean far-end audio. Speakerphone use on the caller's
  side can still leak the agent's voice back and needs the stricter minimum-speech rule above.

| SIP and PBX
| Echo can appear between the PBX and the phone; an echo canceller in the PBX or the SIP gateway is configured per
  channel. See xref:ai/voice-agents/telephony-with-asterisk-and-sip.adoc[Telephony with Asterisk and SIP].

| Server-side audio capture
| Raw audio sent over a WebSocket has no echo cancellation unless the client applied it. Apply it on the client, or
  accept that barge-in will need a higher threshold.
|===

A practical fallback is to suppress barge-in detection during the first few hundred milliseconds of each agent utterance,
and to require recognised words, not only energy, before treating speech as an interruption.

== DTMF

Phone callers can press keys, and the keys arrive as dual-tone multi-frequency (DTMF) events, not as speech. They are the
reliable way to enter an account number, confirm a choice or escape a loop. Twilio ConversationRelay delivers a `dtmf`
message to the WebSocket and Media Streams delivers `dtmf` events; Asterisk raises DTMF events on ARI channels; see
xref:ai/voice-agents/telephony-with-twilio.adoc[Telephony with Twilio]. Treat DTMF as input to the same
`ask(conversationId, userId, text)` core (for example, as the text "caller pressed 2"), and offer it as an alternative
whenever speech recognition fails twice.

== Silence and timeouts

Every state needs a timeout:

* *Caller silent after the agent's question.* Re-prompt once after a few seconds ("Are you still there?"), then offer
  DTMF, then end the call politely.
* *Caller silent in the first seconds.* On outbound calls, silence may mean voicemail or an answering machine; hand over
  to the voicemail logic rather than speaking into it.
* *Agent thinking too long.* If the LLM or a tool has not produced a first token after a threshold, say a short filler
  ("One moment") so the line does not go dead, and give up with an apology at a hard limit.
* *Whole-call limit.* Cap the call duration so a stuck loop cannot run up cost.

== Backchannels and fillers

People say "mm-hm", "right" and "okay" while the other party talks, without taking the turn. A VAD treats them as speech
and may stop the agent for no reason. Options are to require a minimum number of words before an interruption counts, to
list known backchannel words that the pipeline ignores while the agent speaks, or to use a model that classifies the
interruption. In the other direction, an agent can emit its own short acknowledgements ("I see", "let me check") to cover
tool latency; deliver them as a separate, pre-synthesised clip so they cost no synthesis time.

== A tuning checklist

. Record 50 real calls (with consent; see xref:ai/voice-agents/production-voice-agents.adoc[Production Voice Agents]).
. Replay them with different VAD thresholds and endpointing delays and count false interruptions and cut-offs.
. Check that barge-in stops audible output within about 300 ms and that the history shows what was heard.
. Test with a speakerphone, a headset, a noisy room and a bad mobile connection.
. Re-run after every change of model, codec or transport.
