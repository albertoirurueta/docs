== Sizing concurrent calls

A voice call holds resources for its whole duration: a WebSocket or WebRTC connection, a pipeline object with its buffers,
and, with local models, a share of a CPU or GPU. Capacity is therefore a question of *how many calls are open at once*, not
of requests per second. Little's law gives the average:

[stem]
++++
C_{avg} = \lambda \cdot T_{call}
++++

where latexmath:[\lambda] is the call arrival rate (calls per second) and latexmath:[T_{call}] is the mean call duration in
seconds. One call per ten seconds (latexmath:[\lambda = 0.1]) with a mean of 180 s gives latexmath:[C_{avg} = 18] calls open.
Real traffic is bursty, so plan for the busy-hour peak rather than the daily mean, and add headroom for a failed node:

[stem]
++++
N_{pods} = \left\lceil \frac{C_{peak}}{c_{pod}} \cdot (1 + h) \right\rceil
++++

where latexmath:[C_{peak}] is the peak concurrent calls, latexmath:[c_{pod}] is the *measured* number of concurrent calls one
instance sustains while keeping latexmath:[T_{resp}] inside its budget (see
xref:ai/voice-agents/voice-architectures.adoc[Voice Architectures]), and latexmath:[h] is the headroom fraction. The one number
that matters is latexmath:[c_{pod}]: find it with a load test that adds simulated callers until the 95th-percentile time to
first audio degrades, then set the admission limit a little below that point.

Practical consequences:

* *Admit or reject, never queue silently.* A caller who is placed in an unbounded queue hears silence. When a pod is at its
  limit, refuse the call so the load balancer or the telephony provider can try another instance or play a busy message.
* *Scale on concurrent sessions, not CPU alone.* A custom metric such as `active_calls` drives the autoscaler; CPU lags
  behind it. Drain a pod on scale-in: stop accepting calls and let open ones finish before the process exits.
* *Cap what one call can consume.* Maximum duration, maximum silence and a ceiling on tool calls per call protect both the
  bill and the fleet (the telephony page lists the abuse controls for
  xref:ai/voice-agents/telephony-with-asterisk-and-sip.adoc#_security[SIP]).
* *Hosted platforms have their own concurrency limits.* ElevenLabs, OpenAI, Gemini and Twilio each cap simultaneous
  sessions per account or plan; check the limit page of the platform and ask for an increase before a launch, not during one.
* *The core is part of the fleet.* Every call also loads the DocsAssistant core and its vector store; size them for the same
  peak, and use the caches from xref:ai/rag-systems/caching-latency-and-cost.adoc[Caching, Latency and Cost] so repeated
  questions do not each pay for retrieval.
* *Frameworks give you the process model.* Pipecat runs a long-lived asyncio bot per call or per few calls and LiveKit runs
  a job per room on an agent server; both are described under *Deployment* and *Self-hosted or LiveKit Cloud* on the
  xref:ai/voice-agents/pipecat.adoc[Pipecat] and xref:ai/voice-agents/livekit-agents.adoc[LiveKit Agents] pages.

== GPU needs for local speech models

A fully local stack (see the on-premises stack on the xref:ai/voice-agents/pipecat.adoc[Pipecat] page) replaces per-minute fees
with hardware you must size. Speech models differ from LLMs in that they run *per audio stream in real time*, so what matters
is the *real-time factor* (RTF): seconds of compute per second of audio.

[stem]
++++
\mathrm{RTF} = \frac{T_{compute}}{T_{audio}}
++++

A model with latexmath:[\mathrm{RTF} = 0.1] on your hardware can serve about ten audio streams at the limit of one device,
before you reserve headroom for bursts and for the other models sharing it. The same arithmetic applies to the speech-to-text
and the text-to-speech sides separately, and a call uses *both* directions only while that side is active: recognition
runs while the caller speaks, synthesis while the agent speaks, so the sum is lower than the naive count.

[cols="2,3,3", options="header"]
|===
| Component | What drives its cost | How to size it

| STT (`faster-whisper`, Parakeet, Kyutai STT)
| Model size, precision (quantised weights), and whether you decode windows repeatedly to simulate streaming.
| Measure RTF with the production audio (8 kHz telephone audio decoded the same way as in production), then divide capacity by
  the number of callers speaking at once, not by the number of calls.

| TTS (Kokoro, Piper, Sesame CSM, Kyutai TTS)
| Output seconds generated per answer, and the time to the first audio chunk of the first sentence.
| Measure time to first byte under load, because the latency budget depends on it more than on throughput.

| Turn and VAD models
| Silero VAD and the Smart Turn or LiveKit turn detector are small and run per stream.
| Usually CPU; verify that they do not share a thread with the event loop that moves audio.

| A local LLM
| Parameters, context length and concurrent decoding sequences.
| Use the serving engine's own capacity guidance; this is the largest consumer by far and is outside this sub-section.
|===

The general rules are stable even though specific cards and speeds change: keep models resident in memory and load them once
per process; batch only where the engine supports it without delaying the first token; pin GPU memory per replica so that
one noisy call cannot evict a model; and measure with real calls, because published benchmarks use clean wideband audio. The
licences of the open models (Apache-2.0, MIT, CC-BY-4.0, GPL-3.0 for Piper's maintained repository, non-commercial weights
for XTTS-v2) are tabulated on the xref:ai/voice-agents/text-to-speech.adoc[Text to Speech] and
xref:ai/voice-agents/speech-to-text.adoc[Speech to Text] pages and belong in the software bill of materials before production.

== Cost per minute

Voice agents are billed in many units: minutes of conversation, seconds of audio, characters synthesised, tokens, telephone
minutes and GPU hours. The method is to convert every component to *cost per conversation minute* and add them up, using the
unit prices from each vendor's own pricing page at the time you do the exercise.

[stem]
++++
c_{min} = c_{tel} + c_{stt} \, f_{u} + c_{tts} \, f_{a} + c_{llm} + c_{infra}
++++

where latexmath:[c_{tel}] is the telephony or transport cost per minute (Twilio, the SIP trunk, or the WebRTC platform),
latexmath:[c_{stt}] and latexmath:[c_{tts}] are the recognition and synthesis prices per minute of audio,
latexmath:[f_u] and latexmath:[f_a] are the fractions of the call during which the *user* and the *agent* speak, latexmath:[c_{llm}]
is the language-model cost per call minute (input and output tokens times their prices, including the retrieved context that
is resent on every turn) and latexmath:[c_{infra}] is the amortised servers, GPUs and operations. For a hosted voice platform
or a speech-to-speech API the formula collapses to one or two unit prices per minute or per audio token, and the exercise is
to find out what the price *includes*.

Three effects are easy to miss:

* *Silence is billed.* Some services bill the whole connected time, so the fractions latexmath:[f_u] and latexmath:[f_a] do not
  reduce the bill; cap idle time and total duration.
* *Context grows each turn.* The input tokens of turn latexmath:[n] include the earlier turns and the retrieved passages, so
  the LLM cost is not linear in call length. Truncate history and keep the retrieved context small.
* *Failed and abandoned calls cost money.* Ring time, voicemail, transfers and retries are all billable on some platforms.

Multiply latexmath:[c_{min}] by the expected minutes per month, then compare it with the cost of the alternative (a human
agent minute, or a chat session). The question the result answers is usually which calls should *not* go to the voice agent.

== Recording, consent and disclosure

Recording a call, transcribing it, using the audio to improve a model and telling the caller that they are talking to a machine
are four separate obligations, and the rules differ by country, by US state, by whether one or both parties must consent, by
whether the call is crossing a border, and by sector. This site does not summarise them. Start from the sources and ask a
lawyer:

* the General Data Protection Regulation (EU 2016/679), https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj[on EUR-Lex], for
  lawful basis, retention, access and erasure of recordings and transcripts, which are personal data and sometimes biometric
  data;
* Article 50 of the EU AI Act, https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/[Transparency obligations], on telling people
  that they are interacting with an AI system;
* Twilio's https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/api/recording[recording documentation], which states what the platform leaves
  to you, as an example of a provider's shared-responsibility note; every provider has an equivalent.

Engineering consequences that hold regardless of the law:

* Disclose at the start of the call that the caller is speaking to an AI and, if recording, that the call is recorded.
  Greeting prompts already do this in the examples on the previous pages.
* Make recording a *per-call decision* stored with the call, not a global switch; keep a record of the consent text played.
* Do not record when you do not need to. Streaming audio to recognition without storing it is a smaller data protection
  surface than a recording archive.
* Set a retention period for audio and a possibly longer one for redacted transcripts, and delete on schedule.
* Voice cloning of a real person needs that person's explicit permission (see
  xref:ai/voice-agents/text-to-speech.adoc[Text to Speech]).

== PII redaction in transcripts

Callers read out names, phone numbers, account numbers and addresses, and the recogniser writes them down. Redact before the
transcript reaches logs, analytics, evaluation datasets and tracing backends.

. *Decide the order.* Redact in the voice server on the final transcript, before it leaves the process. The live copy that
  the core needs to answer the question is a separate, short-lived value.
. *Use a detector.* Microsoft's https://microsoft.github.io/presidio/[Presidio] combines named-entity recognition, patterns
  and checksums; some STT providers offer built-in redaction of entities, which is worth enabling as a first layer. Test
  against your own transcripts, because spoken numbers ("four one five, five five five") differ from written ones.
. *Replace with typed placeholders* (`<PHONE>`, `<ACCOUNT>`) so the transcript stays useful for analysis.
. *Redact audio too* if you keep recordings: either do not retain them, or retain them encrypted with a short retention.
. *Keep the access-control filter.* The identity that retrieval uses for document-level access comes from the call setup, not
  from the transcript; see xref:ai/rag-systems/access-control-and-privacy.adoc[Access Control and Privacy].
. *Watch the trace attributes.* Span attributes, error messages and tool arguments leak the same data as the transcript.

Spoken text is also an injection channel: a caller, or a recording played into the line, can say "ignore your instructions".
The controls in xref:ai/rag-systems/guardrails-and-prompt-injection.adoc[Guardrails and Prompt Injection] apply unchanged to
the transcript, and xref:ai/security/prompt-injection.adoc[Prompt Injection] in the xref:ai/security/index.adoc[AI Security & Responsible AI] sub-section covers the underlying attack.

== Observability

Instrument per turn, per stage, and report percentiles. The timestamps are defined on
xref:ai/voice-agents/voice-architectures.adoc[Voice Architectures]; emit them as spans or histograms with the call id as a
correlation attribute. Pipecat exposes metrics through `PipelineParams(enable_metrics=True)` (see
https://docs.pipecat.ai/pipecat/fundamentals/metrics[Pipecat: Metrics]) and LiveKit Agents emits metrics events (see
https://docs.livekit.io/agents/build/metrics/[LiveKit: Metrics]); the OpenTelemetry
https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/gen-ai/[GenAI semantic conventions] give common attribute names for the model
spans. The RAG half of the trace is described on xref:ai/rag-systems/observability-and-tracing.adoc[Observability & Tracing].

[cols="2,3,3", options="header"]
|===
| Signal | Definition | Why it matters

| Time to first audio
| `t_audio_sent - t_user_end`, p50, p95 and p99
| The caller's perceived latency; alert on p95 above the budget.

| Per-stage latency
| End-of-turn delay, STT final, LLM first token, TTS first byte
| Tells you which term of the budget to fix.

| Interruption rate
| Barge-ins per agent turn, and the share that occur in the first second of an answer
| High values mean the answer is too long, too slow, or the VAD fires on noise or echo.

| False cut-off rate
| Turns where the caller resumed within a short window after an end-of-turn
| Direct measure of an over-aggressive endpointing delay.

| Silence and dead air
| Seconds without agent or caller audio during a call
| Reveals stalled tools and lost audio.

| Call outcome
| Resolved, abandoned, transferred, error
| The product metric, split by channel.

| Concurrency and rejections
| `active_calls` and refused admissions
| The input to capacity planning.

| Transport quality
| WebRTC round-trip time, jitter and packet loss; SIP MOS where the trunk reports it
| Separates network problems from model problems.
|===

=== Sampling the word error rate

The word error rate defined on xref:ai/voice-agents/speech-to-text.adoc[Speech to Text] needs a human reference, so compute it
on a *sample*, not on every call:

. Sample a fixed fraction of calls, stratified by channel (8 kHz telephone versus wideband browser), language and accent.
. Have people transcribe the sample, from redacted audio or inside the retention period.
. Compute latexmath:[\mathrm{WER}] with identical text normalisation on both sides, and track it per channel over time, together
  with the retrieval hit rate on the transcribed question.
. Re-run the sample after every change of recogniser, model version, codec or vocabulary list.

A rising WER on one channel after a provider change is easier to catch this way than from a satisfaction score. Scoring the
answers themselves (groundedness, correctness) belongs to the xref:ai/llmops/rag-and-agent-evaluation.adoc[LLMOps: RAG & Agent Evaluation] page.

== Testing with simulated callers

Unit tests cover the text core (see the evaluation material in
xref:ai/rag-systems/index.adoc[RAG Systems in Production]); a voice agent additionally needs tests that run *audio* through the
whole pipeline.

* *Recorded calls.* Replay recorded and consented utterances, or synthesised ones, through the transport at real-time speed
  and assert on the transcript, the tool calls and the time to first audio. Include 8 kHz mu-law, accents, noise, hesitation
  and numbers read aloud.
* *Scripted simulated callers.* A caller script (or an LLM playing a persona) speaks through a TTS into the same transport and
  listens to the answer through an STT. A test client can join the room or the WebRTC session like any user; LiveKit documents a testing
  approach for agent behaviour at https://docs.livekit.io/agents/start/testing/[LiveKit: Testing and evaluation].
* *Barge-in cases.* Interrupt in the first word, mid-sentence and at the end of the answer; assert that output stops within
  the target time and that the memory records what was heard.
* *Telephony paths.* Place a real call for each route (browser, Twilio, PBX extension) as a smoke test after every deployment,
  since provider behaviour cannot be fully simulated. Use a number that is excluded from billing analytics.
* *Load tests.* Ramp simulated callers to find latexmath:[c_{pod}] and to verify that admission control rejects excess calls
  cleanly.
* *Chaos tests.* Kill a provider connection mid-call, drop packets, and restart a pod while calls are open.

== Failure handling and fallback to a human

Every provider in the chain can fail: the recogniser, the LLM, the synthesiser, the retrieval store and the telephony leg.
Decide the behaviour *before* it happens.

[mermaid]
....
flowchart TD
  A[Incoming call] --> B{Capacity available?}
  B -- no --> R[Reject: busy message or retry elsewhere]
  B -- yes --> C[Greeting and AI disclosure]
  C --> D[Conversation turns]
  D --> E{Provider error or low confidence?}
  E -- no --> D
  E -- retry --> F[Retry or switch to backup provider]
  F --> D
  E -- repeated --> G{Human available?}
  G -- yes --> H[Transfer with context]
  G -- no --> I[Apologise, offer callback or message]
  D --> J{Caller asks for a person?}
  J -- yes --> G
  D --> K[Call ends: log outcome]
....

* *Backup providers.* Configure a second STT, LLM or TTS provider and switch on repeated errors or timeouts; frameworks make
  the service a replaceable pipeline stage (see the service lists on the Pipecat and LiveKit pages).
* *Degrade, do not hang.* If retrieval times out, answer from what is known and say so, or admit that the lookup failed. Use a
  spoken filler only for waits you expect, and bound them.
* *Transfer to a human.* Offer it whenever the caller asks, after repeated misunderstandings, for sensitive requests the
  agent is not allowed to handle, and when the agent detects distress. Pass the conversation summary and the caller's identity
  with the transfer. The Twilio mechanism is on the "Transfer to a human" section of
  xref:ai/voice-agents/telephony-with-twilio.adoc[Telephony with Twilio]; LiveKit SIP and Asterisk have their own transfer
  operations on their pages.
* *Voicemail and machine detection.* For outbound calls, start the agent only when a person answers; answering machines and
  voicemail greetings must not be treated as a caller. Twilio exposes answering machine detection on outbound calls (see
  https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/api/call-resource[Twilio: Call resource]); others offer similar signals.
* *After hours.* If no human is available, offer a callback or a message instead of a dead end, and record the request.

== Accessibility

Voice is an accessibility feature and, badly done, a barrier. People who cannot use a keyboard or screen benefit from a voice
interface; people who are deaf, hard of hearing, or have a speech difference need an alternative.

* Always offer a *non-voice path* with equivalent capability: the chat channel on the same site (see
  xref:ai/conversational-channels/index.adoc[Conversational Channels]), a text relay, or a callback.
* Let callers set the *pace*: a slower speech rate, a "repeat that" command, and generous endpointing for slow or atypical
  speech, which a semantic turn model may handle poorly; test those voices.
* Provide *captions* of the agent's speech in the browser widget and keep the transcript available to screen-reader users
  (the live-region and focus rules are on xref:ai/voice-agents/browser-voice.adoc[Voice in the Browser]).
* Do not make DTMF the only way to confirm something, and do not use a voice-only check as the only proof of identity.
* Follow the standards summarised on xref:web/accessibility.adoc[Web Accessibility] for the page that hosts the widget.

== Limits and abuse

Beyond the controls on the telephony page: per-caller and per-account rate limits on call creation, a maximum call length and
idle time, an allow-list or authentication for the endpoints that mint ephemeral credentials (see
xref:ai/voice-agents/browser-voice.adoc[Voice in the Browser]), spending caps and alerts on every hosted account, and
toll-fraud protection on SIP (restricted destinations, no open relay). Treat a spike in short calls or in minutes per number as
an incident signal.

== Operations checklist

. Capacity measured with a load test; admission control returns a clean refusal.
. Cost per minute computed for each route from current price pages, with idle time capped.
. AI disclosure played; recording consent captured per call; retention and deletion scheduled.
. PII redacted before transcripts reach logs, traces and datasets.
. Per-stage latency, interruption rate and outcome dashboards; alerts on p95 time to first audio.
. WER and retrieval hit rate sampled per channel.
. Recorded-call, simulated-caller, barge-in, telephony-smoke and chaos tests in the pipeline.
. Backup providers configured; human transfer, callback and after-hours path tested.
. Non-voice alternative and captions available.
. Spending caps, rate limits and SIP restrictions in place.
