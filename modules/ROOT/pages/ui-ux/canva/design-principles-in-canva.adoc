Design principles are habits of seeing. A template or an AI suggestion gives you a starting layout, but only a trained eye notices that two blocks are almost aligned, that the title and the date compete, or that the contrast looks fine on a bright laptop and fails on a phone. The same principles drive the interface work in the xref:ui-ux/figma/index.adoc[Figma sub-section]. This page does not repeat that material; where a principle belongs to research or testing, it points you to xref:ui-ux/figma/information-architecture-and-user-flows.adoc[Information Architecture and User Flows], xref:ui-ux/figma/research-personas-and-journey-maps.adoc[Research, Personas and Journey Maps], xref:ui-ux/figma/accessible-and-inclusive-design.adoc[Accessible and Inclusive Design] and xref:ui-ux/figma/usability-testing-and-iteration.adoc[Usability Testing and Iteration].

== Contrast

Contrast is a visible difference between two things: light against dark, large against small, bold against regular, a saturated colour against a neutral. It tells the eye what to read first and keeps text legible. Low contrast is the most common reason a post looks "washed out", and it is the one problem you can measure.

In Canva you control contrast with text colour, background colour and font weight, and you can check it with the Design Accessibility check: open File, then Accessibility, then Check Design Accessibility. The check flags small type (under 12 pt), poor colour contrast and missing alternative text. Details of the checker and of accessible colour choices are on xref:ui-ux/canva/accessibility-in-canva.adoc[Accessibility in Canva]. Design School adds a lesson on using contrast to make designs impactful, linked in the references.

Source: https://www.canva.com/design-school/resources/creating-impactful-designs-with-contrast[Canva Design School: Creating impactful designs with contrast]

== Balance

Balance is how visual weight is spread across the page. Large, dark, saturated or detailed elements weigh more than small, light or plain ones. A design can be symmetrical (similar weight on each side of a central axis, which feels formal and stable) or asymmetrical (a heavy element on one side offset by several lighter ones, which feels dynamic). Both can be balanced; an unbalanced layout feels as if it is about to tip over.

Use the canvas edges and the page centre as a reference, and turn on rulers and guides from File, then Rulers and guides. Margins are a dotted layout boundary that keeps content away from the edge: File, Rulers and guides, then Show margins. Margins are available on desktop and mobile designs but not in Canva Docs, and they differ from the red danger zone that appears in print proofing.

Source: https://www.canva.com/help/margins-bleed-crop-marks/[Canva Help Center: Use margins, bleed, rulers, and crop marks]

== Alignment

Alignment is the invisible edge that several elements share. Left-aligned text with a common left edge looks deliberate; text that starts a few pixels apart looks like a mistake. Pick one alignment for the main text blocks and keep it. Centre alignment suits a short, symmetrical post; left alignment suits anything longer than two lines.

Canva gives you three layers of help. Guides: choose File, then Rulers and guides, then Add guides, and pick a preset (12 columns, 3 columns or a 3x3 grid) or a custom layout. Preset and custom guides are locked by default so you do not drag them by accident, and applying a new layout replaces earlier guides. Rulers: Show rulers and guides places rulers along the top and left of the canvas and drags new guides from them; rulers are desktop-only. Arrangement tools: the Position and alignment controls and the spacing options are covered on xref:ui-ux/canva/layers-alignment-and-arrangement.adoc[Layers, Alignment and Arrangement].

Source: https://www.canva.com/help/adding-and-removing-guides/[Canva Help Center: Add, unlock, and remove guides]

== Proximity

Proximity means that things that belong together sit together and things that do not are separated. A title, its author and its rating form one group; the call to action is a different group. If every element has the same gap to its neighbours, the reader cannot tell which items are related. Group with space first, and only then add lines or boxes.

In the editor, select the related elements and use the group action so that you can move and align them as one block. Group and ungroup, layer order and lock are covered on xref:ui-ux/canva/layers-alignment-and-arrangement.adoc[Layers, Alignment and Arrangement].

== Repetition and consistency

Repetition reuses the same colour, font, shape, spacing and icon style across a design or a campaign so that the pieces look as if they belong together. Consistency is the same idea at the scale of a brand: five Instagram posts, a story and a deck that share a palette, two fonts and a logo position are recognised before anyone reads the text.

Canva supports this with the Brand tab in the side panel, which holds the Brand Kit: brand colours, brand fonts, logos and Brand Templates. Applying them from the Brand tab instead of picking a similar-looking colour keeps the campaign exact, and brand controls set by an administrator can lock the rest. See xref:ui-ux/canva/brand-kit-and-brand-voice.adoc[Brand Kit and Brand Voice] and xref:ui-ux/canva/templates-layouts-and-magic-design.adoc[Templates, Layouts and Magic Design]. For interface work, the equivalent is a design system, covered in the Figma sub-section.

== Hierarchy

Visual hierarchy is the order in which the eye reads. It is built from size, weight, colour, position and space. A good post has one clear first read (the title), a second (the book name or date) and a clear action. A common test is to squint at the design or look at a small thumbnail: whatever you still see first is your hierarchy.

In Canva set the hierarchy with the font size and weight controls on the quick actions toolbar, and with text effects when you need extra emphasis. The Help Center groups text effects into Style (for example Drop, Glow, Echo, Outline, Background and Splice), Color (Hollow, Glitch and Neon) and Shape (Curve). Use them sparingly: an effect on every line removes the hierarchy it was supposed to add. Typography itself is on xref:ui-ux/canva/text-and-typography.adoc[Text and Typography].

Source: https://www.canva.com/help/text-effects/[Canva Help Center: Enhance text with effects]

== White space

White space (negative space) is the empty area around and between elements. It is not wasted room: it separates groups, gives the title room to breathe and makes a design look calm and premium. Crowded designs read as cheap and tiring. If a post feels busy, remove an element before you shrink everything.

Margins help here, because they stop content creeping to the edge. A practical rule is to leave a margin on all sides at least as wide as the largest gap inside the design.

== Colour theory and harmony

The colour wheel arranges hues in a circle and gives you ready-made relationships between them. Canva publishes a colour wheel tool, and the Design School has a lesson on its basics. The main harmonies are complementary, analogous, triadic, split complementary and monochromatic.

.Colour-wheel harmonies: complementary, analogous, triadic, split complementary and monochromatic
image::ui-ux-canva-design-principles-in-canva-colour-harmonies.svg["A twelve-segment colour wheel with a complementary pair marked, and a legend of five harmonies with sample swatches",width=720]

A reliable recipe is the 60-30-10 split: roughly 60 percent of the area in a dominant neutral or background colour, 30 percent in a secondary colour and 10 percent in a bright accent used for the call to action. Whatever harmony you choose, check value contrast between text and background, because two hues with the same lightness can look identical to many readers. Palettes, colour themes, gradients and Style Match are on xref:ui-ux/canva/color-gradients-and-palettes.adoc[Colour, Gradients and Palettes].

Source: https://www.canva.com/colors/color-wheel/[Canva Colors: Color wheel] and https://www.canva.com/design-school/resources/color-wheel-basics/[Canva Design School: Color wheel basics]

== Type pairing

Two fonts are usually enough: one for headings and one for body text. Pair fonts that contrast clearly (for example a serif heading with a plain sans-serif body) rather than fonts that are almost alike, and avoid more than two or three families in one design. Use weight and size, not new fonts, to create variation. In Canva, Font combinations in the Text tab offer pairings prepared by Canva, and Brand fonts keep a campaign consistent. See xref:ui-ux/canva/text-and-typography.adoc[Text and Typography] for the controls and for typefaces that need a paid plan, and Design School for the lessons on type theory.

Source: https://www.canva.com/design-school/lessons/understanding-typography-theory/[Canva Design School: Understanding typography]

== Finding a style, inspiration and creative block

A style is the sum of your choices: palette, fonts, shapes, image treatment and tone. To find one, start from a purpose and an audience (the research habits are in the Figma pages linked above), collect references in a mood board, and write down three adjectives such as "warm, literary, calm". Then test every choice against those words.

Sources of inspiration are plentiful: Canva Learn articles, Design School courses, the template library and the work of other designers. Use them to learn why a layout works and rebuild it with your own content and brand, never to copy another creator's work or to reuse a template's sample content unchanged.

When you are stuck, shrink the problem. Duplicate the page and try a deliberately different version (a bold colour background, a huge title, a photo instead of an illustration), change one variable at a time, take a break and look at a thumbnail, or ask a colleague which of two versions they notice first. In Canva you can also ask the Canva AI assistant for layout and copy variations, or start from a template and restyle it; see xref:ui-ux/canva/canva-ai-assistant.adoc[the Canva AI assistant page]. Treat any suggestion as a draft that you judge against the principles above.

== Desktop and mobile differences

Rulers are desktop only, and the quick actions toolbar is split into a floating toolbar and a keyboard toolbar on a phone. Guides and margins can be shown from the editor menu on mobile too, but dragging guides from the rulers is a desktop action. Judge contrast and size on the device where the post will be seen: Instagram is viewed mostly on phones, so zoom the editor out to roughly the size of a phone thumbnail before you decide the text is large enough. See xref:ui-ux/canva/desktop-mobile-and-offline.adoc[Desktop, Mobile and Offline].

== Procedure: redesign the "Book of the week" post

The *Bookshelf Book of the Week* post currently has five competing text sizes, uneven edges, grey text on a grey photo and no obvious action. The figure shows the before and after.

.Before and after: the Book of the week post, redesigned with the principles on this page
image::ui-ux-canva-design-principles-in-canva-before-after-redesign.svg["Two schematic posts side by side: a cluttered grey post with ragged edges, and a tidy post with one title, a shared left edge, strong contrast and one amber button",width=720]

. Open the *Bookshelf Book of the Week* Instagram post and zoom out to a small size, or view it as a thumbnail. Note which element your eye reaches first; if it is not the title, the hierarchy is wrong.
. Open File, then Rulers and guides, and choose Show margins. Then choose Add guides and pick the 3x3 grid. Move the title and the details so that they share the left guide and stay inside the margins.
. Open the Brand tab in the side panel and apply the Bookshelf dark background and the light heading colour. Keep one accent colour, used only for the button.
. Give the title one large size from the quick actions toolbar, set the book name and date to one smaller size, and select the author, rating and date and group them so that they read as one block. Leave a clear gap before the button.
. Choose File, then Accessibility, then Check Design Accessibility. Fix every listed issue (small text, contrast, alt text on the cover photo), run the check again and press Command + Z (Ctrl + Z on Windows) if a change is not an improvement.

== Common pitfalls

* Treating a principle as a rule. A deliberately broken alignment or a huge title can be the point of a poster; break a principle on purpose and only once per design.
* Adding more effects, colours or fonts to fix a layout problem that more space or a shared edge would solve.
* Judging contrast by eye on one screen instead of running the Design Accessibility check and testing on a phone.
* Choosing a harmony and ignoring value contrast, so that the colours match but the text cannot be read.
* Sampling colours by eye from a photo instead of using Brand tab colours, so the campaign drifts from the brand.
* Forgetting that guides and margins are layout aids that do not appear in the exported design.
* Using a template's sample layout as a finished design without checking hierarchy and alignment against your own content.
