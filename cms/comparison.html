CMS Comparison

This content was generated with the assistance of AI and should be verified against the official documentation before being relied on in production.

This page compares the two content management systems documented in the CMS section of this site, WordPress and Webflow, with fourteen other systems that matter because they are popular, because they are growing, or because they show a different way of building the same site: traditional open-source systems, hosted website builders, headless and API-first products, and enterprise or stack-specific systems. Every number was fetched on 2026-10-29 from the sources listed in the Bibliography, and the figures change monthly, so treat the page as a dated snapshot. The data and the scripts that draw the figures are in scripts/cms-comparison-charts/, and the charts can be regenerated with npm run charts:cms-comparison.

The page follows the method of the E-commerce Platforms Comparison, the Web Frameworks Comparison and the Documentation Tools Comparison: data kept in CSV files, charts generated from them, a stated source and date for every figure, and n/a for anything that could not be sourced. It does not repeat what the platform sections explain: for the detail of one platform, follow the links to the WordPress comparison and decision guide and the Webflow comparison and decision guide. To go straight to the conclusion, read the summary and recommendations at the end.

Scope and inclusion criteria

What is compared. Sixteen systems in four groups. The groups are not the same kind of product, and the page says so wherever it matters:

Group Systems What they have in common

Traditional open-source (self-hosted)

WordPress (documented on this site), Drupal (documented on this site), Joomla (documented on this site), TYPO3, Craft CMS

Server-side PHP applications that the site owner (or a host) installs, patches and upgrades, with a plugin, module or extension ecosystem. WordPress, Drupal, Joomla and TYPO3 are free software; Craft CMS publishes its source under a commercial licence.

Hosted website builders (SaaS)

Webflow (documented on this site), Wix, Squarespace

The vendor runs the servers, the editor and the upgrades; the owner configures a site inside the vendor’s product.

Headless and API-first

Contentful, Strapi, Sanity, Payload

Content is stored and edited in the CMS and delivered through an API to a separate front end. Contentful and Sanity host the content store; Strapi and Payload are open-source applications that the owner can host.

Enterprise and other stacks

Adobe Experience Manager (AEM, enterprise context), Umbraco (.NET), Wagtail (Python and Django), Ghost (Node.js publishing)

CMSs tied to a platform or a market: an enterprise suite, and three open-source systems for the .NET, Python and Node.js communities.

Context rows. Shopify appears only in the market-share and performance charts, because W3Techs counts it as a content management system; it is compared with its competitors in the E-commerce Platforms Comparison and is not scored here. Drupal 7 and Joomla 3 appear only in the support-window chart, to show what the end of a major line looks like (Drupal 7 ended on 2025-01-05 and Joomla 3 on 2023-08-17).

Inclusion criteria. A system is included if it ranks in at least one main source (the W3Techs share of websites, the HTTP Archive origins, the Stack Overflow Developer Survey, registry downloads or GitHub stars) or if it matters for enterprise or historical context. WordPress and Webflow are always included. A system without public data for a metric gets n/a for that metric: hosted SaaS products have no repository, registry or advisory data, and Payload is not a technology in the HTTP Archive Tech Report, so it has no performance series and no origins count.

Out of scope. E-commerce platforms (Shopify, PrestaShop, Magento and WooCommerce, covered by the E-commerce Platforms Comparison; WooCommerce appears only as an extension of WordPress), static-site generators and documentation tools (see when you do not need a CMS), and the systems that were considered and left out for lack of reachable primary data or marginal rank: Statamic, Concrete CMS, Duda, HubSpot Content Hub, Directus, Storyblok, Hygraph, Sitecore, Optimizely CMS, Blogger, Tilda, Weebly, Movable Type and Plone. WordPress.com is covered as the hosted variant of WordPress, in the cost section only.

Snapshot date. All values were fetched on 2026-10-29, and every CSV row carries its own fetched column. Time series end at the latest complete period at that date: 2026-09 for the HTTP Archive Tech Report, and 2026 to date for the yearly series. A value that could not be obtained is shown as n/a, never as zero, and a value that came from a page that could not be opened from the authoring environment, or from a secondary source, is marked unverified.

How metrics, scales and scores were assigned

Rules that apply throughout.

  • Absolute values from different sources are never plotted on one axis, because they count different things: W3Techs counts the share of websites among the sites it analyses, the HTTP Archive counts origins visited by Chrome users, and registries count downloads, which include continuous-integration and mirror traffic. Downloads from different registries (npm, Packagist, PyPI, NuGet) are never compared with each other.

  • Field data and counts are reported as measured. Where a number is a proxy (CVEs as a proxy for security, Stack Overflow questions as a proxy for community activity), the section says what it cannot show.

  • Colours identify a CMS inside its group: the palette has eight slots for sixteen systems, so a colour is consistent across the figures of one group, and figures that mix groups colour by group or say in their caption that colours are assigned by position in that figure.

  • A pair of numbers from different dates is never compared as if it were one series. INP (Interaction to Next Paint) replaced FID (First Input Delay) in the Core Web Vitals definition on 2024-03-12, so pass rates before and after that date are not strictly comparable, and the charts mark the break.

  • Stack Overflow’s site-wide decline since 2023 affects every tag, so question counts for recent years say little about a CMS on their own.

Scores. The radar charts and the scores table use twelve axes, each scored from 0 to 10 with higher always better. Five axes are computed from the data by a formula implemented in scores.mjs, and seven are editorial: a written rubric applied to cited evidence, with the basis and the source ids of every score recorded in radar-scores.csv. A system whose inputs are missing gets no score, drawn as a gap and never as zero.

Axis Kind Rule

Popularity

Computed

Mean of the available log scores of the W3Techs share of all sites (0.01% scores 0, 50% scores 10) and of the HTTP Archive mobile origins (100 scores 0, 5,000,000 scores 10), latest values. It counts visible fingerprints, so headless CMSs are underestimated; Payload has neither input and gets no score.

Growth

Computed

Ratio of the latest HTTP Archive mobile origins to the same month three years earlier: 5 plus 5 times the base-2 logarithm of the ratio, limited to 0 to 10 (unchanged is 5, doubled is 10, halved is 0). No score without both months.

Talent pool (Stack Overflow)

Computed

Log score of the Stack Overflow questions asked with the CMS tag in the last two complete years, 2024 and 2025 (10 questions score 0, 10,000 score 10). The axis measures developer activity only: the job-market signals of Talent pool and job market (survey usage, Hacker News job mentions) cover too few CMSs to score, so they are not part of it.

Ecosystem and maturity

Computed

Mean of the available log scores of the GitHub stars of the main repositories (1,000 scores 0, 100,000 score 10; the WordPress, Drupal and TYPO3 mirrors excluded) and of the total Stack Overflow questions of the tag (100 scores 0, 300,000 score 10). Hosted systems have no repository, so only the second input counts.

Performance

Computed

Mean of the mobile and desktop Core Web Vitals pass rates divided by 10 and of the mobile and desktop median Lighthouse performance scores times 10, latest HTTP Archive month. No score when one input is missing.

Ease of use (editors)

Editorial

Can editors write, preview, schedule and approve without a developer; the visual building, draft preview, scheduling and workflow rows of the feature matrix are quoted in the basis.

Ease of use (developers)

Editorial

Concepts to learn before a first custom template or content type, local setup, API and documentation; the content modelling, configuration-as-code and REST rows of the feature matrix are quoted.

Features and productivity

Editorial, rule-based

Points per feature of the 19-row feature matrix (yes 1, by extension 0.7, partial 0.5, n/a 0) divided by 19, times 10. n/a means no evidence was found, so it earns no credit, and scope differs by CMS type.

Low cost

Editorial

10 is free software with low hosting cost; lower for paid plans and licences. USD and EUR prices are not converted. No score where no sourced price exists (Contentful and AEM).

Security posture

Editorial

Patching model, advisory process, severity and exploitation history, never raw CVE counts, which are not comparable between SaaS and self-hosted systems.

Low maintenance effort

Editorial

10 is vendor-managed with no customer patching; lower for self-hosted systems with frequent major upgrades and short support windows.

Portability (low lock-in)

Editorial

Licence, data format, export and the ability to change host.

The scores summarise the evidence on this page. They are not a benchmark, and one point is within the uncertainty of the method.

Popularity

Popularity answers one question: how many websites run this CMS, and how much attention does it get from developers? No single source answers it, so the page uses four that count different things and never puts them on one axis: the W3Techs share of websites, the HTTP Archive count of origins, the activity of the developer ecosystem (GitHub, registries) and the questions asked on Stack Overflow. All of them count what can be seen from outside, which favours systems that leave a visible fingerprint in the page. A headless CMS serves content to a front end that shows no sign of it, so it is underestimated by W3Techs and the HTTP Archive; the popularity score of this page has that bias, and the registry downloads below are the better signal for headless products.

Developer activity and downloads

GitHub stars and contributors measure the interest of developers in an open-source CMS. They are not usage, and the main repository of some systems is a read-only mirror: the WordPress repository is a mirror of the Subversion repository (21,509 stars, and 3,496 more on the wordpress-develop repository where core development happens), and the Drupal and TYPO3 repositories are mirrors of their own Git hosts. Hosted SaaS products (Webflow, Wix, Squarespace, Contentful) and AEM are closed and have no repository (n/a). The leaders are Strapi (73,401 stars, 1,412 contributors), Ghost (55,630 and 943) and Payload (45,570 and 592), the Node.js CMSs, followed by WordPress (mirror), Wagtail (20,594 and 1,125), Sanity (6,380), Umbraco (5,278 and 1,215), Joomla (5,157 and 1,210), Drupal (4,295, mirror), Craft CMS (3,621) and TYPO3 (1,218 stars, mirror, and 1,289 contributors).

Community questions

Stack Overflow tags show where developers ask for help. WordPress has 189,983 questions in total, against 20,266 for Drupal, 15,003 for Joomla, 10,432 for Wix, 9,752 for TYPO3, 5,003 for AEM, 4,499 for Umbraco and 2,800 for Strapi. The counts per year show the site-wide decline since 2023 rather than the fate of a CMS: WordPress fell from 13,512 questions in 2020 to 5,286 in 2023 and 613 in 2025, Drupal from 411 to 183 and 40, and every other tag followed. The dedicated Stack Exchange sites show the same fall (wordpress.stackexchange.com: 7,268 in 2020, 2,328 in 2023, 468 in 2025). The 2026 values are a partial year and are omitted from the charts.

Reading the popularity data

WordPress leads every signal that counts websites by a wide margin: about 40% of all websites, 58% of those with a known CMS and about 2.7 million detected mobile origins, 15 times more than the next system. Wix is a distant second, then Drupal, Squarespace, Joomla and Webflow in the same order of magnitude. Among developers the leaders are different: the Node.js CMSs (Strapi, Ghost, Payload) lead GitHub stars, and Contentful, Sanity and Payload lead the growth of npm downloads. Popularity should be read together with the next section: the origins of most systems peaked before 2025.

Growth trends

Growth is measured with the yearly W3Techs share and the monthly HTTP Archive origins. The two series agree on direction for the systems they share, and they show that the market is consolidating around WordPress and the hosted builders at the top while the older open-source systems decline.

The yearly W3Techs share of all websites shows WordPress rising from 23.3% in 2015 to 43.6% in 2025 and then falling to 39.5% in 2026-10, the first fall in the series. Wix rose from 0.1% to 4.2% and Squarespace from 0.2% to 2.4% (Shopify, as context, from 0.3% to 5.3%), and Webflow from 0.1% in 2019 to 0.8%. Joomla fell from 3.3% in 2015 to 1.1% and Drupal from 2.0% to 0.6%.

The HTTP Archive series start in 2020-01, when the crawl counted 1,036,502 WordPress mobile origins, and peaked in 2023 and later. Between 2023-09 and 2026-09 most systems lost origins: Joomla -48%, Drupal -33%, Contentful -22%, WordPress -21%, Wix -20%, TYPO3 -14%, AEM -10%, Craft CMS -10%, Squarespace -7% and Ghost -2%. Webflow gained +21%, Sanity +228%, Umbraco +74% and Wagtail +61%. These origin counts depend on the crawl population as well as on real adoption, so read them as relative trends. Strapi has 14 mobile origins (27 in 2023-09) and the percentages of such small counts say little.

Ecosystem and maturity

Ecosystem and maturity is a computed score: the mean of the log score of the GitHub stars of the main repository (1,000 scores 0, 100,000 scores 10) and of the log score of the total Stack Overflow questions of the tag (100 scores 0, 300,000 scores 10). The WordPress, Drupal and TYPO3 repositories are read-only mirrors whose stars understate their communities, so for those three only the Stack Overflow part counts. The score rewards developer attention and accumulated community knowledge, which is a sign of maturity, and it undervalues hosted systems that have no repository. The scores are WordPress 9.4, Strapi 6.8, Drupal 6.6, Wix 5.8, TYPO3 5.7, Ghost 5.4, Wagtail 5.3, Joomla 5.0, AEM 4.9, Payload 4.2, Umbraco 4.2, Sanity 3.3, Contentful 2.7, Squarespace 2.6, Craft CMS 2.0 and Webflow 1.1; the last two figures show the limit of the method, because Webflow’s extension ecosystem is a closed marketplace that a repository count cannot see.

The ecosystem a project depends on is better described by what exists around the core:

  • Extensions: WordPress.org lists 73,152 plugins and 8,799 themes (2026-10-29, official count, a live figure). The directories of the other systems (Drupal modules, the Joomla Extensions Directory, the TYPO3 Extension Repository, the Craft Plugin Store, the Umbraco Marketplace, the Webflow Marketplace, the Wix App Market) were not counted from a primary source, so their sizes are n/a and are not estimated. On Packagist, 1,640, 4,315 and 2,948 packages require Drupal core, TYPO3 core and Craft CMS respectively, a measure of third-party code built on each.

  • Managed hosting and cloud offers: WordPress.com (Free to Commerce plans, 0 to 70 USD per month billed monthly), Craft Cloud, Strapi Cloud, Umbraco Cloud, Ghost(Pro), the Webflow site plans and the Wix and Squarespace plans; Payload Cloud shows no public price (n/a) and Contentful’s pricing was unreachable.

Performance

What it measures. The Core Web Vitals pass rate is the share of the origins detected for a CMS in which at least 75% of real Chrome page loads are good on all three vitals: Largest Contentful Paint (LCP), Interaction to Next Paint (INP) and Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS). It comes from the HTTP Archive Tech Report, which combines the technology detection of the crawl with the Chrome UX Report field data. The median Lighthouse performance score is a lab score (0 to 100) of the same origins from the monthly crawl under a throttled test profile, so it is lower than the real-user experience on fast devices. Both describe populations of sites, not the product in a controlled test.

Caveats. Field data is per origin, not a benchmark: it reflects themes, plugins, hosting and audiences as much as the CMS. INP replaced FID on 2024-03-12, so the series are not strictly comparable across that date, and every chart marks the break. Small or shrinking populations can be biased by survivor effects, and Strapi, with 14 mobile origins, has percentages that say little. A headless CMS does not decide the performance of the site it feeds: the front end does, so the numbers for Contentful, Strapi and Sanity describe the sites that happen to be detected, and the choice of front end is discussed in the Web Frameworks Comparison. API latency and rate limits published by the headless vendors were not collected for this page (n/a), and no house measurement is used.

What the reader should take from them:

  • Drupal: the core remote-code-execution flaws of 2014 and 2018 (Drupalgeddon) show that an unauthenticated flaw in a widely deployed core is exploited within days.   • Joomla: the 2023 web service disclosure has a medium CVSS score (5.3) and is nevertheless in the KEV catalogue, so a low score does not mean a low risk. The two 2026 critical entries are in extensions (JCE and Page Builder CK), not in the core.

  • WordPress: the plugin entries of 2019 and 2020 (Social Warfare, Duplicator, File Manager) are in the KEV catalogue, and so are the 2026 core entries (REST batch route confusion, page-template file inclusion and the sanitisation flaw), added three to four days after publication. The core is not immune, but the volume of exploited flaws is in the plugins.

  • Craft CMS: four exploited flaws in 2024 and 2025 (CVSS 10.0, 9.8, 8.1 and 5.3), all in the KEV catalogue.

  • Other systems: TYPO3’s 2020 flaw (CVSS 10.0), the Strapi, Ghost and Payload entries and the disputed Umbraco entry are not in the catalogue, except one Strapi entry added in 2026-10; the AEM Forms flaw (CVSS 10.0) is.

Why self-hosted CMSs with large plugin ecosystems have a larger attack surface

Three facts in the data explain why the exposure of the self-hosted CMSs, and of WordPress in particular, is larger:

  • The site owner is the patch manager. On a self-hosted system a fix does not protect a site until someone applies it. The KEV catalogue added the 2026 Drupal SQL injection two days after publication and the Craft CMS flaw CVE-2025-32432 eleven months after, which shows that exploitation continues long after a fix exists.

  • Third-party code runs with the privileges of the site. Plugins, modules and extensions run inside the same PHP process, with access to content and users. 91% of the new WordPress vulnerabilities of 2025 were in plugins (Patchstack), Drupal publishes 126 contributed-module advisories against 8 for the core, and the exploited Joomla flaws of 2026 are in extensions.

  • The stack is larger. The web server, PHP or Node.js, the database and the operating system are the owner’s (or the host’s) responsibility, together with the CMS and every extension.

A hosted builder removes the first and third of these, but not the risk of third-party apps with excessive access scopes or of compromised editor accounts. See the WordPress security hardening page for the measures on that platform.

Why SaaS CVE counts are not comparable

Webflow, Wix, Squarespace, Contentful and Sanity (the hosted content store) have no NVD series, and the same is true of their advisories (n/a). This does not mean they have fewer vulnerabilities. When a SaaS vendor fixes a vulnerability in its platform, it deploys the fix on its own servers and the customers take no action, so the fix does not become a CVE: CVEs exist to tell the users of installed software what to update. A count of zero or near zero for a SaaS product therefore means that its vulnerabilities do not need to be announced to the customers. The same applies in reverse: a large number of CVEs for a self-hosted product shows transparent disclosure as much as a large number of vulnerabilities, and AEM’s several hundred CVEs a year show how one vendor files its components, not how insecure AEM is. Counts should be compared only within one deployment model, and even then they measure disclosure effort, not risk. Hosted vendors are better compared by their security programmes and compliance (SOC 2 and ISO 27001 attestations, bug bounty and disclosure programmes, single sign-on, role granularity), which this page does not score numerically because the attestations were not collected from primary sources (n/a); the security scores of the hosted systems reflect only that the vendor, and not the customer, patches the platform.

Reading the security data. The evidence supports three statements. First, the owner of a hosted site delegates platform patching, which removes a whole class of risk. Second, on self-hosted systems the exploited flaws cluster in extensions (WordPress plugins, Joomla extensions) and in a few core flaws that are exploited within days, so patching speed and the number of third-party extensions a site installs decide the exposure. Third, Drupal, TYPO3 and WordPress have an advisory process and automatic or coordinated updates, while the young headless projects publish few advisories, which could mean fewer problems or less scrutiny. Share of detected sites running unsupported versions, hacked-site report shares and per-version install statistics were not collected for this snapshot (n/a).

Maintenance

Maintenance effort is the work needed to keep a site secure and supported: applying core, plugin and theme updates, running backups, moving to a new major line before the old one ends, and keeping the hosting stack current. The editorial score is 10 for a vendor-managed product with no customer patching and lower for self-hosted systems with frequent major upgrades and short support windows.

Summary and recommendations

The data snapshot date for every figure below is 2026-10-29, and the data changes monthly (the HTTP Archive and W3Techs figures each month, the NVD and the registries every day). The recommendations are justified only by the data on this page. They name the option the evidence favours first and what to check; they are not rankings, and a team with deep experience of one system can reasonably choose it against the table.

In short. Choose WordPress for a self-hosted site where the largest talent pool, ecosystem and portability matter and where you can pay for managed hosting and plugin vetting, and Webflow for a design-led marketing site that should have no server to run and no patching. For headless content, Sanity (hosted) or Payload and Strapi (self-hosted) are the current choices, Drupal or TYPO3 fit complex multilingual or regulated sites, and Joomla, Drupal 7 and other end-of-life lines should be kept only for existing sites.

Trends, cautions and legacy

  • Trends to watch. Sanity (+228% detected origins in three years and rising downloads), Umbraco (+74%), Wagtail (+61%) and Webflow (+21%) are the only systems whose detected origins grew in three years, and Payload has the fastest npm growth; each starts from a small base except Webflow.

  • Established, but under pressure. WordPress and Wix lost about a fifth of their detected origins since 2023, and the W3Techs share of WordPress fell for the first time, from 43.6% to 39.5%. They remain the largest by far, so the figures describe a market that is consolidating, not collapsing.

  • Keep for legacy maintenance, avoid for new projects. Drupal 7 (ended 2025-01-05), Joomla 3 (ended 2023-08-17), Joomla 4 (ended 2025-10-14), Craft 4 (security ended 2026-04-30) and Strapi 4 (security ended 2026-06-09) are out of support. Joomla as a whole declines in every popularity signal (origins -48%, W3Techs share from 3.3% to 1.1%, weakest growth), so a new project is better served by another system.

  • Cost and security perspective. The hosted builders minimise labour (maintenance 9 to 10) and move patching to the vendor, which removes the largest class of risk, at the price of portability (2 to 3). Among the self-hosted systems the exploited flaws cluster in extensions and a few core flaws exploited within days, so budget for patching speed and for a short list of vetted extensions.

  • Hiring perspective. WordPress has by far the largest pool (talent score 8.4 and 13.3% of the 2026 survey respondents). TYPO3, Drupal and Webflow have dedicated certification and partner paths; the headless systems draw on JavaScript and TypeScript developers, and Umbraco and Wagtail on .NET and Python ones.

Framework alternatives

When the requirements go beyond content (application logic, a customised user experience, data that is not editorial), a web framework may fit better than a CMS, or a CMS can be used as the editorial back end of a framework-built front end. The Web Frameworks Comparison and the Backend Frameworks Comparison cover Next.js, Laravel, Django and the others; the stacks are described in Next.js, PHP and Laravel and Django.

When you do not need a CMS

A CMS earns its cost when non-developers edit content regularly. A documentation site or a small site edited by developers in Git needs none: a documentation generator or a static-site generator (Hugo, Jekyll, Eleventy or Gatsby, which are out of scope here) gives lower hosting cost, no patching of a database-backed application and no plugin attack surface. See the Documentation Tools Comparison for that choice.
