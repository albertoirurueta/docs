Partial Periods and Retroactive Accounting

This content was generated with the assistance of AI and should be verified against the official documentation before being relied on in production.

Most months are simple for payroll: a person works the whole month and receives the whole monthly pay. Real life is not that tidy. People join or leave in the middle of a month, take unpaid leave, change department, or get a raise whose start date lies in the past. This page explains the two mechanisms SAP payroll uses for these cases. The first, called factoring, pays only the part of a period that an employee should be paid for, by multiplying an amount by a fraction between 0 and 1. The second, called retroactive accounting, goes back and recalculates a month that was already paid because something that affects it was changed later, and then pays or claims back the difference in the current month. After reading it you can compute a partial period factor with each of the five methods SAP offers, explain why the method matters most for monthly payroll, and follow a retroactive correction from the changed record to the amount on the next payslip. It is written against SAP S/4HANA 2025 FPS02 on premise and Private Edition, where payroll comes from SAP HCM for SAP S/4HANA (H4S4), a separately licensed component. SAP S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition and SAP SuccessFactors are not covered here; the landscape page compares the options.

All rates and amounts in the examples are generic and made up. They are not any country’s rules, and the arithmetic was checked by hand and with a small script, not by running an SAP system.

Before you read

  • HR and Payroll Basics: gross and net pay, the payroll period, the payroll area and the payroll run in plain words.

  • Infotypes: the records (Basic Pay, Absences, Planned Working Time) that payroll reads.

  • Time Constraints and Validity: why a past record can be changed, and why that forces a payroll correction.

Key terms

  • Factoring: reducing or splitting an amount in payroll by a partial period factor.

  • Partial period factor: the fraction by which an amount is multiplied when an employee is paid for only part of a period.

  • Personal work schedule: the planned working time of one employee, day by day, for the period.

  • Retroactive accounting: recalculating already paid periods when data that affects them was changed later.

  • Payroll past: all payroll periods for which payroll was already run and exited.

  • Retroactive accounting limit: the furthest date in the past up to which payroll data may be changed and recalculated.

  • Recalculation difference: the amount by which a recalculated period differs from what was paid, carried to the current period.

  • Wage type: a pay component, such as basic pay, with an amount or a number of hours.

The example used on this page

Every page of this section uses one made-up business, Example Trading Co. All keys, names, numbers and dates are invented example values for this site, not values delivered by SAP. Dates are written DD.MM.YYYY, as an SAP screen shows them.

  • 00001235 Ben Okoye, Sales Clerk, employee subgroup AA "Salaried staff", personnel subarea 0001 "Head office", payroll area E1 "Monthly EX01", monthly basic pay 2,400.00 EUR. Ben is the example employee on this page because Ana’s March 2026 payslip is fixed by the other pages of the section and is not changed here.

  • Planned working time: 8 paid hours a day, Monday to Friday. February 2026 has 20 working days and March 2026 has 22 (public holidays are ignored in the examples, to keep the numbers readable).

  • Chloe Martin (00001236) is paid by the hour in the same monthly payroll area E1; that is the example’s own choice. Her pay follows the hours that time evaluation delivers, so a day she does not work simply has no hours and the methods of this page are not needed for it.

  • The "general period working time" used below is 21 days. That divisor is an invented average chosen for the example. A real company chooses its own in Customizing.

Why payroll needs partial periods

SAP’s documentation says that when an employee did not work for the whole payroll period, a partial remuneration is calculated during the payroll run, so that only results for the current period are considered. It names three uses for factoring:

  • To reduce payments because an employee joins, leaves or is absent during a period.

  • To calculate remuneration for an exact period, because of substitutions, changes in basic pay, organizational reassignments or changes in the personal work schedule.

  • To calculate wage types for cost accounting for an exact period, so that different amounts can be assigned to different cost centers.

The remuneration amount is multiplied by a partial period factor to give the exact remuneration. The documentation lists the infotypes that payroll evaluates for factoring: Actions (0000), Organizational Assignment (0001), Planned Working Time (0007), Basic Pay (0008), Recurring Payments and Deductions (0014), Additional Payments (0015), Absences (2001) and Substitutions (2003). The documentation’s examples of reductions include joining or leaving on a day other than the first or last day of a period, a week of unpaid leave, a transportation allowance that stops during leave, and a bonus paid only if the employee is still employed the next month.

Source: Partial Period Remuneration (Factoring) and Application Examples for Reductions.

The partial period factor

The factor is a fraction. SAP describes its formula in words as planned working time (or the flat-rate period working time) minus absence, divided by a divisor. Written as an equation, with for the time the employee was planned to work, for the unpaid time in that stretch, and for the divisor:

The amount to pay is then the basic amount times the factor. All parts of the formula must use the same calculation unit. SAP’s documentation names three: working hours, work days and calendar days. In the personnel calculation rules the unit is marked by a letter (S for hours, A for work days, K for calendar days). The values come from the personal work schedule (table PSP) and from the work center and basic pay data (table WPBP).

SAP also distinguishes the period on which a parameter is based. A parameter starting with T refers to a partial period (used when a basic pay wage type has a WPBP split indicator), and one starting with G refers to the whole period (used for items such as recurring payments or additional payments, which are not split). For example, SSOLL and ASOLL hold the planned working time in hours and in work days, and their partial-period forms are TSSOLL and TASOLL.

Source: Partial Period Factor and Factoring Parameter.

The five methods

The methods differ in what they put in the divisor and in what counts as the reduction. SAP’s documentation names five.

Method Factor What SAP says about it

PWS method (personal work schedule)

Uses the employee’s own planned working time as the divisor. Suitable for reductions when planned working time is specified exactly and every deviation is entered in Absences, Substitutions and Planned Working Time. SAP says it is not suitable if payroll is run for monthly periods.

Payment method

The employee is paid for time worked or paid absences; a constant general divisor makes every day or hour worth the same in short and long months. Suitable with many unpaid absences, or when the period length does not differ from the general length (for example weekly payment). Not suitable for monthly payroll with few absences: a long month can pay more than the basic amount, and a short month can reduce it too much.

Deduction method

A pro-rata amount is deducted for the unpaid absence. Suitable with few unpaid absences or when period length is constant. Not suitable for monthly payroll with many absences: in a long month the result could become negative, and in a short month an employee absent the whole month still receives pay.

Hybrid of payment and deduction

payment factor or deduction factor, chosen per case

SAP recommends deciding per individual: the payment method for few absences (the pay stays high), the deduction method for many.

All or nothing method

1 or 0

The employee receives the whole amount or nothing, for example if a minimum period was worked. SAP names the cost transfer of a travel allowance and a savings plan allowance as uses.

The choice is usually fixed by the collective agreement or the company agreement, and SAP says the question is extremely important when payroll is run for monthly periods. The standard system delivers a few example factoring rules that a company can use as they are or adapt in Customizing for Payroll under Factoring.

Source: PWS Method, Payment Method, Deduction Method, Hybrid of Payment and Deduction Methods and All or Nothing Method.

Observations that follow from the numbers, and that match the cautions in SAP’s documentation:

  • The deduction method takes the same amount, 5 / 21 of 2,400.00 or 571.43 EUR, off in both months. Under the PWS method the value of one day depends on the month (1 / 22 in March, 1 / 20 in February), so the same five days cost 545.45 EUR in March and 600.00 EUR in February.

  • The payment method with no absence at all pays 22 / 21 of the basic pay in March, which is 2,514.29 EUR and more than the 2,400.00 EUR agreed. That is the long-month problem SAP describes.

  • The deduction method for an employee absent all 20 working days of February still pays 1 / 21, which is 114.29 EUR. That is the short-month problem SAP describes.

How payroll applies the factor

Payroll runs factoring as a schema step. SAP’s documentation describes the technical sequence of the international schema: personnel calculation schema X000 calls subschema XAL9 (factoring and storage), and country-specific versions exist (the letter X stands for the country indicator and the international versions). The documented prerequisites are the reduction factors set in Customizing for Payroll under Factoring, Create Reduction Factors, and processing classes 10 (Mark wage types for factoring) and 31 (Split monthly lump sums for cost accounting) on the wage types to be factored.

In short, the documented flow is:

  1. Function GEN/8 generates the secondary wage types /801 to /8nn, one per partial period, each with the value 1 multiplied by a constant to keep the arithmetic precise. The standard system generates 16; /801 to /809 are free for a company’s own partial period factors, and /810 to /816 are used for monthly lump sums.

  2. Operation PPPAR reads the partial period parameters, for example whether the employee started a new job in the period or took unpaid leave. If no split indicator exists, the factor stays 1 and the wage type is paid in full.

  3. Personnel calculation rule xPPF determines the partial period factor for each partial period.

  4. Personnel calculation rule xCMO determines the flat-rate period for cost accounting.

  5. Personnel calculation rule xVAL multiplies the amount of each primary wage type by the factor and writes the reduced value back.

This runs only inside an SAP system. Requires an SAP system; not run here. The numbers in the previous sections were computed outside SAP.

Source: Technical Sequence for Partial Period Remuneration.

Retroactive accounting

Payroll results cannot be corrected by editing them. Instead, SAP’s documentation says that if you change master or time data for a payroll period for which payroll has already run, the existing results are rechecked in the next regular payroll run and included in the payroll again. Not every change causes this: SAP checks whether a change is relevant to retroactive accounting, per infotype or per infotype field, set in Customizing for Personnel Administration under Customize Procedures, Infotypes, Define Fields Relevant for Retroactive Accounting. Changes to Customizing itself do not trigger retroactive accounting. To force it for all personnel numbers, SAP names the field Forced retro. accounting as of on the payroll program’s selection screen.

The documented recognition steps are:

  1. Editing a retro-relevant infotype writes the date of the changed record into the Payroll Status infotype (0003), in the field Earliest Master Data Change Since Last Payroll Run.

  2. At the next regular payroll run or correction run (off-cycle type B), the system compares that date with the date up to which payroll was already run for the employee.

  3. If the date lies before it, the system recalculates every period from the period containing the change up to the last period already paid.

  4. It then deletes the stored change dates from infotype 0003.

flowchart TD A["Edit of a retro-relevant infotype in a paid period"] --> B["Infotype 0003 stores the earliest change date"] B --> C["Next regular or correction payroll run"] C --> D{"Change date before the paid-up-to date?"} D -- "No" --> E["Normal calculation of the current period"] D -- "Yes" --> F["Recalculate each period from the change date"] F --> G["Differences go to the current period"] G --> H["Change dates deleted from infotype 0003"]

Source: Retroactive Accounting.

The retroactive accounting limit

A correction cannot reach back forever. The retroactive accounting limit is the date furthest in the past up to which payroll-relevant data may be changed. SAP’s documentation says it is derived from three dates and that the most recent of them is used: the limit stored for the payroll area in the payroll control record, the earliest personal limit in infotype 0003, and the hiring date of the employee, which is the absolute limit. The payroll area’s limit can only be edited when its control record has the status End of payroll. For Example Trading Co. the payroll area is E1, so one limit in its control record applies to Ana, Ben and Chloe unless an employee has a more recent personal limit.

Source: Retroactive Accounting Limit and Example: Determining the Retroactive Accounting Limit.

Recalculation differences

The corrected period is not paid again. SAP’s documentation describes this process. The system calculates a new payment amount for the earlier period but does not save it as the period’s result. It forms the difference to the original payment amount and forwards it to the current period. For a period with a new result and the original result , the recalculation difference is

and the current period receives the sum over all recalculated periods, . A positive sum is an arrears payment, a negative sum is a repayment withheld from the current pay.

SAP stores the differences in two wage types with opposite signs. In the retroactive accounting result the difference is saved in wage type /551 (Recalculation difference): negative when the new amount is greater (an arrears payment), positive when it is less. The amount is transferred with the sign reversed to the current period’s result in wage type /552 (Subs.clearing/prev.month). The original amount in wage type /560 (Payment amount) is not changed.

What older books show, and what has changed

The book SAP ERP HCM: Technical Principles and Programming, 1st edition (2013), mentions retroactive accounting in its payroll chapter: a change to a payroll-relevant infotype in a past period triggers recalculation back to the period of the change, whether an infotype triggers it is a property that can be checked per infotype, and recalculated periods keep their earlier results marked with status values. These ideas are unchanged in the 2025 FPS02 documentation. This page’s factoring material and the wage types /551 and /552 rest on the product documentation. What has changed is the product around them: the book was written for SAP ERP HCM, while in SAP S/4HANA 2025 these functions come from SAP HCM for SAP S/4HANA, which needs a separate license and whose business function is active by default, so the older compatibility mode is no longer an option.

Source for the H4S4 statement: SAP HCM for SAP S/4HANA.

Summary

  • Factoring pays an employee for part of a period by multiplying an amount by a partial period factor between 0 and 1.

  • SAP documents five methods: PWS, payment, deduction, a hybrid of payment and deduction, and all or nothing. They differ in the divisor, and the choice matters most for monthly payroll, where short and long months distort some methods.

  • In Ben’s example, five unpaid days in March 2026 give 1,854.55 (PWS), 1,942.86 (payment) or 1,828.57 EUR (deduction), and only PWS splits a month into partial periods that add up to the agreed 2,400.00 EUR.

  • Retroactive accounting recalculates already paid periods after a retro-relevant change, back to the retroactive accounting limit, and never edits the original results.

  • The corrected difference, , is stored in /551 and cleared in the current period through /552, so a raise with a past start date shows up as arrears in the next payslip.
