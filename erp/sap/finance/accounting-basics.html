Accounting Basics

This content was generated with the assistance of AI and should be verified against the official documentation before being relied on in production.

Every company has to answer two questions at any moment: what does it own and owe, and did it earn money or lose money over the last month or year? Accounting is the discipline that keeps the records needed to answer them, and bookkeeping is its daily routine of writing every business event down in a fixed way. This page teaches that routine from zero, with no SAP knowledge and no accounting background assumed, because every SAP Finance page of this site builds on it. The rules here are general accounting rules and are the same on any release of any system; the last section shows how SAP S/4HANA 2025 FPS02 (on premise and Private Edition) and Cloud Public Edition 2608 apply them. The money amounts are made up (Example Trading Co., company code EX01, leading ledger 0L) and rounded so that you can check every sum by hand.

Before you read

  • Organizational Structures: what a company code such as EX01 is, the unit that keeps one set of books.

Key terms

  • Double-entry bookkeeping: recording every business event in at least two accounts so that the books always balance.

  • Debit: the left side of an account.

  • Credit: the right side of an account.

  • T-account: a drawing of one account as a letter T, debits on the left and credits on the right.

  • Asset: something the company owns or controls that is expected to bring future benefit, such as cash, stock or equipment.

  • Liability: something the company owes, such as a loan or an unpaid supplier invoice.

  • Equity: what is left for the owners after the liabilities are subtracted from the assets.

  • Revenue: the value of goods and services sold in a period.

  • Expense: the cost used up to earn the revenue of a period.

  • Balance sheet: the statement of assets, liabilities and equity at one date.

  • Profit and loss statement: the statement of revenue and expenses over a period, also called the income statement.

  • Accrual: recording a cost or a revenue in the period it belongs to, whether or not money has moved yet.

  • Journal entry: one posting that records one business event, with equal debits and credits.

  • General ledger: the collection of all accounts of a company.

  • Trial balance: a list of all account balances used to check that total debits equal total credits.

Why companies keep books

A company keeps books for four reasons. Owners and managers need to know whether the business makes money. Banks and investors want a reliable picture before they lend or invest. Tax authorities require records of income and costs. And in most countries the law requires a company to prepare financial statements by fixed rules; for example, the IFRS Conceptual Framework sets out definitions of an asset, a liability, equity, income and expenses. Because many readers depend on the books, they must be complete, consistent and easy to check. Double-entry bookkeeping, described next, was designed to make mistakes visible.

Source: Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting (IFRS Foundation)

The five kinds of account

All business events are recorded in accounts, and every account belongs to one of five kinds. Two ways of looking at the company use them.

The first three kinds describe the company at one moment, like a photograph, and make up the balance sheet. The last two describe what happened between two moments, like a film, and make up the profit and loss statement. The difference between revenue and expenses is the profit (or loss) of the period, and that profit flows into equity, which is what joins the two statements.

The accounting equation

Think of a house bought for 300,000 with a mortgage of 200,000. The house is an asset worth 300,000. The mortgage is a liability of 200,000. What is left for the owner, 100,000, is equity. Every company has the same structure: assets equal what is owed to outsiders plus what is owed to the owners.

Every business event changes the two sides in a way that keeps the equation true. If the company buys equipment for cash, one asset (equipment) rises and another asset (cash) falls by the same amount, so both sides are unchanged. If it borrows 20,000 from the bank, assets rise by 20,000 (cash) and liabilities rise by 20,000 (loan).

Debit and credit

Debit and credit are the two sides of an account, nothing more. Debit is the left side and credit is the right side. Despite everyday speech, a credit is not good and a debit is not bad. The meaning comes from the kind of account.

  • Assets and expenses increase on the debit side and decrease on the credit side.

  • Liabilities, equity and revenue increase on the credit side and decrease on the debit side.

The rule that makes the books self-checking is the one of double entry. Every event is a journal entry of at least two lines, and the debits of the entry equal its credits. If you debit an asset account and the entry is complete, a credit must exist somewhere else. This is also why the equation holds: a debit to an asset on the left side of the equation is always matched by a credit to another asset (left side), a liability, equity or revenue (right side).

T-accounts

A T-account draws an account as the letter T: the account name on top, debits on the left, credits on the right. The balance is the difference between the two sides. Here is the first event of the example, the owners paying 50,000 into the company bank account, as two T-accounts:

Bank is debited because an asset increases; share capital is credited because equity increases. Debit 50,000 equals credit 50,000. When the company later pays 9,600 for equipment, bank receives a credit of 9,600 on the right, and the bank balance becomes 50,000 - 9,600 = 40,400 on the debit side. An account that has more on the debit side has a debit balance (typical for assets and expenses); more on the credit side gives a credit balance (typical for liabilities, equity and revenue).

The path from event to statement

A business event reaches the financial statements in the same steps in every company, on paper or in SAP:

flowchart LR A[Business event: invoice, payment, payroll] --> B[Journal entry: debits = credits] B --> C[General ledger: accounts with balances] C --> D[Trial balance: total debits = total credits] D --> E[Balance sheet] D --> F[Profit and loss statement]

The journal entry records the event with its date and its lines. The general ledger collects all entries by account. The trial balance lists the balance of every account at a date and proves that debits equal credits. The two statements are then built from the same balances: asset, liability and equity accounts feed the balance sheet and revenue and expense accounts feed the profit and loss statement.

Accruals and depreciation

Cash is not the same as profit. Under the accrual principle, a revenue is recorded when it is earned and a cost when it is incurred, whatever the date of the payment. Three situations show the difference, and all appear in the worked month.

  • Sale on credit. The company delivers goods worth 15,000 and the customer pays later. The revenue belongs to this month, so a receivable (an asset) is booked instead of cash.

  • Cost incurred, not yet paid. Employees worked all month but are paid next month. The salary expense belongs to this month, so a liability for the unpaid amount is booked (an accrued liability).

  • Cost spread over time. Equipment bought for 9,600 is used for years, not for one month. Instead of charging the whole price at once, the company charges a share each period; this is depreciation. With straight-line depreciation over 4 years (48 months) the monthly charge is 9,600 / 48 = 200. The depreciation expense is debited, and the credit goes to accumulated depreciation, an account that reduces the equipment asset without changing its purchase cost.

The same rules in SAP

SAP S/4HANA does not change any of the rules above. The general ledger is the set of all G/L accounts of a company code, and every posting is a journal entry with a header, which holds information valid for all items, and items, each with an account, an amount and a debit or credit assignment. The system accepts the entry only when total debits equal total credits; the SAP Fiori app Post General Journal Entries shows a balance indicator while you enter lines. A journal entry is posted at company code level, and only G/L accounts of the operating chart of accounts assigned to that company code are available. Most postings are not typed by hand: a goods receipt, a vendor invoice or a customer payment creates its journal entry automatically, and manual entries are used for operational adjustments such as an accrual or depreciation correction. The later Finance pages of this site (Finance Overview, Organizational Units in Finance, General Ledger Accounting) show how SAP stores these entries in the Universal Journal and how its accounts, ledgers and company codes are set up. Posting itself needs an SAP system and is not run here.

Source: Managing General Ledger Accounting: Basic Functions (SAP Learning)

Common mistakes

  • Reading debit as decrease and credit as increase. The effect depends on the kind of account: debit increases an asset and an expense, and decreases a liability, equity and revenue.

  • Treating cash as profit. In the worked month the bank balance is 56,400 largely because of the owners' capital and the loan, while the profit is only 800.

  • Charging a long-lived asset to expense at once. Equipment is depreciated over its useful life, 200 a month in the example.

  • Believing a balanced trial balance proves the books are right. It only proves that debits equal credits.

  • Forgetting the accrual. Leaving out the unpaid salaries of 3,000 would raise the profit to 3,800 and understate liabilities.

Summary

  • Companies keep books to know their result, to inform lenders and investors, to pay tax and to meet legal reporting duties.

  • There are five kinds of account: assets, liabilities, equity, revenue and expenses. The first three form the balance sheet, the last two the profit and loss statement.

  • The accounting equation holds after every event, and equity includes profit.

  • Debit is the left side and credit the right side. Debit increases assets and expenses, credit increases liabilities, equity and revenue.

  • Double entry means every journal entry has equal debits and credits, and the trial balance checks the total.

  • Accruals record a revenue or cost in the period it belongs to; depreciation spreads the cost of equipment over its life.

  • In the worked month Example Trading Co. earns 800, with total assets of 73,800.

  • SAP applies the same rules: balanced journal entries on G/L accounts of a company code, mostly posted automatically.
